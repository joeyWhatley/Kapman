# Kāpman: the winter

10 October 2026. A PROPOSAL, NOT BUILT (the road's step 15). Written for the user to read; the living copy is the
doc "Kāpman: the winter", https://claude.ai/artifact/99UVN2T6hDqbH79YVmwka5 . This file is that page as it stood

Each year at Martinmas you choose. You lay her up, and one winter page settles the winter and wakes you at Easter,
or you stay out at your own risk.

## What the user ruled, and what the game has today

On 7 October (DECISIONS 31): winter is the player's choice each year. Lay up, and one winter page settles it
(repairs, what was spent, who died, the news, plans for spring), or stay out and play on day by day at their own
risk. For the alpha the page comes in its plainest form.

| | Today |
| --- | --- |
| The sea | Nothing closes. She can sail every day of the year. |
| The other ships | They do lie up in winter, so the quays and the sea are quieter. |
| The weather | It has its winter. In the last walk the gales came between August and April, and the days are short. |
| The markets | They have their seasons: new wine from Martinmas, dear grain in spring. |
| The men | If they sail on shares, their tenth of the season is reckoned at Martinmas. Bread and pay run every day, in port too. |
| Written, and read by no screen | Lines for a good, a middling and a bad year. Forty winter cards. Ten things to build over a winter. What an heir inherits. (Game/Data/story/winter_793.json) |

The first life crosses one winter before the debt's day at Michaelmas 794. Easter 794 falls on 23 March (the game's
own Feasts.Easter), so that winter is 132 days long.

## The first winter and the debt

In the fourth recorded walk (Design/checks/playthrough_4_journal.txt) the first winter is when the debt was earned.
Her purse stood at 16 d at Martinmas 793 and at 1,408 d on 24 March 794. She paid the last of the 1,920 d on
24 September 794, five days before the day.

So a plain trader who lays up the first winter comes to Michaelmas 794 far short. Three ways, the user's to choose:

| Way | What it means | What it costs to build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The first winter is a working winter | The debt keeps its day. While she owes, she cannot afford to lie up, and need drives her out. Laying up becomes the ease of a house that owes nothing. | Nothing new |
| The debt moves | Its day or its size changes, so that a winter laid up can be afforded. | A ruling, and new walks to find the numbers |
| The winter earns ashore | Laid up, the house earns over the winter: weaving, carrying, a share in another man's cargo. | New design and a sitting or two |

Proposed: the first. Design/research_trade_seasons_and_hints.md says there was "no open-sea passage from Martinmas
to Easter unless need or greed drove it", and the debt is that need.

## The year's turn, as you meet it

1. Michaelmas, 29 September. The log says the season is ending, and the Voyage window counts the days to Martinmas.
2. From Michaelmas on. At a port where she may winter, the Port window has the line "Lay her up for the winter".
3. Martinmas, 11 November. The sea closes. If she is not laid up, the clock drops back and you are asked once: lay
   up here, make for home, or stay out.
4. Laid up. The winter page opens. When you have settled it, the winter passes while the page counts the weeks.
5. Easter. She is afloat with the men aboard. The log opens the year with the debt's day, the fairs to come, and the
   first rumour.

The one question at Martinmas is the only new interruption.

## Staying out

Today a winter at sea costs her nothing. The fourth walk made 9 calls in January 794 and 10 in June 794, and she was
never lost. For the choice to be real, staying out needs a price. The least that gives it one:

| | Today | Proposed for the first cut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The men | Nothing changes for them. | From Martinmas to Easter they want a winter wage at sea: double pay. |
| The boat | An untarred hull wears twice as fast (Boat.untarredWear). | A boat laid up is tarred on the winter page. A boat that stays out goes into spring untarred, unless a yard does it. |
| The weather | Gales and short days, as the weather makes them. | The same, with no new rule. |
| The other ships and the markets | Fewer ships are out, and prices follow their seasons. | The same, with no new rule. |
| Ice | A number the cards can read. It stops nothing. | Nothing yet. The chronicle's hard winters (810, 813, 821, 859) lie after the first release. |

It may still pay better to stay out than to lie up. The long walk should play a winter both ways before it is made
harder.

### The ship that sails in winter

The user's idea of 10 October, in their words: "I want there to be a low chance event in winter if you're out doing a
dangerous journey there's a rare event where you come across a ship sailing and if you hail it everyone is acting like
it's normal and being non chalant about the fact that they're sailing in winter".

A rare meeting at sea, built as the other meetings are, with the Hail button. Its lines are the user's to write, and
it stays held until they have passed them. Open: how rare she is, and whether she is only a joke or also gives
something (a word of a market, a trade, a warning).

## The lender in winter

The first winter needs no new rule for the lender's men. They are sent on game days 21, 130, 335 and 445
(22 May and 8 September 793, 1 April and 20 July 794), and none falls between Martinmas and Easter.

For later years: the rope men do not sail in winter either. A sending that falls due while she is laid up waits for
the first day she is afloat.

The written winter card of the debt speaks of "the count's man". It is older than the lender and his men, so it
stays held. On the winter page the debt is one plain line with the pay buttons.

## How it is built

Four to five sittings, by the rule of 7 October.

| Part | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Screens | The winter page. The line "Lay her up for the winter" in the Port window. The days to Martinmas in the Voyage window. |
| Flow, as a graph | "Year - the winter": the season's end is said, Martinmas asks, she is laid up, each part of the page is settled, the winter passes, spring comes. |
| Rules, as graphs | May she winter here. Is the year good, middling or bad. What the winter keep costs. What the winter wage is. When the sea opens. |
| Table | Game/Data/winter_rules_793.json holds every number. The written winter_793.json keeps the words and the cards. |
| Code | The pass through the winter, and the year's figures that nothing keeps today: gain, ports first seen, men lost. Each rule also stands in code as the fallback. |
| Tests | A test holds each graph and its code to the same answer. A play test gives pictures from the game. The long walk lays up one winter and stays out another. |

1. The season's end, laying up, the page in its plainest form, the pass through the winter, and spring: two sittings.
2. The year read back and judged, and the boat and the men on the page: one or two sittings.
3. The price of staying out, and the walk played both ways: one sitting.

The game's clock can already jump (Core/Sim/TickLadder.cs replays every minute, hour and day in between for the
other ships, the markets, the weather and the news). How long a whole winter takes that way is not measured yet.

## The user's to rule

- The first winter and the debt: a working winter (proposed), the debt moves, or the winter earns ashore.
- Where she may winter: at home only, at home or at any port for a host's fee (proposed), or anywhere.
- When the sea opens: at Easter, which moves (23 March in 794, 12 April in 795; proposed), on a fixed day, or on a
  day chosen on the page.
- The price of staying out: a winter wage and no tar (proposed), something harder, or nothing new.
- The men over the winter: kept for a winter keep of a fifth of the wage, the other ships' own number
  (WorldSim: 0.25 d working, 0.05 d laid up; proposed). Paying off and hiring again waits for hiring (step 27).
- The year's name: whether the user wants to name each year.
- The winter's cards: none in the first cut (proposed). The forty stay held until they are read.
- The words: every line on the page is a placeholder. The written lines for a good, a middling and a bad year speak
  of "the count" and of "he".
