# Kapman: the music, the books, the faith and the arts you can legally ship, and how to bring them in

**The ask (4 Oct, evening):** "A big aspect of this game will be history / arts / poetry / culture / religion. Research the
most appropriate classical music I can legally incorporate, and which books etc, and how to do it."

**The short answer.** Everything written or composed in 793-1066 is out of copyright. What is *not* free is the
modern recording of the music and the modern translation of the text. So the rule is: free work + free recording, or
free work + free translation, and both must be checked. There is a real body of music from inside your period
(plainchant, the first sequences, the earliest polyphony from Winchester, Latin songs), and a real library of texts
a merchant of the time would have known, with old translations that are free. For wider "classical" mood music
there are a few high-quality recordings made on purpose to be public domain. Where nothing free exists, the right move
is to commission a singer for an hour and own the recording outright: that is a real recording, not an invented sound,
so it keeps your rule.

---

## 1. The legal frame in plain words

Two copyrights sit on every piece of music: the **composition** and the **recording**. Two sit on every text: the
**original** and the **translation**. Your period's originals are free everywhere. For the rest, as of 2026:

| Thing | Free in the US when | Free in the UK and EU when |
|---|---|---|
| A composition, text or translation | published before 1931 | the author (or translator, or editor) died before 1956 |
| A sound recording | made before 1926 (the 1923-1946 recordings come free 100 years after publication, one year at a time); all US federal government recordings (the military bands) | published before 1963 |
| A CC0 release | always, if the uploader really held the rights | the same |

Rules of thumb for a game sold worldwide:

- **Use only what is free in both columns**, or CC0. For texts: translator died before 1956 and the book was printed before 1931. For recordings: pre-1926, or a US government band, or CC0.
- **Modern editions of chant** (Solesmes books, Graduale Triplex) can carry a thin edition right in some countries. Use the 1961 Liber Usualis scans on the Internet Archive if you commission a singer; the edition rights on it have run out.
- **Translating a free Latin or Old English text yourself** makes a new text you own. I can draft; you edit. That fits "the user writes the words".
- **CC BY** (credit required) is also shippable, but your standing rule for sound is CC0 and public domain only, so CC BY is listed here only where it is the only option, marked for you to decide.
- **Record every source** in SOURCES.md: title, performer or translator, date, where it came from, which rule makes it free.

## 2. Music from inside 793-1066

This is what was actually sung and played in the ports and churches the game visits.

### 2.1 Plainchant (the daily sound of every church and minster)

The Carolingian court imposed Roman chant across Francia from about 790 (Admonitio generalis 789). Dorestad, Utrecht,
Cologne, Lundenwic and York all heard it. The Hours were sung eight times a day (Matins, Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext,
None, Vespers, Compline) and the Mass. The game's clock and chronicle already know the hour and the feast, so the right
chant can play at the right time in port:

| Chant | When | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Ave maris stella** ("Hail, star of the sea") | Marian feasts, Vespers | A sailors' hymn; earliest manuscript is 9th-century St Gall. The one piece to have. |
| **Veni Creator Spiritus** | Pentecost, Vespers | Written by Rabanus Maurus of Fulda c. 809, inside your period. |
| **Te lucis ante terminum** | Compline, every night | Short, haunting, 7th century. The night-fall sound in port. |
| **Te Deum** | great feasts, victories | Sung at Charlemagne's coronation. |
| **Pange lingua / Vexilla Regis** (Venantius Fortunatus) | Passiontide, Holy Cross | Old by 793, everywhere. |
| **Rorate caeli** (Advent), **Puer natus est** (Christmas), **Resurrexi** (Easter), **Spiritus Domini** (Pentecost) | the Mass of the day | The calendar's turning points. |
| **Media vita in morte sumus** | Lent; funerals | Attributed to Notker of St Gall (d. 912); the words were a Frankish battle-and-funeral cry. |
| **Salve Regina, Alma Redemptoris Mater** | Marian antiphons | Both attributed to Hermann of Reichenau (d. 1054), inside the period's last years. |
| **Victimae paschali laudes** (Wipo, c. 1040) | Easter sequence | Dated and in period. |

### 2.2 The new music of the period

- **The sequences of Notker Balbulus** (St Gall, c. 840-912): Latin poems fitted to the long Alleluia melodies. The first named composer of the north whose work survives. *Natus ante saecula*, *Sancti Spiritus assit nobis gratia*.
- **The Planctus de obitu Karoli** (814): the lament on Charlemagne's death, with music in the manuscript. The earliest surviving secular-style song of the north. The game's chronicle has 814; this is its song.
- **The Winchester Troper** (c. 1000): two eleventh-century manuscripts from Winchester's Old Minster with the earliest substantial body of two-voice polyphony in Europe. English, in period, and the sound of Æthelred's and Cnut's England.
- **The Cambridge Songs** (copied c. 1050, texts from the 9th-11th centuries): the only Latin song anthology between Charlemagne and Hastings: spring songs, laments, a song on Otto's victory (*Modus Ottinc*), drinking and love songs. Secular, for the tavern.
- **The Musica enchiriadis** (c. 900): the first organum, two voices in parallel. A sound for a cathedral city (Cologne, Mainz).
- **Secular instruments:** the six-string Germanic lyre (Sutton Hoo, Trossingen c. 580, Oberflacht), bone flutes, horns, bells, drums. The Frisian singer Bernlef, blind, "beloved by his neighbours because he sang of the deeds of the ancients and the wars of kings to the lyre" (Altfrid's Life of Liudger, c. 840): a historical Frisian bard from the Dorestad coast, in your start decade.
- **The Winchester organ** (c. 990): Wulfstan's poem describes 400 pipes and 26 bellows, heard across the city. A marvel for the chronicle.

### 2.3 Free recordings of period music

This is the hard part. Modern chant recordings (Solesmes, Silos, Schola Hungarica, Sequentia, Ensemble Organum) are all
in copyright.

| Source | What | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **The 1904 Gregorian Congress recordings** (Gramophone Company, Rome): Dom Mocquereau's Solesmes-trained choir and others singing *Haec dies*, *Pascha nostrum*, *Sacerdotes Dei*, the Easter Alleluias, Sanctus and Agnus Dei | The earliest chant recordings; thin and crackly, which can be a feature (a memory of a sound) or a problem | Pre-1926, so public domain in the US; pre-1963, so free in the UK and EU. Catalogued in UCSB's Discography of American Historical Recordings; copies on the Internet Archive and on 78 rpm transfers (check each file's provenance) |
| **OpenGameArt "Chant"** and Freesound items marked CC0 | amateur chant, short | CC0; quality varies; check each |
| **Wikimedia Commons** audio marked PD or CC0 | a handful of chant and hymn recordings | check each file's licence tag; many are CC BY-SA, which you do not want |
| **Commission** | one trained singer, one afternoon, a dry room: the ten chants above plus the Compline psalm, each in two or three takes | you own the recording outright under a work-for-hire or full buyout contract; roughly £150-500 for a church singer or early-music student; the only way to get clean period chant under your rule |
| **Lyre and bone flute** | Freesound CC0 samples exist for lyre strings and bone flutes; a commissioned lyre player is cheap | free assets for simple sounds, as your rule allows |

**My recommendation:** commission the chant (one singer, the Liber Usualis as the score, a plain Solesmes rhythm, no
organ), and use the 1904 recordings as found objects (a far-off service heard through a wall, in a memory, over the
credits). Record a lyre player for the tavern and Bernlef.

## 3. Wider "classical" music that is free to ship

If you also want orchestral or keyboard music for menus, the chart, the credits or the quiet of the open sea, the
composer must be free (died before 1956, published before 1931) and the recording must be too.

| Recording source | What is there | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Musopen** (musopen.org) | Commissioned public-domain recordings: the 32 Beethoven piano sonatas (2008), the 2010 Kickstarter set (Beethoven, Brahms, Sibelius, Tchaikovsky symphonies and more), the complete Chopin (2015), plus a large archive; most under CC0 or PD mark | the best single source; check each file's tag |
| **Open Goldberg Variations** and **Open Well-Tempered Clavier Book I** (Kimiko Ishizaka) | Bach on a Bösendorfer, studio quality | CC0; the model of what a free recording can be |
| **US military bands**: "The President's Own" Marine Band, the Air Force bands, the Army bands | Holst (Suite No. 1, Hammersmith; the Air Force Heritage of America Band's *The Planets*), Grieg, Dvořák, Wagner, Sousa, much else; downloads from marineband.marines.mil | US government works, free everywhere; the compositions must be checked separately (Holst d. 1934 and Grieg d. 1907 are free; Vaughan Williams d. 1958 is free in the US only until 2029; Sibelius d. 1957 until 2028) |
| **Internet Archive, Great 78 Project** | tens of thousands of 78s, 1898-1950s | only the pre-1926 ones are free in the US; the crackle is the point |
| **IMSLP recordings** | performer uploads, many CC BY or CC BY-SA | mostly not CC0; use only the ones marked PD or CC0 |

**Composers that suit the game and are free everywhere:** Grieg (the north; *Holberg Suite*, the *Lyric Pieces*), Carl
Nielsen (d. 1931), Wilhelm Stenhammar (d. 1927), Johan Svendsen, Niels Gade (Danish, d. 1890), Edvard Grieg's *Peer
Gynt*, Elgar (d. 1934; *Introduction and Allegro*), Delius (d. 1934; *Sea Drift*, *A Song of the High Hills*), George
Butterworth (d. 1916), Parry, Stanford (Irish, d. 1924), Bach, Buxtehude (Lübeck; d. 1707), Sweelinck (Dutch, d. 1621),
Tallis, Byrd, Dunstable, Purcell, Debussy (d. 1918; *La mer*), Mendelssohn (*Hebrides*). Not yet free in the UK and EU:
Sibelius (2028), Vaughan Williams (2029), Holst is free (1934).

Caution: a period game full of 19th-century symphonies will sound like a film. Keep this music for the menu, the chart
and the credits, and let the voyage itself have chant from a shore church, the lyre in the tavern, the sea, the birds
and the crew. That is also what Sailwind and Dredge players praise.

## 5. How to bring it into the game

The design already has the pieces: the chest with the chart, the riddles and the rune sticks; a crewman who reads
part of Beowulf and you finish it; text encounters with facts and the user's words; the chronicle with its dates; crew
with a religion. This plugs in:

1. **A library file** (`Data/Texts/*.json`): each text with its title, date, place, language, the free translation
   used, the credit line, and its pieces (a fitt, a riddle, a letter, a law clause). The game never invents text;
   it shows these.
2. **The chant follows the clock and the calendar.** Each port with a church has a bell and a choir. The chronicle's
   date gives the feast; the Hours give the time; the game plays the right chant from the right building, through
   walls and distance. At sea on a Marian feast the crew's Christian men hum *Ave maris stella*. On Lindisfarne's day
   the chronicle opens with Alcuin's letter.
3. **Two faiths in the crew.** A Frisian or Saxon crewman may be newly baptised; a Dane may hold to Thor. Your
   decisions (sailing on a Sunday, trading at a pagan feast, carrying relics or captives) sit differently with each,
   through the crew traits already planned. The baptismal vow and the Indiculus give the exact words and practices.
4. **Bernlef in Frisia.** A blind singer at a Frisian port who sings "the deeds of the ancients" to the lyre:
   historically true, c. 785-800. He is the natural teller of the Old Saxon and Frisian pieces.
5. **The riddles for the chart** come from the Exeter Book and Aldhelm, period-true and free, with the answers tied to
   the world (an iceberg, a storm, an anchor, a ship).
6. **Period knowledge as the steersman's lines:** Bede on the tides, the Landnámabók sailing directions ("so that the
   sea is right in between the distant mountains"), birds as landmarks, the lead and line (Bayeux shows the sounding pole).
7. **Credits and a "where this came from" tab:** every text and recording with its line, as SOURCES.md already does
   for data.

## 6. What I would do first

1. Pick the ten chants (section 2.1) and the lyre; get a quote from a singer and a lyre player for a buyout recording.
2. Pull the 1904 recordings from the Internet Archive and check each file's date.
3. Build the library file from the free translations: The Seafarer, the Exeter riddles, Ohthere, Ælfric's Merchant,
   the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle entries the game uses, Rimbert on Birka, Hávamál, the baptismal vow.
4. Draft translations (for you to edit) of the short Latin pieces no free English covers: the Offa letter, Alcuin on
   Lindisfarne, the Annals of St-Bertin on Dorestad, Lex Frisionum's trade clauses, Bede on the tides, Altfrid on Bernlef.
