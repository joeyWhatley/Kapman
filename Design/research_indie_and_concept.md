# Kapman: what the loved indie games do, and one simple design that takes it all in

**The ask (4 Oct, night):** "research all extremely successful indie made games that are similar or in the same breath of
life as this game concept, research the reason they're so praised and beloved, then try and apply those to our project
and come back with a smart intuitive game design concept to incorporate all the ideas in a smart simple way."

**The short answer.** The small games people love in this corner all do the same five things. They put the whole game on
one table. They spend one resource, and it is nearly always time. They make knowledge the treasure. They give you a
handful of named people who can be lost. And they are small on purpose. Kapman already has the parts for all five. The
concept below puts them in one loop of five beats, on the chart you chose tonight as the game.

---

## 1. The games, why they are loved, what to take

| Game (team) | Numbers | Why people love it | Take for Kapman |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dredge** (Black Salt, 4 people, 2 years) | over 1 million sold; they hoped for 100,000 in a year; 100,000 in the first day | The loop is one line: "Go Out > Get Fish > Sell > Upgrade > Go Out > Get Caught At Night." Time is the only fuel: it passes when you move or work, and stops when you stop. The hold is a grid puzzle, "because it actually contributes to gameplay". Fishing completes itself if you do nothing. Cosy by day, uneasy by night. No crunch, because the scope never grew. | Time as the one resource. The hold as a small puzzle. Every task can finish itself. A cosy day with a dark edge you can see coming. Four regions, placed first, then filled. |
| **Sunless Sea** (Failbetter, 3 core, 15 months, £250,000) | 100,000 in three weeks; about 500,000 in all | "Exploring destinations, not rock formations": every scrap of land has a story. The slow ship was kept on purpose for dread and distance, though the director says it "might be more fun if ships were 50% faster". The line of captains (your heir inherits a chart, a house, a weapon). | Every port has a story or it is cut. Slow is right, with a speed key. What went wrong is the real lesson: see section 3. |
| **80 Days** (inkle, 3 people, 9 months) | TIME's Game of the Year 2014; four BAFTA nominations (from memory) | One idea "so simple, and so strong, that it shone through": round the world by any route against a clock. Cities are stand-alone modules, so writing never blocked. Halfway through they made exploring a city **optional**: "this single change transformed the experience from tedious to compelling". A run shows a few per cent of the writing, so people play again. | The route is the choice. The clock is the game. A port's story is a module you may skip. Never the same text twice. |
| **Roadwarden** (Moral Anxiety, 1 person) | 88% of critics recommend; Eurogamer "Essential" | A merchant guild sends you to learn a wild peninsula in 40 days. Text over small pixel pictures in **eight colours**. You pick an attitude (friendly, playful, distanced, intimidating, vulnerable) for each talk. You may type what you saw and tell it to someone. Time-limited quests make every road a choice. | Almost Kapman's own premise. A small fixed palette is a voice. Attitude as a one-tap choice in talks. The player's own notes matter in the world. A season as the deadline. |
| **Battle Brothers** (Overhype, 3 people) | about 1-2 million owners (est.) | A world map you travel freely; contracts "based on what is actually happening around settlements"; a company of named men with backgrounds and traits who die for good. No main story: "the lack of a central narrative combined with the constant threat of character loss" makes the stories. PC Gamer: "I just have to make enough to fix my gear, hire a new sword, and go on to my next contract." | The chart with the world really happening on it. Commissions that come from the world's state. A crew of names. No plot: a life. |
| **Mount & Blade: Warband** (TaleWorlds, began as two people) | "Overwhelmingly Positive", played steadily for fifteen years | "A story engine that never repeats": a map of towns, caravans and lords all going about their business; you start with nothing and trade, fight and rise. | The AI fleets are this. Let the player see them at it on the chart. |
| **The Banner Saga** (Stoic, 3 founders) | a trilogy; Viking-flavoured | A caravan on a long road. Three numbers: supplies, morale, days. One currency buys both food and skill, so every purchase hurts. Road events with no right answer. | Stores, heart and days are enough numbers. One purse for everything. Events where both choices cost. |
| **Curious Expedition** (Maschinen-Mensch, 2 people) | two games; pixel art | An expedition as a run of 20 minutes. One resource, **sanity**, falls with every step and "pretty much everything revolves around it"; at zero the party turns on itself. Discovery gives the reward, not killing. | A voyage as a short run. One falling number that everything touches. Reward for finding, not fighting. |
| **Nantucket** (Picaresque, small team) | 79% positive, about 870 reviews | The whole game on a paper map on a wooden table; the fog lifts as you sail; crew with four trades; random events that **show their odds**; a sea-shanty score. Players: "someone cared here and it shows everywhere". Its complaint: too short, events repeat. | The closest look-alike to tonight's chart game. It proves the table works. Odds shown (yours are words: sure, likely, doubtful). Guard against repeats. |
| **FTL** (Subset, 2 people) | a Kickstarter landmark; GDC talk "Designing Without a Pitch" | Built from one feeling: being the captain. "The player experience was the primary goal; gameplay structures, mechanics, and genre were all secondary." A map of nodes, one event per node, something always behind you. | Start from the feeling (a merchant with one boat and a debt). One event per leg. Something always closing: the season. |
| **Citizen Sleeper** (1 person) | IGF and GDC award winner | Tabletop bones: each day you get a few dice and spend them on actions; **clocks** fill on screen for your work and for other people's plans. Little art, few mechanics, strong mood of scarcity. | Clocks you can see: the count's day, the fair, the tide, the raiders' season, a crewman's patience. A few actions a day in port. |
| **Stardew Valley** (1 person) | 41 million sold; 11 million of them in 2024, eight years after release | Chores that become comfort. A 28-day season, four seasons, feasts on fixed days. Townsfolk whose talk changes with what you did and gave. "Rewards curiosity rather than strict optimization." | The calendar as the heartbeat: you know the fair is coming. People who remember. No best build to solve. |
| **Dave the Diver** (Mintrocket) | several million sold (from memory) | Two loops that feed each other: dive by day, serve at night. "The kind of repetitive that enriches itself constantly." | Two halves that feed each other: the sea and the port. What you bring decides the evening; the evening pays for the next day. |
| **Potion Craft** | 4.7 of 5 across stores | A medieval manuscript look; brewing is moving a mark across a **map**; every tool is something you touch. | Diegetic tools on the table: the tally stick, the scales, the chest. A period look sells the game in one screenshot. |
| **Wildermyth** | "Overwhelmingly Positive" (from memory) | Heroes age over a campaign, from "their pitchfork days" to old age; the best go into a **legacy** and turn up in later stories as myths. "Reminds players of oral storytelling." | Your heirs, and your old crew as tales other crews tell. The saga of your house, told back to you. |
| **Kingdom Two Crowns** | a long-running series | One resource (coins), one verb, no text. "Trusts the player, stripping away everything unnecessary." Pixel art chosen because it is quick to make. | Fewer numbers on screen. Show, do not label. |
| **Papers, Please / Return of the Obra Dinn** (Lucas Pope, 1 person) | IGF Grand Prize twice | A dull job made into play. One strict look (1-bit) kept legible above all. "Embarrassingly simple" mechanics: watch and deduce. | The toll, the tally and the stowage are play, not menus. The palette is law. Riddles answered by what you noticed. |
| **Sailwind** (1 person) | 89% positive | Real sailing with your hands. Top complaint: a dead world, boxes from one still man to another. | The hands-on part is yours in the 3D window; the living world is the answer to its weakness. |

## 2. The ten things they share

1. **One table.** The whole game is one screen you learn once: a map (Nantucket, Battle Brothers, FTL, 80 Days), a desk (Papers, Please), a brewing map (Potion Craft). Nothing important lives three menus deep.
2. **One resource, and it is time.** Dredge's daylight, 80 Days' clock, Roadwarden's forty days, Curious Expedition's sanity, Citizen Sleeper's dice. Everything is priced in it, so every choice is a real one.
3. **Time moves when you act.** Dredge stops the clock when you stop. Nobody loves a game that punishes thinking.
4. **Knowledge is the treasure.** Sunless Sea's stories, Roadwarden's notes, Obra Dinn's deductions. What you learn stays when the money is gone.
5. **A few named people who can be lost.** Battle Brothers, Wildermyth, The Banner Saga. Three to twelve names, one line of character each, real loss.
6. **The world goes on without you.** Mount & Blade's caravans, Battle Brothers' contracts from real trouble. You read the world; it does not wait.
7. **A cosy loop with a dark edge.** Dredge by day and by night. Stardew's mine. Fear you can see coming is fun; fear that jumps you is a reload.
8. **A calendar you can feel.** Stardew's seasons and feasts. A known date ahead is a plan, and a plan is play.
9. **One voice in the art.** Eight colours, one bit, a manuscript. Made fast, known at a glance, sold by one screenshot.
10. **Small on purpose.** Four regions, two years, no crunch (Dredge). The ones that grew (80 Days' hundred cities, Sunless Sea's fifteen months for six) say so in their own postmortems.

## 3. What went wrong for them, so it does not for us

- **Sunless Sea did not decide what it was.** Permadeath roguelike and long story game at once: "the source of very nearly everything that's wrong". *Kapman must decide: a life, with heirs, not a run that resets.* A wreck should cost the ship, the cargo and maybe a man, and pass the house on; it should not delete an evening. This is question 1 of the earlier report, still open, and yours.
- **Sunless Sea tuned for veterans.** Old saves kept testers in the mid-game, so the start was never fixed. *Play the first hour fresh, every week.*
- **Sunless Sea's first combat pulled you off the sea into a separate screen** and broke the mood; it was thrown away. *Raiders and the king's men stay on the chart, as cards. No battle screen.*
- **Sunless Sea had no tools.** Editing data by hand was "like chopping down trees with your hands". *The encounter and text files are data already; a small editor panel is worth a night.*
- **80 Days grew from "a few routes" to over a hundred cities** and the team burned out: the Americas are thin. *Fifty ports are in the table. The first release needs about twelve done well.*
- **80 Days tested with seven or eight friends.** Routing bugs shipped. A feedback button that attached the save "saved them". *A "send this" button that saves the game and the log.*
- **Nantucket's events repeat.** *Seen-flags from the first day.*
- **Sailwind's world is dead.** *Yours is the opposite, if the player can see it: show the fleets on the chart.*

## 4. The concept: one table, one clock, five beats

**In one line.** *You are a merchant with one boat and a debt. The chart is your table. Days are your only fuel. What
you know is your wealth. Your crew are four men with names.*

### 4.1 The table (what is on screen, always)

The chart fills the screen: your pixel map, the fog lifting where she has been, her mark moving on the clock, other sails
where the lookout has made them out. Three things lie on it, and they are the whole interface:

| On the table | What it is | Replaces |
|---|---|---|
| **The day stick** (top) | A notched stick: today, the season, and the notches ahead that matter to you: the count's day, the next fair, the feast, the sea closing. A game device; say so in the credits (calendar sticks are attested later than 793). | The clock, the calendar, the quest log |
| **The tally** (bottom left) | Silver by weight, the debt, days of stores aboard, and the hold as a small grid | The purse, the inventory, the supplies screen |
| **The chest** (bottom right) | The texts and riddles, the rune sticks of prices heard, your own notes, the crew | The ledger, the journal, the codex, the party screen |

A port, a sail or a card opens as a panel beside the chart, never over it, and closes back to it. The 3D pixel world is
a **window**: one key and you are on the water watching her; one key back. Nothing in the game requires the window. It
is the reward, the way Dredge's sea is.

### 4.2 The one resource: days

Everything is priced in days, and the day stick shows it before you choose.

- Sailing costs days (the wind decides how many: the stick shows "2 to 4").
- Waiting for a tide, a wind or a fair costs days.
- Stores are "9 days aboard", not three meters.
- Cargo spoils in days. News goes stale in days. The debt falls due on a day. The sea closes on a day.
- **Time passes only when she sails or you wait.** In the market, the chest and the talk panels the clock stands still (Dredge's rule; the earlier report's idea 2).

Silver is the score, not a second fuel. One purse pays for cargo, stores, wages, tolls and gifts (The Banner Saga's rule),
so every purchase is felt.

### 4.3 The five beats

Every leg of every voyage is the same five beats. Learn it once.

1. **Hear** (in port, clock stopped). Each good carries its word: "A Frisian skipper said Lundenwic was paying well for wine. Last week." A buyer with a name and a date. A rumour. You choose who to talk to: you have three talks a call (Citizen Sleeper's dice, made plain), and an attitude for each (Roadwarden): plain, warm, hard.
2. **Choose.** A cargo, fitted into the hold's grid. A port on the chart: the course draws itself and the day stick shows what it costs and what it crosses. One crew matter if there is one.
3. **Sail.** Her mark moves. One to three cards a leg (FTL's node, 80 Days' road), drawn from where she really is and what is really there: the weather, a sail made out, a shoal, a feast day ashore, a man's mood. Each card is a fact, two or three choices, and the odds as a word. Speed 1x to 4x; it drops to 1x on any card. Open the window whenever you like.
4. **Arrive.** The unknown is shown: the price moved, three Frisians got in first, the town burned in June, the fair is on. The toll-keeper. The sale, cask by cask.
5. **Reckon.** One line goes in the log: what the run made after wages and bread, who did what, what was learned. The word on every good you saw is renewed. The crew's hearts move. The day stick is cut.

Then Hear again. A leg is 5 to 15 minutes. A season is an evening. A life is a week of evenings.

### 4.4 The year is the run

The sailing season (Easter to Martinmas) is one run, like one expedition in Curious Expedition. Then winter:

- **Winter is the reckoning** (clock jumps; one screen). The year's log is read back as a short saga in your own entries. The debt is paid or it is not. The boat is hauled and mended. Men leave, marry, die, are hired. An heir grows a year. The chronicle's news of the year arrives. You choose one thing to build over winter: a bigger boat, a share in a second, a factor in a far port, a gift to a church.
- **Spring** begins with the day stick fresh and three things known: the debt's new terms, the fair dates, the first rumour.
- **A life** ends by age, wreck or ruin, and is ranked in the chronicle; the heir takes the house, the chart's lifted fog, the chest, one feud and one friend (the earlier report's idea 13). The best crewmen become tales that later crews tell (Wildermyth).

This settles the save question without a rule: the unit of loss is a cargo or a boat, not the game, and the house goes on.

### 4.5 Four men with names

A Gislinge boat carries four. Each has one trait, one want and one fear, a home port and a faith (your crew notes).
They speak in the cards. A man overruled against his nature grows uneasy; at the end of his patience he leaves, fights or
vows. A want tied to a place ("home to Ribe for my sister's wedding") is a route the game offers you, not a quest marker.
One of them knows a song by heart and gives you a fitt of Beowulf each evening at sea: the library built tonight.

### 4.6 The dark edge

Fair days are cosy: a following wind, gulls, the tally. The edge is night offshore, fog, the bar at low water, a strange
sail closing, a gale building. Every one of them shows itself hours ahead on the chart and the day stick, has a way out,
and wounds before it kills: cargo over the side, a mast gone, a man lost. No battle screen: raiders are a card, then a
chase on the chart, then a card.

### 4.7 What is deliberately not here

No steering in chart mode. No skill trees. No crafting. No second currency. No minimap, because the map is the game. No
text the game invents: every word is a real text, a fact, or yours.

## 5. How it maps onto what is built

| Beat or part | Already built | Next |
|---|---|---|
| The table | The chart scene (tonight): the map open, her mark sailing the course on the clock, 50 ports, the market docked beside it, the fog, the AI fleets, the chest, the ledger | The day stick; the tally as one strip; panels beside, not over |
| Days as the one resource | The clock, Voyage stores, spoilage, the debt's day, fairs, seasons, TideWait | The clock held in panels; days shown on the course before it is set; stores as "days aboard" |
| Hear | Price notes with source, age and sureness; port people; news by ship | The word on the item (design in research_trade_seasons_and_hints.md, two nights); three talks a call; attitudes |
| Choose | Course planning by the lanes, commissions | The hold grid |
| Sail | Encounters engine, raiders, king's men, trader meetings, weather model | Cards at sea in chart mode (the engine's port calls need chain berths today); the pacer; seen-flags |
| Arrive | Markets, tolls, shocks from the chronicle, reputation | The reveal line: why the price is what it is |
| Reckon | Deeds log, chronicle, save | The voyage log line; the winter screen; the saga read back |
| The window | The pixel demo (tonight), the sail tank's water, sky, weather and sound | The same ship and place shown in 3D on a key; later |
| The crew | Crew notes and plan | Four men, one trait, one want, one fear each |
| The library | 267 texts (79 added tonight), riddles | The evening fitt; the riddle setter |

**Order I would build in:** the word on the item; the day stick and the clock held in panels; cards at sea; the voyage log
and winter; four men; the hold grid; the window. Each is one or two nights, each leaves the game playable.

## 6. Your decisions

1. **A life with heirs, not a reset.** A wreck costs the boat and cargo and passes the house on. (Changes "wreck = game over".)
2. **Days as the only fuel**, with the clock held in panels.
3. **The year as the run**, with winter as one reckoning screen.
4. **Four named crew** from the start, not later.
5. **The first release: twelve ports** round the Rhine mouth, the Frisian coast and the Thames, done well.
6. **The 3D pixel world as a window**, optional, after the table is fun.
