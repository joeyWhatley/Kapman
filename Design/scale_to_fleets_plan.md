# From one boat to a house of ships: the plan

**In one line.** The house grows in **men and shares, not in a list of ships**. You always sail one boat. Everything
else of yours is a person you sent, known only by word as old as the ship that brought it, and there is never more of
it than the chart, the day stick and one winter page can show.

Three findings decide the shape. History: no northern merchant of 700 to 1100 is known to have owned two whole ships;
Godric, after twenty years, held part of a half and a quarter of another (history.md 3.2, 7). Games: of the ten that start
with one small vessel, none sends a second ship away under a person while you sail your own; the games that do send
ships away as units (X4, Bannerlord) end in babysitting, and the ones that give convoys are told by their players to
go back to one. Taste: the first mockup was "big and bulk", so this plan adds **no tab and no
list**. You click the boat, the notch, the face or the word.

## 1. The stages, and what one turn is

A stage opens by a deed, never by a sum or a level. Nobody is pushed up; three well-run boats to 1066 is a full game.

| Stage | Opens when | One turn is | Places on the stick | What is new in your hands | Rough hours (D) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The boat** | The start | A leg: hear, choose, sail, arrive, reckon | 0 | Everything | 0 to 3 |
| **2. The season** | The first Martinmas | A season, then winter | 0 | A better boat, better men, a corner of a storehouse, a bale in another man's hold | to 10 |
| **3. The fellowship** | The debt paid, one run crossed three times, one man aboard for all three | A season, with word | 1 | One boat sent, with orders in words | to 25 |
| **4. The house** | A jetty at home and a host abroad who has sheltered you three nights | A year: the winter page, then your own voyages | 3 | A plot abroad, your man there, a lord's letter | after 25 |
| **5. The name** | A grant of standing (section 5), or the first heir | A life | 6 | Rank, kin houses, the big hull with partners | across lives |

A leg stays 5 to 15 minutes at every stage. That is a test in the build (step 14), not a hope.

## 2. The rules, with numbers

**2.1 Three crossings.** A run is a pair of ports. Sailed there and back three times by you, it can be given away. The
third reckoning writes one more line: "You know this road now. So does Folkmar." An heir needs one crossing of a run
his father knew. Say the period's rule when it happens (three crossings of the wide sea at a man's own cost; England,
c. 1010, one copy only: history.md 2.10).

**2.2 Who may steer.** Only a man who was aboard for all three crossings of that run, or kin, or a partner made by one
season's venture reckoned without a broken word. Nobody is hired from a bench. The house can therefore grow by one boat
a season at the very fastest.

**2.3 Yours to sail.** Three things belong to the boat you are aboard and to no other: she alone lifts fog (a boat can
be sent only where your chart is clear); she alone makes a first call at a port; and a grant is asked for in person.
She hears three talks a call; a steersman brings home one price word a season, marked "heard from Folkmar".

**2.4 How many keels.** Your own boat and **six** others at the most: the six notches of the day stick's second row.
The house's parts in the six may add up to no more than **three hulls** (D). A kin boat is a whole hull; a partner's
is a half. So a house of two sons' boats and two partners' is full, and a house of six is six partners. More keels
means less of each. Beyond the six: up to four **sleeping shares** in other men's ventures, one line each.

**2.5 The reach.** You give orders to at most **three** steersmen. The fourth keel and on sail under one **senior
partner**, who takes up to three boats and gives them their own orders. You give him one sentence. The house names at
most **twelve** people: your four aboard and eight more. Other crews are a number.

**2.6 What a steersman costs and keeps.** No wages for a steersman, ever; crews are fed and paid from the voyage as
the world's ships already are (0.25 d a man a day). The gain after that is parted on the quay:

| How the keel is held | You lay in | The split | Fixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kin boat** | The whole hull and the cargo | Two thirds to the house, one third to him | At his oath |
| **Partner's boat** | Half the hull, half the cargo | Half and half | When goods are first laid together |
| **Sleeping share** | A quarter or a half of one season's cargo (30 to 150 d) | That part of the gain, no say | For the season |

A share never creeps up. A good man is paid in what he asks at a later winter: a part in the hull, a dowry, a boat of
his own. That is a card. All keep is paid on one day, **Martinmas**, as one sum of named parts: each keel's mending
(2.5 per cent of her price; 4 for river craft), each plot's due, your man's household (24 d a year (D)). A boat
hauled out on the bank costs her tar and nothing else, so a house may shrink after a bad year.

**2.7 How old word is.** Word of a boat is posted where she calls and carried by whatever ship next goes that way. It
is exactly as old as that passage: at Dorestad about 6 days for Lundenwic, 9 for Ribe, 13 for Hedeby, 35 for Birka
(D, at the fleet's 60 km a day). Her mark is **solid** when seen today, **hollow with its age** up to 30 days, **faint**
beyond 30 days or past her due day. Word reaches you only in port, as one bag: at most **three** pieces a call, at most
one of them asking anything. A reply costs one of the call's three talks and sails when a ship does. Nothing of the
house ever opens a card while you are under way, except meeting your own sail.

**2.8 Orders.** Given in two places only: the winter page, and face to face where you and he lie at one quay or meet
at sea. Word sent after him reaches him if he calls where it waits. Your marks on the chart are not buttons.

**2.9 Seven matters.** The winter page has **seven tally sticks**. New orders, a keel laid, a man placed, a marriage,
a gift, a suit, a partner's terms: each breaks one. Whatever is not touched runs as last year. The number is the same
for one boat and for seven; a clerk kept through the church adds an eighth.

**2.10 Silver.** Silver is where it lies: you can spend only what is in the hull under you or in your store in the
port you stand in. Nothing earns while it sits: no interest, no rent. Nothing is charged at sea.

## 3. The people

Each has a name, a home, a faith, one trait, one want, one fear, and three plain words learned from his own tallies
and never shown as numbers: how he is **at sea**, **at the scales**, and **with the truth** (poor, fair, good, or "not
yet known"). Every one of them has a **floor**: whatever his skill, he does not sail into a gale he could see, sell
below cost, or cross after the sea closes. Skill moves his margin and his days, not his life.

| Who | Where he comes from | What he is good for | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A steersman raised from your crew** | One of your four, aboard for the three crossings | He knows the bar and the toll-keeper as you do | He wants his own boat one day; refused twice, he leaves and takes the road's goodwill |
| **Kin** (son, brother, in-law, the wife at home) | Born, married in, fostered | Loyal; the wife or widow keeps the keys and the silver at home and can head the house between heirs | Has the right and perhaps not the skill; quarrels are with you, not about pennies |
| **A partner** | Met in a port; one shared venture first | The best seamen; brings half a hull and his own roads (inside your clear chart) | Honest reckonings that are thin; or he grows and wants to be the senior man |
| **A host** | A local man who has sheltered you three nights | Answers for you at law, stores a little, passes word | He is liable for you: your crew's blow is his fine, and his door shuts |
| **Your man abroad** | Kin or a sworn countryman settled on your plot in the Frisian row | Keeps the store, buys on a few spoken words, sends word | Dies abroad; is blamed for another Frisian's deed and must leave with all of them (York, c. 773) |

There is no hired captain and no salaried factor: the period had neither (history.md 2.4, 2.6). "Factor" in the first
answer becomes "your man at Lundenwic". Before c. 1000 the sworn brothers are "men who drink and help each other in
fire and wreck"; the guild by name comes after.

## 4. Orders as words

One sentence, three slots and one rule. On the winter page you change it by clicking a word in it; a strip of at most
six other words opens under that word, made only of what you know: runs you have crossed three times, goods seen
there, feast days.

> **Folkmar** carries **wine** to **Lundenwic**, brings **wool cloth** home, back by **Lammas**. He **shelters in any gale**.

Rules (one): sells at the first fair price; holds for the fair; shelters in any gale; never crosses after Michaelmas;
never carries arms; gives the toll-keeper his cask; sails in company where raiders are. Beside the sentence stands the
odds word the cards use: **sure, likely, doubtful**. Under a sentence that cannot work the page writes one line, once:
"No wine at Dorestad before the vintage boats come." The senior partner's sentence has two dials instead:

> **Odo** sails for **a half**, in **the Frisian sea** (this run only / this sea / where he sees fit).

Orders are a fence round the world's own trader mind, not a script, so he cannot jam: where they run out he does what
his nature allows, and "he did otherwise, and here is why" is a normal homecoming. Each voyage comes back as **one
line with its reason**:

> Folkmar sold the wine at Quentovic, not Lundenwic: a Frisian at Walcheren told him Lundenwic was full. 61 d to the
> house. Nine days late: four of them windbound at the Maas mouth.

One order for one leg is yours alone to give: **in company**, with one other boat, at the slower boat's pace.

## 5. What silver turns into

Every rung is chosen in winter, one rung a port a winter, each with an errand that is a reason to sail.

| Silver becomes | Rungs and prices (works file, roster) | Who grants it, and the yearly due |
|---|---|---|
| **A home** | Corner of a store 6 d (offered at the first Martinmas); the plot 480 d (offered the winter the debt is paid, by the same man); storehouse 360; jetty 600; boat shed 240; hall 1,200 | The reeve; the plot's due |
| **A better hull, then a second** | Coaster 1,200 or river freighter 300; the keel 3,000; half a partner's keel 1,500; knarr from 900, in halves or quarters; the great ship from 1025, never alone | A wright, a winter, the right port |
| **A foothold abroad** | A host (gifts); a plot in the Frisian row; your man's house 400. At most **three** plots abroad | The town: a stretch of wall, a share of the watch |
| **A lord's letter** | A gift and a cargo carried for an abbot or the palace | No toll but the tenth at Dorestad and Quentovic; one cargo a year at his price; lost with the lord |
| **Standing** | Alms, a bell 480, a book 900, a chapel 1,500; a seat among the sworn men 240 (from c. 1000) | The feast; a dead brother's share |
| **Kin** | A dowry, a fostering, a ransom, a stone raised | Allies, heirs, your men back |
| **Nothing** | A boat that does not come home. Silver buried in a pot | The price of size; or a way to be smaller |

Each winter the house may take **one vow of three** offered from what is true of it (a third crossing at your own
cost; a bell for Traiectum; a daughter wed at Ribe). A life is ranked in the chronicle by vows kept, not by silver.
After the debt is paid, any winter may be the last: **lay down the oar**, and the heir takes the chart.

## 6. How danger grows with the house

One word stands beside the tally, and a click on it gives three plain lines of why. Only what can be seen counts:
keels in a harbour, goods in a store, gifts given in public.

| The word | What makes it | What it draws |
|---|---|---|
| **Unnoticed** | One small boat | Weather and the toll |
| **Known** | Two or three keels, a name in two ports | Rivals learn your runs; the reeve expects his cask |
| **Well-off** | A plot, a store, a man abroad | The toll-keeper weighs harder; kin ask loans |
| **Rich** | Stock in three ports, a big hull | Raiders choose the port where your goods lie; the lord asks a ship |
| **A prize** | Seven keels, rank | A king wants you as his man, or wants what you have |

- A sent voyage has **five ends**, each told with its cause: home and well; home and thin; late (a red notch); hurt;
  gone. A boat with no word by the winter after her due day gets one card: send to ask, pay for a search, or have a
  Mass said and write her off. There is no button that makes a new steersman.
- The chronicle's blows fall where they fell (Dorestad from 834, gone by 863) and are seen a season ahead on the stick.
  What they cost is what lay there that year. They can be met only in person: a store emptied, a ransom carried.
- Your name abroad is your people's: an embargo or a flight for another man's quarrel closes a port to all your keels.
- A bigger hull is seen from farther off (one step of the odds word a rung) and closes creeks and beaches. The six-oar
  boat is never sold: she lies on the hard for life and you may take her out in any season.
- **Death divides the house.** Each kinsman and partner keeps the boat he steers and becomes a **kin house**: a named
  house in the world that carries your word for nothing and sometimes asks help. The heir keeps the home plot, the
  chart, the chest, the six-oar boat and one keel, one feud and one friend. So every life starts near stage 3, in a
  wider world.

## 7. The end game, concretely

### 7.1 What is on the chart

The same pixel chart as the first hour, clear along the lines the house has sailed and dark elsewhere. Byzantium is
used for one thing only: what is yours.

- **Your mark**, solid, the only one that takes a course.
- **Up to six marks of the house**: solid, hollow with a small age ("9 d"), or faint. No threads until one is clicked.
- **A small pennon** on each port where you hold a plot (at most four). A kin house's pennon carries a bar.
- **Fog** in front of you. That is where the year's best voyage goes.

### 7.2 The screens, to be drawn

1. **A boat's slip.** Click a house mark, or her notch. A small slip lies beside the mark, about 220 by 90 pixels at
   1080p, a degree off true: her hull in outline, the steersman's name, her sentence, and "Lundenwic, 9 days ago, by a
   Frisian of Tiel". Her run shows as one thin thread while the slip is open. No buttons. Click the chart to close.
2. **The day stick's second row.** Six small notches under the days, each on its boat's due day, in Byzantium. A notch
   whose day passes with no boat turns red, without a sound. That is the only alarm in the game.
3. **The tally.** As now, with silver in two heaps (aboard, in store here) and a paler figure for what lies elsewhere.
   After it, one word: "Rich".
4. **The bag.** In port, in the right-hand panel: "Word from Dorestad: three." Three short slips, one above another,
   each with who carried it and its age. One may have two or three replies at its foot.
5. **Your house on the home port's picture.** A site like any other: a gable, a jetty, and the boats that are in,
   drawn hauled on the bank, more of them each decade. Click a hull for her slip. Click the door for **the hearth**:
   at most eight faces in one row, each with one line ("Folkmar. A third. At sea: good. Last word 9 days old.").
6. **The winter page.** The one large, drawn screen, once a year, clock held. The home picture fills it, under snow.
   Left edge: seven sticks. On the bank: each keel with her sentence under her; a boat with no orders stands first,
   in red. At the hearth: the faces; one with a small mark has something to ask. On the table: three small things, the
   three vows (a bell, a ring, a keel). At the foot: the Martinmas sum as heaps of pennies with a word under each.
   Click a word in a sentence to change it; a stick breaks. Click the door to end the winter.
7. **The reckoning.** When a keel comes home: one card, her one line, the split shown as two heaps, one thing learned,
   one thing he wants.
8. **Your own sail.** At sea, a card: "Your own sail. Folkmar hails you." News, a swap of cargo, or new orders by mouth.

Nothing above is a list longer than eight, a table of numbers, or a tab.

### 7.3 One year of play: 1012, the house at Tiel

Dorestad is gone; the grandfather moved the house to Tiel. Four keels besides your own: your brother's (whole), two
partners' (halves), a raised crewman's (whole): three hulls, full. The clock keeps its one rate; at 4x a day at sea is
six minutes, and days in port pass only when you wait. About four hours of play (D).

| Real time | In the game | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:15 | Winter, clock held | The winter page. Last year read back in six lines. Seven sticks: Folkmar to London again but "back by Lammas" (the Danish army lay in Kent last year (?)); a quarter of a knarr laid at Hedeby with Odo; a dowry; a bell vowed. Three sticks left unbroken |
| 0:15 to 1:00 | Easter to Whitsun | Your own boat up the Rhine to Cologne for wine, as in the first hour: five beats a leg, two cards. At Cologne the bag: two pieces, 11 and 20 days old |
| 1:00 to 2:15 | Whitsun to Lammas | The voyage only you can make: past Ribe, where the chart is dark. Fog lifts. A first call, a host, three nights under his roof. At Ribe a bag: London paid tribute and the army broke up (?); Folkmar is "likely" again |
| 2:15 to 2:25 | Lammas | One notch turns red: your brother, due from the Maas. Nothing to click. You choose: home by the coast to ask, or on to the fair |
| 2:25 to 3:20 | To Michaelmas | The fair, the dear price of the year, in your own hold. Off Texel, your own sail: Folkmar, nine days late, hails you. New orders by mouth |
| 3:20 to 3:45 | Martinmas at Tiel | Three keels on the bank, three reckoning cards. The sum is paid. Your brother's notch is still red |
| 3:45 | Winter | A man from Walcheren has word of him. The winter page opens |

## 8. The build order

Each step leaves the game playable and has its own test. The sim already has what this stands on: 138 ships with
beliefs (`TraderBrain.Plan`), news carried port to port (`WorldSim.Board`, `Knowledge.SwapNews`), save and `StateHash`.

| # | Step | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The run count.** `RunLog`: there-and-back crossings by port pair, and which named men were aboard; saved | `RunLogTests`: three round trips make a run known, two do not; a man absent once is not a steersman for it; an heir needs one |
| 2 | **A house ship is one of the fleet.** `SimShip.house`, `steersman`, `HouseOrders` (carry, to, bring, back by, rule); `Plan` fenced to the order's ports and the lifted fog | `HouseOrdersTests`: over a seeded season she calls only at ordered ports and home, is back by her day when wind allows, and `StateHash` repeats |
| 3 | **The floor.** No sailing into a gale seen, no sale below cost, no crossing after the close | 200 seeded seasons: no loss from those three causes; losses that remain each carry a named cause |
| 4 | **The line home.** A record of each call (bought, sold, waited, why, from her beliefs) folded into one line and one of five ends | The line names port, price and days late with the cause; its pennies equal the change in her purse |
| 5 | **Word of the house.** A news item posted at each call and carried by the existing swap; `HouseWord.Latest(ship)` from what the player has heard | She calls at Lundenwic on day 10; at Dorestad nothing is known before a ship from there arrives; age equals today less day 10 |
| 6 | **Marks, slip and second row.** Solid, hollow, faint; the slip; six notches; red when overdue | PlayMode: three states drawn from three ages; a click opens the slip; no course can be set on a house mark; a capture is saved |
| 7 | **The bag and the reply.** One card a call, three pieces at most, one asking; a reply is a news item addressed to a man and costs a talk | Five pieces waiting show three, oldest first; the reply changes his orders only after he calls where it lies |
| 8 | **Shares, Martinmas, silver where it lies.** The three splits; one sum; a purse by place; laid up | The parts add to the gain; a laid-up keel costs tar only; a purchase with silver that lies elsewhere is refused with its reason |
| 9 | **The winter page.** Seven sticks, sentences changed by their words, the odds word, the one line under a sentence that cannot work | The eighth change is refused; untouched orders hold next year; an order for a run not yet known cannot be made |
| 10 | **Face to face.** The own-sail card; orders at one quay; in company for one leg | Orders change at once when both lie in one port and never otherwise; in company both arrive on the slower boat's hour |
| 11 | **A host, a plot, your man.** Three nights make a host; a store by port (town step 8); his spoken word to buy | He buys only within his word and the silver that lies there; word of it comes at ship speed |
| 12 | **The word said of you.** Seen wealth to one of five words; raiders, toll and the lord read it; buried silver is not seen | Over seeded years raiders strike a port more often with house stock at "Rich" than at "Unnoticed"; the three lines of why match the sums |
| 13 | **The division, kin houses, vows, the last voyage.** | A death with two kin boats leaves the heir one keel and two kin houses; they carry word free; save and load keep it |
| 14 | **The pacer.** | One leg timed in a stage 1 save and a stage 5 save: the second is no more than a quarter longer |

Steps 1 to 6 are the risk: nobody has shipped "ships that are somewhere else, known by word". Build them first with
one boat sent, and play it before anything after step 6 is begun.

## 9. Your decisions

1. **How big may the house be?** (a) *Recommended:* your boat and six, in shares that add to three hulls. (b) Eight
   wholly owned keels, as the first answer. (c) Your boat and three: a small house, done well.
2. **What do you see of your own ships?** (a) *Recommended:* only word, as old as the ship that brought it. (b) Word
   everywhere, but live in the home river. (c) Always live (easier; loses the game's best idea).
3. **What does a death do?** (a) *Recommended:* the division: each man keeps the boat he steers and becomes a kin
   house; the heir starts with one keel. (b) The heir keeps every hull and each man renews, bargains or leaves.
   (c) The heir keeps everything.
4. **Who may steer for you?** (a) *Recommended:* only kin, a crewman who made the three crossings with you, or a
   partner after one shared season. (b) Also strangers hired in a port, for wages (quicker; not of the period).
5. **Where is the house managed?** (a) *Recommended:* nowhere but the things themselves and one winter page with seven
   sticks. (b) The same, with no limit on changes. (c) A House tab with ships, men and stores, as the first answer.
6. **Does a steersman really sail and trade in the world?** (a) *Recommended:* yes, as one of the fleet, so his tale is
   what happened. (b) A rolled result round your own three tallies (cheaper to build; reads as a timer).

Left open on purpose, raised in history.md 9: whether the house may deal in slaves, only meets the trade, or works
against it.
