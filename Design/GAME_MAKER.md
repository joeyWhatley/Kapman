# Kapman: the book of the game

7 October 2026. Written by the game maker from the two apprentices' pages (`Design/maker/hands.md` and `heart.md`),
the two critiques, the designs, the code and the data. The game was not run and no web search was made. **This page
replaces the scattered plans: where another page disagrees, this one wins.** A number marked (g) is a first guess for
the soak to tune. Words in quotes are placeholders: the wording is yours.

## 1. The game

You are a merchant of Dorestat in 793 with a six-oar boat, four men and a debt to the count. You never see a true
price. You hold words: a skipper said Lundenwic pays well for wine, six days ago, and you bet your cargo and your
days on him. You run the voyage from a thick frame round the chart: orders on the left, the calendar across the top,
the face of whoever speaks top right, your boat on her own water bottom right. The chart in the middle is the key to
all of it, and it shows the whole known world from the first minute, dim where you have only heard of it. The world
does not wait: 138 other ships sail the same winds, carry the same news and get to market first. Days are the only
fuel. A year ends in winter, a life ends in an heir, and the house goes on to 1066 through wars, burned ports and new
kings, richer in names and knowledge than in silver.

**The promise:** every price is something a named person told you last week, and you stake your summer on whether
it is still true.

Three feelings must reach the player in the first hour, and today none does: *I know something. The day is coming.
They know me here.* The fourth is why anyone finishes: *I will not be here, and the house will.*

## 2. How it plays

### An evening: a new game to the first winter (about two and a half hours)

**Minute 0.** The frame is up. The chart shows the world from Vinland to the Caspian in grey, with one inked patch:
the Rhine mouth. On the day rule: Friday 21 June 793; three notches on, a flag, "St John: 40 d"; at the far end,
"Michaelmas: 480 d". The lamp is lit. The count's steward is in the portrait with half a split hazel stick. Three
choices, each with its cost under it. You press **2** and leave twelve pennies for the count's table. 74 d.

**Minute 1.** People is in focus: three pips. The keeper asks "What tidings?" You have none he lacks, so talk costs
ale, 2 d a time. One talk goes on a skipper six days out of Lundenwic: wine pays well there. Lundenwic's name inks
in. One goes on the keeper: two Frisians left for Lundenwic yesterday with wine. Two grey sails appear at the river
mouth, marked a day old. You keep the third pip. 70 d.

**Minute 2.** Trade. The wine row carries its word. Under it: "Lundenwic: pays well, 6 days old, 3 to 4 days, about
60 d for the four if the word holds. Two keels a day ahead of you." A fifth cask is 47 d and St John's earnest is
40. You cannot do both. You pay the earnest, and the far flag reads 440 d.

**Minute 3.** You click Lundenwic on the chart. Three ways draw themselves, with a tick for each dawn. On the day
rule a pale stretch runs from today to Monday or Tuesday; your bread line reaches past it. You press the preset "By
the coast, lie up at dusk". The frame reads the order back as one sentence. **Enter.**

**Minutes 3 to 6.** **Space**: Sail on. Her mark, a hull under a Byzantium sail, runs down the Lek. In the ship
window the western sky darkens. Then the weather-wise man's head lights: the wind has backed south-west. The clock
has stopped. Beat on, row under the dunes, or go back to Witla. You beat on: half a day. A card: two men and a boy
on a drying bank; taking them off costs an hour and three days of bread. The lookout's head lights: a sail ahead,
one of the two Frisians. She lies up at dusk. You flip one switch, sail on through the night, and pass her.

**Minute 6.** Lundenwic. The door is shut to Frankish keels: 20 d to the reeve, three tides lying off, or round to
Sarre. You pay. His man asks "What tidings?" You hold three things he lacks and tell him the price of wheat at
Dorestat in the famine. He gives a word back: cloth is cheap on the Strand since Saturday. Then the reveal: "One
Frisian got in on Saturday. Wine pays a tenth less than your word said." You sell. The reckoning: "Dorestat to
Lundenwic, 3 days. Four casks: made 52 d after the reeve, the toll, wages and bread. The skipper's word was true,
and nine days old."

**The rest of the evening.** A dozen more legs. One word proves false and the reveal says why. English grain keels
come up the Lek a week after you spoke of wheat, and wheat falls at home: your tongue did that. The tally sentence
moves with every sale: "Owed 440. In hand and in the hold 268. At this pace: short by 90." At day 80 you choose
between one more crossing in a foul wind and the widow's surety. Michaelmas is a Sunday (my sum; check it): the
market is shut and the count's card opens. You pay, or serve, or pledge the boat, and each is a road. Then wheat
before the frost. At Martinmas the sea closes and the frame gives way to one board: your own log lines read back as
the year, the men paid, the hull tarred, one thing bought. You pick the line that names the year, and stop there.

### Hour ten

About the second autumn, 794. Twelve ports are inked. The Thames verse is whole: three crossings gave three lines
and the pilot's daughter the fourth, so its bars show on the chart and that run can be given away. The order has all
five switches, and reef bands from the yard let her carry sail a step of wind longer. You own the widened logboat
too (300 d). Folkmar has her on the river run to Daventre, which you have made three times, under an order of one
sentence. You did not see him go. Six days on, a skipper says he was well at Daventre; his notch on the rule is
three days off. The king's new penny has come: pay the count 480 by tale or 367 by the moneyer's weight. The Witla
keel's skipper has told you true four times and stale once. Thiadbod's man has lied to you twice. Three promises
stand on the rule and a fourth is offered: the frame asks which you will break, and names who will mind.

### Hour fifty

About 866, the third head of the house (g). The frame is the one from the first minute, with seven buttons in the
rack. Home is Daventre: Dorestat burned four times, and the fourth time your store was empty, because you had word.
The winter page has seven sticks and you break three: new orders for two captains, a keel laid in shares, the
younger son to the minster for his Latin. In spring you still sail one boat, and a leg is still two minutes. The
chart is purple where the house reaches: known waters from Dublin to Birka, two plots, five keels as hollow marks
with the age of the last word of each. One notch on the rule has turned red: Ubbi's son is nine days late from
Sliesthorp. The great army is in East Anglia, and a flag on the rule gives York forty days. You can carry that word
north in time, or sell wine to the army's camp. What you do goes into the chronicle in Byzantium under "Otherwise
than the annals tell", or it does not. The face in the portrait is fifty-one winters old. The draw comes this year
or next.

## 4. The systems

Five systems carry the game. The world's ships make prices and news. Talks and tidings turn that into words you
hold. A word chooses a leg. The leg spends days and meets signs and cards. Arrival settles the bet. Silver and
standing go to the debt, the winter and the house. What the house holds changes what you hear and where you may go.
And what you tell goes back into the world's ships.

### 4.2 The leg and its decisions

**The leg** is a record from cast off to mooring: the order, the word sailed on, the hours, wages and bread, the
signs and cards met, the field, the reveal and the reckoning. Every design assumes it and no code has it (`Voyage`
counts days, `Course` is a path).

**Sail on.** Empty hours pass at an hour a second, the rate a tide wait already uses. She stops dead for a sign, a
card, a sail made out, or the berth. A leg is one to three real minutes: Dorestat to Lundenwic is about 76 seconds
running and 45 deciding. Dates, distances and the clock's rule do not change. A season is an evening.

**The order.** Set at the quay, live at sea. This is "intricate controls" with no steering. It is also what you
later tell a captain: one language for your boat and for the fleet.

| Switch | Positions | What it costs | In code |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cast off | now, first light, on the ebb, when the wind serves | Each day waited: 1 d of wages, a day of bread, every word a day older. The ebb gives 1 to 1.5 knots for six hours | Half: `Rest`, `TideWait`, `WorldSim.FairWind` (private) |
| Way | inside, by the coast, straight across | Inside: longest, sheltered, dries at low water, toll stations. Coast: you can lie up; a lee shore in a gale. Across: shortest; nights at sea, no landing | Half: three cost rows for `Course.Profile` |
| At dusk | lie up, sail on | Lie up: the dark hours are lost (5 in June, 13 in October). Sail on: near a coast each dark hour is 2% to touch ground with no moon, 0.5% under one | Half: `Rest.Dark`, `Sight.MoonLit`, `Aground.Offer` |
| Sail (2) | full, reefed, down; shorten at a fresh wind, a strong wind, never | Full in a strong wind: 2% an hour to hurt sail or strake. Reefed: seven tenths of her speed (g); needs reef bands, 12 d | Half: `Travel.Speed` |
| In a calm (2) | row, drift, tow | Row: weary after four hours (g). Tow: upriver, needs gear, 15 d | Half: `VoyageRules.rowHealth` |

**Signs.** An order that covers the case is obeyed without a word. When it cannot be kept, or its cost has changed,
the man in that seat speaks: his head lights, one switch lights, the clock stops. Space is "as ordered". Five signs:
dusk with no landing in reach; the wind turned, risen or died; a sail; a bar on a falling tide; bread shorter than
the way. Four a game day at most, and never the same one twice in six hours (g). Signs come from the simulation, so
they never wear out. This answers "a crossing needs eight cards": it needs two cards and three signs.

**The measure.** A short count in today's wind and a long count in a foul one, walked along `Course.Plan` with
`Travel.Speed`, said as words and laid on the day rule. `Course.Days` and its flat 3.5 knots go: it said "about 0.1
days" for six hours.

**The reckoning.** One line after the sale: what the leg made after cost, toll, wages and bread, and whether the
word was true. Its pennies must equal the change in the purse.

## 5. Ten ideas

**1. "What tidings?" (STAKE)** Every port asks you first. Hearing is paid for by telling, and what you tell goes on
that port's board, where the world's ships read it and sail on it.
*Why here:* news already travels by ship in this game, as old as the ship. You become one of the ships. Hearing
stops being free, and your tongue can move a price for you or against you.
*Cost:* three nights. `Knowledge.Hear` and the boards exist. It needs the question, the worth of a tiding, one test.

**2. The field. (STAKE)** Every leg is a race you can see. The keeper says who left with what; they are grey sails
on the chart with their age; a sign speaks when you gain or lose on them; the reveal names them.
*Why here:* the 138 ships really sail and really sell. An hour matters because someone is ahead of you, not because
a card said so. It makes the order's switches bite with no new content.
*Cost:* two nights. `SimShip.dest` and `cargoNote` exist. It needs one query and three places to show it.

**3. The teller's record.** Every word you act on is marked true, stale or false at the reveal, against the man who
gave it. "The Witla keel: true four times, stale once."
*Why here:* a price is a person in this game. Strangers of the simulation become people you trust or doubt with
nothing written, and the same record is how you later judge your own captains.
*Cost:* one night, once the reveal exists.

**4. The window is the forecast.** No text says what the weather will do. The sky in the ship window shows what is
coming, and from where, six hours out. The weather-wise man adds one word.
*Why here:* you asked for the boat in her own place, bottom right. This gives that picture a job: it is the
instrument you read before you set the order.
*Cost:* two nights for `ShipView` and a drawn stand-in. The 3D stage reads the same record later.

**5. Byzantium means yours.** One colour, one meaning: your sail, your course, your known waters, your pins, your
plots, your keels, your lines in the chronicle, the choices only you can make.
*Why here:* the theme colour becomes the score. At hour fifty the size of the house is how purple the chart is, and
no number says it.
*Cost:* a rule for the palette and one layer: a night, then nothing.

**6. The pilot's verse.** Each water has a verse of four lines. A crossing gives a line; a pilot gives one. A whole
verse shows that water's bars on the chart, lets you send a boat there, and passes to the heir.
*Why here:* poetry carries weight. "Three crossings", known waters and the heir's inheritance become one thing you
can read aloud. It is the game's own device: say so in the credits, as for the day rule.
*Cost:* three nights in stage 2, and four lines of your writing for each water.

**7. The portrait is the life's clock.** At rest the face is yours, a year older each winter. The day it becomes
your son's is the handover.
*Why here:* a house with heirs needs you to feel a life pass. The old-school face in the frame does it with no
screen and no number.
*Cost:* four drawings for each head of the house.

**8. Name the year.** On the winter board you pick one line of your log and it names the year: "The year the bar
took the cask." The House panel lists the years. One key copies the saga out as plain text.
*Why here:* annals name years by what happened. The log is already plain sentences, so the saga writes itself, and
it is the page a player shows a friend.
*Cost:* one night in stage 2.

**9. The rival is a real ship.** Thiadbod is one of the 138. He reads Dorestat's board, so he hears what you tell
there. The reveal sometimes carries his name. He is the one bidding for your stick.
*Why here:* the written rival and the simulation meet. "Nothing is at stake" gets a face, with a dam head next to
yours.
*Cost:* two nights in stage 2: mark one `SimShip`, and lean his `TraderBrain` toward your best run.

**10. The hold lights the world.** Press a lot and the chart lights where it was made and where it is wanted, as
far as you have heard. Six dirhams in the first purse light a road toward the Caspian on day one.
*Why here:* it makes the colossal map the key you asked for. You learn the world from what is in your hands.
*Cost:* one night in stage 2. The goods table already holds the makers and the wants.

## 8. Six decisions that are yours

My advice is the first answer each time.

1. **Sail on.** May empty hours pass at an hour a second between stops, as a tide wait already does?
   Yes, with every stop named above: without it I cannot promise the hour. / Only to the next dawn or dusk. / No:
   keep 1x to 4x.
2. **The portrait.** Whose face is top right?
   Yours at rest, the speaker's while he speaks. / Always whoever speaks or stands watch. / Always yours.
3. **The first release.** How much is in it?
   One life, 793 to about 805, on twelve ports, with the whole world dim on the chart; later ages as releases. /
   Three lives before release. / The whole road to 1066 first.
4. **The ship window.** When does it turn 3D?
   A drawn stand-in on the final record in stage 1, the 3D stage in stage 2. / 3D before the hour is proved. /
   Drawn for good.
5. **This morning's wide wiring run.** What becomes of it?
   Hold it: keep the foundation, and let a loader in only when its stage opens. / Run the place loader only. / Run
   all eight now.
6. **The slave trade.** It was the richest trade of these seas, and the richest ending of silver_road is the pen.
   How does the game carry it?
   In the world and in the cards: you may carry or refuse, word of it sails as fast as news, and the heirs bear
   it. / In the world only, never your cargo. / Left out.
