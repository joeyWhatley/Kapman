# Kapman: from one boat to a house of ships

**The ask (4 Oct, late):** "research a way for the game to scale from slow and methodical at the start to managing fleets
of traders, and how that end game will look from a gameplay look standpoint."

**The short answer.** Do not speed the game up. Change what one turn is. At the start a turn is a leg of a voyage. Then
it is a season. Then it is a year of the house. Three things make that growth good where other games' late games go
wrong:

1. **You hand over only what you have done yourself.** A route you have sailed three times can be given to a skipper.
2. **Your ships are people you sent, not units you drive.** You know where they are only by letter, and a letter is as old as the ship that carried it. You cannot babysit them, so the game never asks you to.
3. **You never stop sailing.** One boat is always yours. The quiet, hands-on game of the first hour is still there in the hundredth.

---

## 1. What other games teach about growing

| Game | How it grows | What works | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Factorio** | By hand, then machines, then robots that build the machines | You do a thing by hand until you understand it, then earn the tool that does it for you. "Before robots, it is about careful placement and personal labor. After robots, it becomes a game of design at scale." Mistakes cost time, never the game. | Nothing here: it is the model. |
| **Patrician III** | One ship, then a captain and a trade route, then offices in other towns | Players call setting up a route "one of the most fun things to do", and save afterwards because it is "a lasting change". A route is a decision made once. | Late game is spreadsheets if you let it. |
| **X4: Foundations** | One ship to fleets, stations and an economy | The promise that you can always still fly yourself. | "Later in the game you mostly play from the map ... babysitting your traders who are constantly trying to commit suicide." "The AI is entirely incapable of making a decent decision on its own." The early game's feel is lost. |
| **Euro Truck Simulator 2** | Hire drivers, buy garages | Passive income feels like arrival. | "Once you get a certain amount of employees ... money becomes pointless, as there is little to spend money on other than more garages and trucks." No sink, no risk, no meaning. |
| **Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord** | A band, then clan parties, caravans, workshops, a kingdom | "The early and mid-game phases are particularly praised for their pacing." | "Once the player reaches Clan Rank 5 or 6, the game really stagnates." Clan parties are "a nuisance", always getting captured. The late game repeats one tedious verb. |
| **The Guild 2 and 3** | A craftsman becomes a family that holds offices over generations | Goals other than money: titles, council seats, marriages. "The game goes on until you die without an heir." | Thin simulation under the goals. |
| **Crusader Kings III** | A house across centuries | Each life ends; the house is the player. | Not a trading game. |

**Six lessons.**

1. Hand over only what the player has mastered by hand.
2. Handing over is a decision made once, with a lasting result, not a thing to tend.
3. The people you hand to must be competent and their competence must be visible, or the late game is babysitting.
4. Money needs things to become that matter: people, standing, buildings, safety, a name.
5. The first hour's verbs must still be there at the end.
6. Each scale needs its own danger, or growth is only bigger numbers.

## 2. How a real merchant grew, 793 to 1066

This is the period's own ladder, and it is richer than a tech tree.

- **Partnership.** The Norse *félag*: "to lay property together". Partners (*félagi*, the root of "fellow") pooled a cargo or a ship and shared what came back. The word is cut on many rune stones.
- **Shares and kin.** A boat was often owned in shares. Sons, brothers and in-laws sailed the second boat.
- **A host or factor in a far port.** A man who keeps your goods, buys at the right season, and writes or sends word by the next ship.
- **A plot in an emporium.** Dorestad, Hedeby and Birka were laid out in plots. York had its Frisian quarter; Mainz had one too.
- **Sworn brotherhoods.** The merchants of Tiel in 1020 "swear and drink together" and judge their own cases. At Sigtuna, two eleventh-century rune stones were raised by "the Frisian guild-brothers" for a dead member (U 379, U 391).
- **A lord's protection.** Charters of toll freedom, held mostly by abbeys; a merchant sailed under one for a share.
- **Rank by the sea.** An English text of the early eleventh century (*Geþyncðo*, among Archbishop Wulfstan's writings; older books give it to Athelstan): a merchant who crossed the wide sea three times at his own cost was worthy of a thegn's rights.
- **Bigger ships.** From a seven-metre boat with four men to the sea-going traders of about 1030 that carried 24 tons with six to eight men, and the Hedeby ship of about 1025 that carried 60.

Every rung is something a player can earn, see and lose.

## 3. The ladder: five stages, each with its own turn

| Stage | You are | One turn is | What you do with your hands | What you hand over | The new danger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The boat** | A man with a boat and a debt | A leg: hear, choose, sail, arrive, reckon | Everything: the cargo, the course, the tide, the talk | Nothing | Weather, the bar, a bad price, the debt's day |
| **2. The season** | A known trader | A sailing season from Easter to Martinmas, then winter | You sail every leg, but you plan the year: a buyer, a fair, a wintering | The crew does what it has learned: stowing, the routine berth | A bad year, a man leaving, raiders in summer |
| **3. The partnership** | A senior partner with two or three boats | A season, with letters | You sail your own boat. In winter you agree a partner's run | One route you have sailed three times, to a kinsman or partner, with orders in words | An honest man who is a poor skipper; a good one who wants a bigger share; a boat that does not come home |
| **4. The house** | A shipowner with factors | A year: the winter council, then your own voyages | The voyages that matter: opening a new sea, a court, a wedding, a ransom, a first crossing | Routes, a storehouse, a factor's standing orders to buy at the right season | Kings who tax, rivals who undercut, raids that go where the silver is, credit that fails |
| **5. The name** | A house with rank | A generation | What you choose to: you are old, and the heir sails | The house's daily trade | The succession, a feud, the great turns of the age: 834, 865, 991, 1016 |

Rough hours, to be tested: stage 1 for the first three hours, stage 2 to about ten, stage 3 to about twenty-five, stage 4
after that, stage 5 across lives. Nobody is pushed up: a player who loves stage 1 can stay there for a whole life.

## 4. The rules that keep the late game good

### 4.1 The three-crossings rule

A route can be given to a skipper only when you have sailed it three times yourself, end to end. The game then knows the
route, and so do you: its bar, its toll-keeper, its season. This is Factorio's rule, and it is also the period's: three
crossings made a merchant a thegn. Say so in the game when it happens.

### 4.2 Letters, not telepathy

You see your own ships only where your own eyes or a letter can. On the chart:

- a **solid mark**: seen today (she is in the same port, or in sight);
- a **hollow mark with an age**: where the last word put her, "Lundenwic, 9 days ago";
- a **faint mark ahead**: where her orders should have her by now.

Word comes as fast as a ship. A letter from a factor is a card: what was sold, what is wanted, who is sick. Your reply
goes by the next ship that way and arrives when she does. This is the game's own knowledge rule turned on your own
fleet, and it removes the babysitting other games suffer: there is nothing to click on between letters. You choose
people and orders, and then you sail your own boat.

### 4.3 People, not units

Each skipper and factor is a person with a name, a trait, a want and a fear, a skill at sea and a skill at the scales,
and a degree of honesty you learn over years. Kin are loyal and may be poor skippers. A partner is good and wants his
share. The world's AI traders already choose ports by what they believe about prices; your skippers use the same mind,
inside your orders. Their competence is shown plainly: the tally they bring back and the tale that goes with it.

### 4.4 Orders in words

Three slots and one rule, set once in winter:

> Carry **wine** to **Lundenwic**, bring **wool** home, be back by **Lammas**. Rule: **never cross after Michaelmas**.

Rules to choose from: sell at the first fair price; hold for the fair; shelter in any gale; never carry arms; give the
toll-keeper his cask. A skipper with a strong trait may bend a rule, and tell you so.

### 4.5 Silver must become something

So money never goes flat as it does in ETS2, every rung costs something other than more ships:

| Sink | What it is | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| A ship built | A winter, timber, a shipwright, the right port | A bigger hold or a second keel |
| A storehouse plot | A plot in an emporium | Buy in the cheap season, sell in the dear one |
| A factor's keep | A man and his household | Eyes, a stock, and letters from that port |
| Shares bought and sold | A part of another man's boat | Spread risk; ties to other houses |
| Gifts | A cask to a reeve, a cloak to a king's man | Standing, a charter, a blind eye |
| The church | Alms, a bell, a book, a relic | Standing with the Christian ports, a place to be buried, a scribe for letters |
| Kin | A dowry, a fostering, a ransom | Alliances, heirs, your men back |
| The king | The ship-levy, tolls, tribute years | Protection; or the cost of having none |
| Loss | A ship that does not come home | Nothing. It is the price of size |

### 4.6 The dangers grow with you

A small boat is beneath a raider's notice and beneath a king's. A house with a storehouse at Dorestad in 834 is exactly
what the raid comes for. Rivals watch your routes and undercut them. Credit lets you grow faster than your silver, and
fails in a bad year. The game's chronicle supplies the great turns; the house's size decides how hard they land.

### 4.7 You always have a boat

There is no stage at which the game is only the map. The owner of eight ships still sails one, and the game gives him
voyages only he can make: the first crossing to a new sea (nobody can be sent where you have not been), a king's hall
at Yule, a daughter's wedding at Ribe, a ransom carried north.

### 4.8 No time warp

The clock keeps its one rate. Late-game years pass by the player's own choice to wait: winter over, wait for a ship,
wait for the fair. A season in stage 4 is three to five voyages of your own, the letters between them, and the winter.

## 5. What the end game looks like

It is the same table as the first hour, with more on it. Nothing is replaced.

**The chart (depth 0).** Your ships are on it in your house's colour, Byzantium: solid, hollow or faint by how old the
word is. Each has a thin thread along its ordered route. Your factors' houses are small flags on their ports. A sixth
map layer, **House**, with chips: Ships, Routes, Factors, Owed to you. The other sails of the world are still there,
and some of them are your rivals'.

**The day stick (depth 1).** A second row of notches: when each of your ships is due. A notch that passes with no ship
turns from gold to red. The tally shows the house's silver by where it lies: aboard, in the storehouse, lent out, owed.

**Your things (depth 2).** A sixth tab on the left, **House**, with sub-tabs: Ships (each with her skipper, orders and
last word), Men (skippers, factors, partners, kin, each a person), Stores (what lies in which storehouse), Shares.

**The place (depth 3).** A port where you have a plot gains a sub-tab, **Your house**: the stock, the factor, his
standing orders.

**The card (depth 4).** Letters. "Folkmar writes from Lundenwic. Nine days old. The wine sold at 80. The abbot wants ten
more casks by Lammas. Ubbi has a fever." Three replies, and the reply sails when a ship does.

**One new screen: the winter council.** Once a year, the clock held. The year's log is read back. Then the plan for
spring on one page: each ship, her skipper, her orders; what to build; whom to marry, foster or bury; what to give.
It is the only management screen in the game, and it comes once a year.

A late-game moment, as the player would see it: *June. Your own boat lies at Hedeby, the first of your house to come
this far. Two hollow marks sit on the chart, one at Lundenwic nine days old, one somewhere off the Frisian coast that
should have been home at St John's. The day stick has a red notch. A skipper in from Dorestad has a letter for you.*

## 6. How it maps onto what is built

| Part | Already there | To build |
|---|---|---|
| Skippers who trade by what they believe | The AI fleet (138 ships, trader brains, beliefs, lanes, wintering) | An owner on a ship; orders that fence the brain in; the tally and the tale on return |
| Letters | News carried by ship, price notes with age and source | A letter as a card; replies that travel |
| The three-crossings rule | The voyage log, the course planner | A count of routes sailed end to end |
| Storehouses and factors | Markets with stock and seasons | A stock that is yours at a port; a factor's standing buys |
| The House layer and tab | The chart, the map layers and rail of the new layout | One layer, one tab, age marks |
| The winter council | The clock held in panels; the concept's winter reckoning | The screen |
| Sinks | Reputation, gifts, commissions, the debt | Ship-building over a winter, plots, shares, dowries, the levy |

Order: the three-crossings count and the log first, because they need nothing new. Then one partner with one boat and
orders in words. Then letters. Then the winter council. Then storehouses and factors. Each step is playable.

## 7. Your decisions

1. **The three-crossings rule** as the gate for handing a route over.
2. **Letters only**: no live view of your own ships beyond sight. (The alternative, seeing them always, is easier and loses the game's best idea.)
3. **How many keels at the most.** History suggests a great house had a handful. I would cap at eight.
4. **You always sail one boat**, even as an old man; or the heir sails and you stay ashore from a certain age.
5. **Rivals as named houses** that grow as you do, three or four of them, met in ports and on the water.
