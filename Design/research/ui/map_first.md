# UI research: games where the map is the screen

6 October 2026. Twelve games, each asked the same five questions:

1. What is always on screen, and how much of the screen does it take?
2. What opens when you click a place?
3. How many clicks to buy, and to sell?
4. How are time and money shown?
5. What makes it feel made by a hand?

Then section 13 lays them side by side, and section 14 says what Kapman's chart should take.

## How to read this

- **Percentages are of screen area at 16:9, judged by eye from the screens as I know them, not measured on
  screenshots.** Take them as right to about five points. Before a layout is copied pixel for pixel, lay a ruler on a
  real screenshot.
- **Clicks are counted from the moment the ship (or the person) is at the place**, to the moment one unit of one good
  has changed hands. Walking, sailing and reading are not clicks.
- **(?)** marks what I could not check tonight. Only five pages answered usefully (see Sources); the rest is from
  knowing the games. Corner positions of small HUD parts are the likeliest things to be wrong; what the parts are,
  and how they work, is solid.
- The sketches are 16:9 wireframes. `#` is world or map, boxes are interface.

## 1. 80 Days (inkle, 2014)

The closest relative Kapman has: a journey sold as a map, a clock as the enemy, and a market in every town.

**Always on screen.** A globe, drawn flat like cut paper, fills the whole screen behind everything. Over it:

- A thin top strip, about 6% of the height: the day ("DAY 12") and a clock with hands or digits that really runs,
  and the purse in pounds ("£3,240"). Fogg's health is a number from 0 to 100 beside a small round portrait (?).
- In a city, a row of four to six round buttons along the bottom, about 10% of the height: Explore, Market, Bank,
  Hotel (sleep to the morning), Plan (the routes out), Depart.
- Nothing else. Interface in all: about 15%. Globe and city picture: about 85%.

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
| DAY 12   10:42            LONDON                   £3,240    |  6%
|##############################################################|
|#############  globe, flat colours, city as a  ###############|
|#############  black skyline on the horizon    ###############|
|######  o Paris ------- o Munich                ##############|
|##############################################################|
|   (Explore)  (Market)  (Bank)  (Hotel)  (Plan)  (Depart)     | 10%
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Click a place.** In Plan, the globe turns to show the cities you can reach, each a dot with its name, joined to
where you stand by an arc. Tap a dot: a small card comes up beside it (about 25% of the screen) with five lines and
one button: the kind of transport, when it leaves, how many days it takes, the fare, how many suitcases it will
carry. Routes you have not learned are not drawn at all; you learn them by exploring, by talking on the journey and
by buying timetables. A route that leaves in three days makes you wait, and the clock shows you waiting.

**Buy and sell.** Market opens one screen in two halves: the stall above (four to eight things, each with a small
picture, a price and one line), your suitcases below as grids of slots (a case holds a handful of things; big things
take more slots) (?). Tap a thing, tap Buy: **2 taps.** Tap a thing in a case, tap Sell: **2 taps.** The line under
a thing is the whole trading game: "Sells for a high price in Yokohama." No price table anywhere. You carry a
promise, not a spreadsheet.

**Time and money.** The day number and the running clock are the top strip; the clock runs while you stand in the
market making up your mind, so dithering costs. Money is one number. The bank lends more for days of waiting: money
is bought with time, in so many words.

**The hand.** One illustrator's flat poster shapes (Jaume Illustration): each city is a skyline in three or four
colours. One display typeface, Art Deco in feel. The text arrives a paragraph at a time and the last sentence is the
choice. The globe shows other players' journeys as faint lines, so the world feels travelled. Nothing is boxed in
grey: there are no panels with borders, only type laid on colour.

**For Kapman.** The route card (transport, leaves, days, cost) is the "Set course" card already built; add the cost
in stores. The one line under a good is "the word on the item". The clock that runs while you stand in the market is
the opposite of Kapman's decision (the clock is held in panels), and that is right for a game of years, not days.

## 2. Sunless Sea (Failbetter, 2015)

**Always on screen.** The sea from straight above, nearly black, the ship in the middle with a cone of lamplight.
The sea is the map: place names are written on the water itself, in a serif face, when you first come near
("VENDERBIGHT"). Over it, small instruments in the corners, about 12% in all:

- Lower left (?): the engine telegraph (full, half, stop, astern), the lamp switch, the hull as a bar.
- Lower right (?): four numbers with small pictures: Fuel, Supplies, Terror (0 to 100), Echoes (the money). Crew as
  a number.
- Bottom centre: the guns, each a round button that fills as its aim comes on.
- Top right: a round mark that fills slowly: "Something awaits you". It is the only clock on the screen.

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
|##########################################  (S.A.Y. dial)   ##|
|#####################   V E N D E R B I G H T   ##############|
|##############   dark sea, top down     ######################|
|######################   >ship, light cone   #################|
|##############################################################|
|[telegraph][lamp]      (gun)(gun)       Fuel 12  Supplies 9   | 12%
|[hull =====    ]                        Terror 34  Echoes 210 |
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The full chart is a second screen (one key): a parchment sheet, your track not drawn, tiles you have not seen left
blank. It is for looking, not for playing; you cannot set a course on it.

**Click a place.** You do not click; you sail into a port and press dock. The Gazetteer opens over about 80% of the
screen, a book with tabs down or across its edge: Story, Hold, Officers, Shops, Journal. Story is a column of cards:
a square painted picture (about 80 px), a title, a paragraph, and under it one to five choices, each with its own
small picture, the things it needs shown as little squares (lit if you have them, dark if not) and a Go button. The
sea stays visible, dimmed, round the edge.

**Buy and sell.** Shops tab (1), pick a shop from the list if the port has more than one (1), click a good to buy
one of it (1): **2 or 3 clicks.** Your hold is listed on the same page; click a thing there to sell one: **2 or 3
clicks.** Buy and sell price sit side by side on each thing. No price history, no "prices elsewhere": you remember,
or you write it down. Players did write it down, on real paper, and that became part of the game's fame.

**Time and money.** No date on the sailing screen. Time is felt as three numbers falling (fuel, supplies) and one
rising (terror). The date exists only in the journal. Money is Echoes, one number.

**The hand.** Hundreds of small square paintings by one hand (Paul Arendt), one for every thing, person and choice.
Names written on the water. The writing does the rest: a port is three paragraphs and you see nothing of it but a
few roofs from above.

**For Kapman.** Names written on the chart as she nears, not on labels with backgrounds. The "needs" squares on a
choice, lit or dark, show why a choice is shut without a sentence of explanation. The warning: a full-screen book
over the sea is what Kapman has already decided against (beside, never over).

## 3. Dredge (Black Salt Games, 2023)

Not a map game by its camera (you sit behind the boat in 3D), but the cleanest harbour interface of the twelve, and
the source of Kapman's rule that time stands still in port.

**Always on screen.** At sea, very little, about 5%:

- Top centre: a small round dial, half sun, half moon, with a hand and the hour in figures. Under it, when things
  go wrong, an eye that opens wider: the panic.
- Top right: money ("$342.50").
- Top left: the errand you are following, one line.
- Bottom left: the tool in hand (a lamp, a horn) as one round picture.

**Click a place.** Sail to a jetty, one key: the camera leaves the boat and you get the **dock screen**. It is one
still painting of that harbour, filling the screen, and the places you can go are name plates set on the painting
where the buildings stand: Fishmonger, Shipwright, Mayor, Storage, and "Undock" at the foot. The clock stops. Click a
name plate: the person stands on the left as a tall painted portrait (about 30% of the width, cut off at the waist),
their words in a box under them, and their goods as a grid on the left, your hold as a grid on the right.

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
|                         (dial 14:20)                $342.50  |
|  +---------+   +----------------+    +--------------------+  |
|  |         |   | their grid     |    |  your hold, the    |  |
|  | portrait|   | [][][][]       |    |  shape of the hull |  |
|  | painted |   | [][][][]       |    |  [][][][][]        |  |
|  |         |   +----------------+    |  [][][][][][]      |  |
|  +---------+   "Fresh today?"        |  [][][][][]        |  |
|                [Sell all]  [Leave]   +--------------------+  |
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**Buy and sell.** Dock (1), Fishmonger (1), click a fish in your hold to sell it (1), or Sell all (1): **3 clicks.**
Buying a rod or an engine: click it in their grid (1), then click where it goes in your hold (1): **4 clicks**, and
the fourth is the interesting one, because the thing has a shape and must fit. Each fish shows its price as it is
hovered, and how fresh it is as a small mark that worsens.

**Time and money.** The dial; time moves only when the boat moves or a line is in the water. Money is one number
with pence. Night is told by the dial, the light, and the eye.

**The hand.** The portraits: thick, visible brush strokes, hard shadows, the same painter throughout. The harbour as
one painting with names set on it, not a menu. The hold as the hull's own outline, so the inventory is a picture of
the boat. The fish book: each catch drawn as in a field guide.

**For Kapman.** This is the port panel to copy: one picture of the place, the things to do as names set on the
picture, one person, two grids. It also answers "the hold as a grid": draw the Gislinge boat's plan from above and
let casks and bales fit between the ribs.

## 4. Pentiment (Obsidian, 2022)

**Always on screen.** Nothing. 0%. The scene is a painting in the manner of a manuscript page and an early woodcut,
seen from the side; the people walk on it. A small hand appears over what can be touched. The only standing marks are
one or two key hints in a lower corner.

**Click a place.** Press the book key and the picture pulls back: what you were looking at was an illustration on
the left page of an open book lying on a desk. The book takes about 90% of the screen. Its edge has cloth tabs:
Journal (what you have promised to do), Map, People (a page of small portraits joined by family lines), Glossary.
The map is a drawn bird's-eye view of the valley with buildings in elevation, as a 1500s town view; places you may
go now are marked with a small figure; you choose one and walk there. Words in the dialogue that are underlined in
red have a gloss: press, the view pulls back to the page margin, and a small drawn pointing hand shows a note in a
smaller script, with a picture of the person or place.

**Buy and sell.** None. There is no purse. What you spend is time.

**Time and money.** The day is cut into the hours of the monastery: Matins, Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext, None,
Vespers, Compline. When you choose to do a thing that takes an hour, a wheel appears, a round calendar picture with
the hours round its rim and small scenes of the day's work, and turns one place on. That is the only clock. Meals
are scenes: you choose whose table to sit at, and the food on it tells you how rich they are.

**The hand.** Everything. Each speaker's words are written out stroke by stroke in a script that fits who they are:
a quick cursive for country people, a formal book hand for monks, a rounded Italian hand for the schooled, and for
the printer real type, set letter by letter, upside down first. The ink goes down wet and dark and dries paler. The
writer makes a slip, scratches it out and writes it again. Holy names are left blank and filled in red a moment
later. When the speaker's mood changes the hand grows hurried and blots.

**For Kapman.** The wheel of the day is the day stick's cousin: time shown only at the moment it is spent. The red
underlined word with its note in the margin is the right way to carry the period's hard words without a codex screen.
And the brief's rule "plain wording" is Pentiment's too: the period is in the lettering and the pictures, not in
"thee" and "thou".

## 5. Return of the Obra Dinn (Lucas Pope, 2018)

**Always on screen.** Nothing but the ship, in two colours. 0% interface; a small book mark and watch mark show in a
corner when either can be used. The picture is drawn at a low size (800 by 450, raised from 640 by 360 during the
making) and each grey is turned into a pattern of dots.

**Click a place.** There are two "places": a body and the book.

- A body: the watch opens on its own, one click, the screen goes black, you hear the death, then stand inside the
  frozen moment and walk round it.
- The book: about 90% of the screen, an open ledger, two pages. Its parts, in order: the contents (ten chapters,
  each with numbered parts); a plan of the ship's decks with a cross where each body lay; three drawings of the whole
  company, made by an artist aboard, every face in them; the muster roll of sixty names with rank and country; a
  glossary; then one opening for every death: the frozen scene on the left with the words that were spoken, and on the
  right one sentence with holes in it: "This unknown soul [was ...] by [...]."

**Buy and sell.** None. The comparable act is entering a fate: click the face (1), click the name hole (1), pick a
name from the roll (1), click the fate hole (1), pick a verb (1): **5 clicks**, and no answer. The book says nothing
until three fates are right at once, then sets all three in type together. That one rule stops guessing.

**Time and money.** No clock. The chapters are the order of time, read backwards. Money appears once, at the end, as
an insurance reckoning in pounds, shillings and pence for each estate.

**The hand.** Two colours. A choice of old screen tints named after the machines. The company drawn in pen. Faces in
the roll are blurred until you have seen the man clearly enough, which tells you what you know without a number. A
dotted line on the deck plan shows where one man went.

**For Kapman.** The sentence with holes is a better form than a menu for anything the player must state: a toll
declared, a price guessed, a riddle answered. "The book says nothing until three are right" is the right rule for
the chest's riddles. Blurred until known is the fog of war applied to people and prices.

## 6. Sid Meier's Pirates! (1987; remade 2004)

**Always on screen (2004).** The sea in 3D from above and behind, the Caribbean's shores drawn as a soft chart at
the edges. Interface about 10%:

- Lower left (?): a small parchment with gold, crew, food and the date as month and year.
- Lower right (?): a round chart of the waters nearby with towns as dots.
- A face for the crew's mood, from glad to mutinous.

The full chart is one key away: a parchment sheet with every town as a small flag in its nation's colours; click a
town to read what is known of it (wealth, soldiers, who holds it); pieces of treasure maps are kept beside it as torn
corners with a landmark on each.

**Click a place.** Sail into a town: a parchment list comes up over a picture of the town, about 35% of the screen,
seven plain lines: Visit the Governor, Visit the Tavern, Trade with the Merchant, Consult the Shipwright, Divide the
Plunder, Check Status, Set Sail. The 1987 game is the same list over a still painting. This list has not been
bettered in forty years.

**Buy and sell.** Trade with the Merchant (1) opens a table: one row for each of six kinds of cargo (food, luxuries,
goods, spice, sugar, cannon), your ship's amount on the left, the merchant's on the right, the price between, and an
arrow each way. Click an arrow (1): **2 clicks** to move one lot, then one click a lot. Your gold and the hold's free
room are at the foot and change as you click. Whether the town is rich or poor is said in a word at the top, and
that word is the price.

**Time and money.** Month and year. Time runs only at sea. The real clock is the captain's age: the men grow harder
to lead, and at last you retire and are ranked by what you ended as, from pickpocket to governor. Money is gold, one
number; "divide the plunder" turns the voyage's gold into the life's score and starts a new voyage.

**The hand.** In 1987, one still painting for every kind of place and a printed paper chart in the box that you
really used. In 2004, parchment and torn map corners. The treasure maps are the best thing: a scrap with one hill,
one wreck and a cross, and you match it to the coast yourself.

**For Kapman.** Seven plain lines for a port. A word for the town's wealth instead of a number. "Divide the plunder"
is the Reckon beat and the winter screen in one. Age as the long clock is the life with heirs.

## 7. Uncharted Waters: New Horizons (Koei, 1993)

The grandfather of the kind: a merchant, a small ship, the whole coast of the old world, prices that differ by
port.

**Always on screen, at sea.** The screen is split once, upright. The left three quarters is the sea from above in
small tiles, the fleet as one ship. The right quarter, about 25%, is a fixed column of instruments (?):

- the date (day, month, year),
- a compass rose with the heading,
- the wind as an arrow and a strength, the current as a second arrow,
- the speed,
- water and food as two numbers,
- days at sea.

```
+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
|##############################################| May 12 1522   |
|##########  sea in tiles, top down  ##########|   (compass)   |
|##############################################| Wind  NE  3   |
|#################  >ship  ####################| Tide  W   1   |
|##############################################| Speed 6       |
|######   coast, a port as a small house  #####| Water 40      |
|##############################################| Food  38      | 25%
+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
```

The world chart is a separate screen, and it starts covered in cloud; the cloud is cleared only where your own ship
has been. Selling what you have charted to a map-maker is one of the ways to earn.

**Click a place.** Sail onto a port and you are put ashore as a small figure in a town drawn in tiles, in the
building style of its part of the world. You walk to a door: market, shipyard, harbour, inn, lodging, guild, palace,
church, and in the larger ports a bank. Through a door: the keeper's portrait in a frame on the left, about a third
of the screen, and a box of four or five commands on the right.

**Buy and sell.** In the market: Buy (1), choose a good from the list with its price (1), set how many (1 or more),
choose which ship takes it (1), agree to the price or bargain (1): **about 5 inputs**, selling the same. Bargaining
is a sentence: the merchant names a figure, you say more or less, he may lose patience. "Market rate" tells you
whether this port's prices stand high or low as a percentage, and it moves when you or others trade heavily.

**Time and money.** The date as day, month and year, always in the column. Money is gold pieces; ten thousand make
an ingot, which a bank keeps.

**The hand.** Small portraits with real character. A different tile set and tune for each coast. The cloud over the
chart.

**For Kapman.** The fixed column proves that a quarter of the screen given to instruments is too much: three
quarters of what it shows is not needed three quarters of the time. Walking to doors is charming for an hour and a
tax for the next forty; Pirates' list beats it. But the cloud that lifts only where she has been, and charts as a
thing to sell, are both already in Kapman's plan and both come from here.

## 8. Heaven's Vault (inkle, 2019)

**Always on screen.** Nothing fixed. Walking, the world is in 3D and the people are flat drawings standing in it.
Sailing, your boat rides rivers of air between moons, and the only interface is an arrow at each fork (left or
right), which your companion calls out. 0 to 3%.

**Click a place.** Three things open, each full screen:

- **The chart of the rivers.** Moons as named dots on a web of streams. Choose one; the game then calls the turns
  for you. A place you have only heard of is not a dot but a hazy patch: "somewhere in this reach". Each find narrows
  the patch until it is a dot. This is the best fog of war of the twelve.
- **The timeline.** One long line from this minute back through thousands of years. Zoomed in, it is what you did
  this morning; zoomed out, it is the history of the world, with your guesses marked as guesses. It is the journal,
  the quest log and the lore book in one object.
- **An inscription.** The carved line across the top; under each group of signs, two to four words to choose from;
  a guess is written with a question mark and stays a guess until a second inscription agrees or disagrees.

**Buy and sell.** No money worth the name. You give a thing you found for what a person knows: pick the thing (1),
offer it (1): **2 clicks.** What you get is a place on the chart.

**Time and money.** No clock. The timeline is the clock, read after the fact.

**The hand.** The people are drawn by hand and do not walk: they fade from one drawn pose to the next, like the
pages of a picture book, and it works. The script was designed as a real writing system and looks cut by a chisel.

**For Kapman.** A known-of place shown as a hazy patch that narrows is exactly "position offshore as a fuzzy area"
(decision 11) and is how a rumoured port should first appear on the chart. A guess that stays marked as a guess is
how a price heard third-hand should look. The timeline that runs from this morning to the making of the world is
what the voyage log and the chronicle become if they are one scroll.

## 9. Citizen Sleeper (Jump Over The Age, 2022)

**Always on screen.** A space station seen from outside as a model you drag round and zoom, filling the screen.
Places are small round marks floating on it, joined by thin lines; a mark with something new pulses. Over it,
about 12%:

- Top left: two short bars, Condition and Energy, and under them this day's dice, up to five, each showing its face.
- Top right: the day ("Cycle 14"), the money, and the button that ends the day.
- Right edge, when called: the list of what you are working toward, each with a round clock cut in segments.

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
| Condition [=====  ]                    Cycle 14   Cryo 62    |
| Energy    [===    ]                         [End cycle]      |
| [5][4][2][1][ ]                                              |
|##########   the station, turning slowly   ####+-------------+|
|#######   o Yard ---- o Noodle stall  #########| NOODLE STALL||
|##############   o Docks (pulsing)   ##########| two lines   ||
|###############################################| Eat  [ ] 8c ||
|###############################################| Work [die]  ||
+-----------------------------------------------+-------------++
                                                   panel 30%
```

**Click a place.** A panel slides in on the right, about 30% of the width; the station stays visible and still
turns. In the panel: the place's name, two lines about it, and its actions. Each action is a line with an empty
square beside it. Drag a die into the square, or click the die then the square. Before you let go the action shows
its chances as three figures, good, middling and bad, and a word for the kind of risk: Safe, Risky, Danger. Talk
opens a larger sheet: the person drawn tall on the left, their words on the right.

**Buy and sell.** Click the stall (1), click the action "Buy, 8" (1): **2 clicks.** Selling is the same. There is
no quantity and no hold screen: a thing bought is a line in a short list.

**Time and money.** Time is the day's dice: when they are spent the day is spent, and you press the button. The
clocks are the calendar: a ring of six or eight segments on each thing that is coming, filling one segment a day or
one a deed, on the map at the place where it will happen. Money is one number.

**The hand.** One comics artist drew every person (Guillaume Singelin). The words of a tabletop game are used
without shame: clock, drive, die. The station is a model, but the marks on it are flat and plain like pins in a
board.

**For Kapman.** This is the layout the concept asks for: the map never covered, a 30% panel on the right, a few
actions each with its odds as a word. The ring that fills, pinned on the map where the thing will happen, is the
day stick's notches seen from the other side: put the same mark on the stick and on the port.

## 10. Wildermyth (Worldwalker Games, 2021)

**Always on screen, between fights.** A land drawn as an inked and coloured sheet of paper, cut into a few dozen
pieces like a puzzle; each piece is one place. The heroes stand on it as paper figures on little bases. Over it,
about 12%:

- Top, a strip about 7% high: the calendar. A row of day marks runs left to right; set on it, ahead of today, are
  the things that are coming, each a small picture with a number of days: a raid, the enemy growing stronger, the
  end of the chapter. This is the nearest thing in any of the twelve to Kapman's day stick.
- Top left: the stores (a few kinds of material, each a picture and a number) and the house's renown.
- Bottom left: the heroes as a row of small heads; click one for his page.

**Click a place.** Click a piece of the land: a small card by it, about 20% of the screen, names what can be done
there and what it costs in days: scout it (3 days), clear it, build on it, walk through it. Click a hero, click the
deed: the hero's figure walks there, a line is drawn, and the days appear on the calendar strip as a bar from today
to the day it will be done. Time moves only when you press the hourglass, and stops by itself when anything
finishes or arrives.

**Buy and sell.** No market. Things are made from stores: hero's page (1), the slot (1), the thing to make (1):
**3 clicks.**

**Time and money.** Days on the strip, years between chapters ("ten years of peace"), in which the heroes age,
marry, have children and retire: the same idea as Kapman's winter, on a longer scale. No coin.

**The hand.** Paper. The figures are flat cut-outs with a white edge, the scenery stands up like a toy theatre, the
stories are told as comic-strip frames with speech balloons, and the land looks drawn with a pen and washed with
colour. A hero's scars and changes are drawn onto his figure and stay.

**For Kapman.** The calendar strip with coming things set on it by their day, and a deed shown as a bar on it before
you agree, is the day stick, already proven. The clock that stops by itself whenever something arrives is the pacer
("it drops to 1x on any card").

## 11. Curious Expedition (Maschinen-Mensch, 2016)

**Always on screen.** A land of six-sided fields in small pixels from above, mostly cloud at the start, your party
as one small figure. About 75% map. Round it (?):

- Top centre: Sanity, a bar and a number out of 100. It is the fuel.
- Top left: the day.
- Bottom: the party as a row of small portraits with their health, and the pack as a row of things.
- Top right: a compass that points at the goal once someone has told you where it lies.

**Click a place.** Click a field once: the way there is drawn as a dotted line, and the sanity it will cost is
written at its end. Click again: you go. That first click, which costs nothing and tells you the price, is the whole
game. On arriving at a village, shrine or cave, a sheet opens over the middle, about 60% of the screen: a pixel
picture of the place above, a few lines written as the explorer's own diary below, and two to four choices.

**Buy and sell.** In a village, Trade (1) opens two packs side by side, yours and theirs, with a pair of scales
between. Click your things to put them on your side (1 each), click theirs to put them on theirs (1 each); the scales
tip; when they are level or in the villagers' favour, Agree (1). **About 4 clicks** for a plain swap. No coin in the
field: every thing has a worth, and the worth differs by who is asking (a village prizes what a museum does not). At
home afterwards, each find is given either to the museum for fame or sold for money: one choice a find.

**Time and money.** Days as a number; a table at the end ranks you against rival explorers by fame. Money exists
only between journeys.

**The hand.** Small pixels, and the diary: every happening is told in the first person in a few plain sentences,
built from parts, and read back at the end as the journey's account.

**For Kapman.** One click shows the cost, a second commits: this must be how a course is set (it nearly is: the
port's card, then "Set course"). The scales are a good picture for a haggle. The diary built from what really
happened is the voyage log read back in winter as a short saga.

## 12. FTL: Faster Than Light (Subset Games, 2012)

Not a map game on its main screen, but its map is the model for "one to three cards a leg".

**Always on screen.** Your ship cut open from above on the left, about 45% of the screen; when there is an enemy,
his ship in a framed window on the right, about 25%. Round them, about 20%:

- Top left: the hull as a row of 30 small blocks, green going red; shields as round marks under it; then three
  numbers with pictures: fuel, missiles, drone parts.
- Top, beside the hull: scrap, the money, one number.
- Top centre: the drive charging as a bar, and the JUMP button, dark until it is full.
- Left edge: the crew as a list of names with a health bar each.
- Bottom left: the engine's power as stacked bars under each system; bottom centre: the weapons as four boxes.

**Click a place.** JUMP (1) lays the sector map over the middle, about 55% of the screen: twenty or so dots on a
dark field; lines appear from your dot to those in reach when you hover; some dots are labelled (store, distress,
exit); and from the left a red curve, the pursuing fleet, covers more of the map after every jump. Click a dot (1):
**2 clicks to travel.** On arriving, a text box about 40% of the screen wide: a short paragraph and one to four
numbered choices. A choice you have only because of what you carry is printed in blue. The outcome comes as a second
short paragraph and a row of what you gained or lost, as pictures and numbers.

**Buy and sell.** At a store the panel has two tabs. Buy: each thing is a box with its price on a button; click it
(1): **1 click.** Sell (1), click your thing (1): **2 clicks.** Fuel, missiles and repairs are each one click a
unit. Nothing asks "are you sure".

**Time and money.** No clock and no date. The red curve on the map is the clock, and you can see exactly how many
jumps you have left to spend. Money is scrap, one number, top left.

**The hand.** Small pixels, one room at a time. The ships are drawn, not built. Plain words.

**For Kapman.** Time shown as something moving across the map itself (for Kapman: the sea closing from the north in
autumn, drawn on the chart as it comes). The blue choice: an option you have because of a man aboard or a thing in
the hold should look different from the rest, so that carrying it is felt. One click to buy, no "are you sure".

What the table says:

1. **The loved ones keep the standing interface at 15% or under.** Uncharted Waters at 25% is the only one that
   feels like a dashboard, and it is the oldest.
2. **Only two keep the map in view when a place opens:** Citizen Sleeper (a 30% panel on the right) and Wildermyth
   (a 20% card by the place). Both are praised for exactly that. Everyone else covers the map, and gets away with
   it only because their clock is stopped.
3. **Two clicks to trade is the norm; one is possible; five reads as old.** Nobody who is loved asks for a
   quantity in a box, and nobody confirms.
4. **Money is always one number.** Never a breakdown, never two currencies on the travel screen.
5. **Time is never a clock face with a date beside it, except in the two oldest.** It is a day count (80 Days,
   Curious Expedition), a strip with coming things on it (Wildermyth), rings that fill (Citizen Sleeper), a thing
   crossing the map (FTL), or it is shown only at the moment it is spent (Pentiment).
6. **The cost is shown before the click that commits,** on the map, at the far end of the line: days in 80 Days and
   Wildermyth, sanity in Curious Expedition.
7. **Made by a hand comes from five things, and none of them is detail:**
   - one artist for every person and thing (Sunless Sea, Dredge, Citizen Sleeper, 80 Days);
   - a material you can name: paper (Wildermyth), parchment and ink (Pentiment, Pirates!), two-colour dots (Obra
     Dinn), cut colour (80 Days);
   - lettering treated as drawing: names written on the sea, hands that differ by speaker, ink that dries;
   - the interface as an object that could lie on a table: a book, a ledger, a torn map, a dial, a hull;
   - no grey boxes. Where there is a panel it has the material's edge, not a window's.

## 14. What Kapman's chart should take

The concept (section 4.1) already says: the chart fills the screen; the day stick on top, the tally lower left, the
chest lower right; a port, a sail or a card opens beside the chart, never over it. The twelve bear that out. What
they add is sizes, counts and a few forms.

**The standing layout, with a budget of 12%.**

```
+--------------------------------------------------------------+
| |'|'|'|'|'|V|'|'|'|'|o|'|'|'|'|'|'|+|'|'|'|'|'|'|'|'|'|'|X|  |  day stick, 5%
|##############################################+---------------+
|#####  the chart, names written on it   ######|               |
|##############################################|  the panel,   |
|##########  >her mark ..... o Traiectum  #####|  30% wide,    |
|#####################  "1 to 2 days" #########|  only when    |
|##############################################|  called       |
|[silver 212 d | debt 480 | 9 days | hold]#####|          [box]|  tally 4%, chest 3%
+----------------------------------------------+---------------+
```

1. **The day stick: a strip 5% high across the top** (Wildermyth). Notches for days; today is where the knife is;
   ahead of it, small cut signs on their days: the count's day, the fair, the feast, the sea closing. When a course
   is offered, the days it will take are shown on the stick as a paler stretch from today, shortest to longest
   ("1 to 2 days"), before "Set course" is pressed. The same small sign is put on the chart at the port where the
   thing will happen (Citizen Sleeper's rings).
2. **The tally: one line, lower left, 4% high, a quarter of the width.** Four things only: silver, the debt, days of
   stores, and the hold as a small plan of the boat that fills. One number for money (all twelve).
3. **The chest: one small drawn box, lower right, 3%.** It opens into the right-hand panel, not a full screen.
4. **The panel: the right 30% of the screen, full height under the stick** (Citizen Sleeper). The chart slides left
   so that her mark stays in view. Everything opens here: a port, a card, a sail, the chest. This closes the first
   gap listed in chart_game.md ("open over the middle of the chart, on top of her").
5. **A port in the panel, from the top:** one picture of the waterfront (Dredge; a placeholder until the user draws
   it), the port's name written, one word for how the town stands ("thriving", "hungry", "burned": Pirates!), then
   at most seven plain lines (Pirates!): the market, the people, the yard, the church, news, wait, cast off. The
   sites and `do` lists of BRIEF_2 fill these lines; a port with two sites shows two lines, not seven empty ones.
6. **The market in the same panel, two clicks to trade** (80 Days, Citizen Sleeper): the market line (1), then a
   good (1) buys one lot of it and it drops into the hold's plan. Click a thing in the hold: it is sold. Hold a key
   to move ten. No quantity box, no "are you sure". Under each good, one line and no more: the word on the item
   ("A Frisian skipper said Lundenwic was paying well for wine. Last week.").
7. **One click shows, the second commits** (Curious Expedition, 80 Days): clicking a port on the chart draws the
   course as a dotted line, writes the days at its far end and lays them on the stick. Nothing has happened yet. "Set
   course" is the second click. Already nearly built.
8. **A card in the panel:** a fact in two or three sentences, two or three choices, each with its odds as a word
   (decision 18) placed where Citizen Sleeper places its risk word, at the right end of the choice's line. A choice
   that exists because of a man aboard or a thing carried is drawn in the theme colour and names him or it (FTL's
   blue choice). A choice that is shut shows what it lacks as a small dark sign (Sunless Sea).
9. **A place only heard of is a hazy patch on the chart,** which narrows as more is heard, and becomes a dot when
   she has been there (Heaven's Vault; decision 11 does the same for her own position). A price only heard is
   written paler than a price seen (Obra Dinn's blurred faces).
10. **Time that can be seen coming is drawn on the chart itself** (FTL): the sea closing in autumn as a line coming
    down from the north week by week; a gale as a patch that moves.

**The hand.** In the order of what it costs:

- Names written on the chart in one drawn alphabet, no label boxes (Sunless Sea). Costs a font.
- Panel edges that are the material's: a board with a cut edge, a strip of hide, not a window with a border. Note
  the present UI look is black see-through glass with a thin hard border and square corners: that is a decision
  already made, and this is the one place the research pulls against it. A way to have both is to keep the glass for
  the panel's ground and give only the day stick, the tally and the chest the look of objects. For the user to
  decide.
- The ledger written, not printed: a line in the voyage log appears stroke by stroke and dries a shade paler
  (Pentiment). Costs one shader and a font drawn for it.
- One picture a port and one small picture a good, all by one hand (Dredge, Sunless Sea). This is the user's own
  pixel work and cannot be hurried; the layout should look right with flat placeholders until then.
- Hard words carried in the margin (Pentiment): a word such as "sceatta" or "wic" underlined in the theme colour,
  and its note shown at the panel's foot when pointed at, with its source. No codex screen.

**What not to take.**

- A fixed column of instruments (Uncharted Waters): wind, tide and speed belong on the chart beside her mark, drawn,
  and only when they matter.
- A town to walk in before you can trade (Uncharted Waters). Charming, then a tax.
- A book that covers the sea (Sunless Sea, Obra Dinn, Pentiment). They can afford it; a chart game cannot, because
  the chart is the thing being read while the choice is made.
- A clock that runs in the market (80 Days). Right for eighty days, wrong for two hundred and seventy years.
- A date written in figures on the travel screen. A person of 793 says a feast and a moon (BRIEF rule 2); the stick
  can say the same when pointed at: "four days before Michaelmas".
