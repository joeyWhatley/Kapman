# One ship: how ten games grow, and what their late games feel like

**The ask.** "Research a way for the game to scale from slow and methodical at the start to managing fleets of
traders, and how that end game will look from a gameplay look standpoint." The first answer is
`Design/research_scale_to_fleets.md`. This paper tests it against ten games that begin, as Kapman does, with one
small vessel: Sunless Sea, Sunless Skies, 80 Days, Dredge, FTL, Sailwind, Uncharted Waters: New Horizons, Sid
Meier's Pirates!, Nantucket and Bounty Train.

## 0. The finding in short

**Most of them never leave one hull, and the two that do are told by their own players to go back to one.**

| Game | Does one become many? | What grows instead |
|---|---|---|
| Sunless Sea | No. One ship; a new one is a trade-in | The hull, and a line of captains who inherit |
| Sunless Skies | No. One locomotive | The engine, a bank, a line of captains |
| 80 Days | No. One valet, one suitcase | What you, the player, know of the routes |
| Dredge | No. One boat | Hull, engines, rods, lamps, the cargo grid |
| FTL | No. One ship a run | Systems, crew of up to eight (?), ships unlocked for later runs |
| Sailwind | Hardly. You may own more boats but sail one, alone (?) | A bigger boat, instruments, a furnished cabin, farther seas |
| Uncharted Waters: New Horizons | **Yes.** Up to ten ships, each under a hired mate | A convoy that moves as one mark |
| Sid Meier's Pirates! | **Yes.** Up to eight ships, taken as prizes | A convoy; only the flagship fights |
| Nantucket | No. One ship | More whaleboats and men aboard her |
| Bounty Train | No. One train | More cars, a stronger engine, later mines and shares |

Three things follow, and the rest of this paper is the evidence for them.

1. **Nobody in this list has built what the first paper proposes.** None of the ten sends a second ship off on
   her own while you sail yours. The two fleet games give you a convoy: one mark on the map with more hold. Their
   players strip it back to one ship because the extra hulls slow them and decide nothing.
2. **The late-game complaint is the same in nine of ten, in the players' own words:** the road repeats, the fear is
   gone, and the money has nowhere to go. "In the late game I had a lot of money and nothing to spend on."
3. **The games that keep their first hour are the ones that end before the road repeats** (80 Days, FTL) **or pass
   the game to an heir** (the two Sunless games). Kapman already has both tools: the year as the run, and the house.

## 1. Sunless Sea (Failbetter, 2015)

**One to many: it does not happen.** You start with the *Ligeia*-class steamer (hold 40, quarters 10; 2,000
echoes new) and may trade her in at a shipyard for one of six others: the steam launch (500 echoes, hold 5), the
cutter (3,000, hold 30), the corvette (4,000, hold 40), the merchant cruiser (8,000, hold 120, quarters 30), the
frigate (15,000, hold 70) and the dreadnought (30,000, hold 100, quarters 40). Two more come only from stories.
The wiki is blunt about the rule: "When you *Trade* your current ship into a new one you are really selling it to
buy another. This means you cannot keep past ships in London." There is no second ship, no skipper, no route to
hand over.

What multiplies is **captains in time**. A captain dies or retires and the next one inherits by rule:

- one legacy of five: Rival (half the Iron and one weapon), Pupil (half the Mirrors and half the money), Salvager
  (half the Veils and half the money), Shipmate (half the Hearts and one officer), Correspondent (half the Pages and
  the whole chart);
- a will, commissioned for 100 echoes, passes on the lodgings and the heirlooms;
- a child raised ashore (the Scion) lets the heir take two legacies, so Pupil and Salvager together pass on all
  the money;
- retirement needs a townhouse at least, and the "wealth" win needs a mansion and 30,000 echoes.

**Minute to minute.**

| Stage | What your hands do |
|---|---|
| Start (first 3 hours) | Steer a slow steamer out of London by hand into the dark. Watch four numbers: fuel, supplies, terror, hull. Reach a port, read its story, write a port report, carry it home for a little money and fuel. Die, mostly. |
| Middle | The map is known. You run the one or two trade loops that pay, between stories. You buy the corvette or the merchant cruiser and find she eats more fuel and food. |
| End | You cross the whole map again and again to finish an Ambition: fetch this from the far east, bring it to London, go back. The stories are read; the travel remains. |

**The screen at the end** (it is the screen at the start). Looking straight down on black water. Your ship, a few
centimetres long, in the middle, with a cone of yellow lamplight ahead of her. Shores are dark green and grey
shapes that come out of the black as the light touches them. Top left, a small brass cluster: the engine
telegraph, then bars for hull, crew, fuel, supplies and a terror dial (?). When you dock, a broad parchment
book (the Gazetteer) covers most of the sea: tabs along its top for the story, the hold, the officers, the shops,
the journal and the chart (?). A frigate looks like the steamer with a longer hull and a second gun flash. Nothing
else on the screen says forty hours have passed.

**What players praise.**

- "I think the first hours of play for this game are wonderful." ([review, not recommended, 62 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197970730557/recommended/304650/))
- "I am never simply going to 'zee to sell goods or kill monsters in a monotonous grind." ([review, 137 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198079430949/recommended/304650/))
- "My next captain started with enough wealth & heirlooms to instantly acquire a frigate with the best engine and guns money can buy." ([review, 90 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197971880699/recommended/304650/))

**What they hate about the late game.**

- "next thing you know ten hours have gone by and your still running sunlit mirror boxes back and forth for cash because your nice new ship consumes more fuel and supply." ([review, not recommended, 21 h; 569 found it helpful](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198000237362/recommended/304650/))
- "You can hold more goods in a bigger ship, but this requires more fuel, more crew, more food, and we're back at square one even though you plunked down an hour's grind's money on the thing." ([review, 29 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198800083534/recommended/304650/))
- "Sadly, it will start to feel slow once you start sailing the same routes over and over." ([review, 85 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198202843656/recommended/304650/))
- "Lategame was a bit of a drag, since there weren't many storylines left and I was basically grinding." ([review, 42 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198368131229/recommended/304650/))

**The makers agree.** From their own postmortem: "*Sunless Sea* is a stately game. You could reasonably call it a
slow game. But we've resisted speeding up the ship, because it would reduce the tension, the sense of space and
distance, and the menace of the dark." And: "It has the rich story, predictable early-game, slow pace, and flat
late-game difficulty arc of a CRPG." ([Failbetter postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/postmortem-failbetter-games-i-sunless-sea-i-))

**First hour kept or lost?** The look, the pace and the verbs are kept exactly. What is lost is the unknown.
Slowness was tension while the dark hid things; once the chart is full it is only time. The game has no way to
take a known road off the player's hands.

## 2. Sunless Skies (Failbetter, 2019)

**One to many: no.** One locomotive. You start in the "Spatchcock" reclaimed engine and buy others at the engine
yards of the larger ports: a nimble one, an armoured one, a long-hold freighter. What Failbetter added after Sunless
Sea is a set of things that last beyond one captain:

- **a bank** at the major ports with no limit on space, which "will be passed down upon your Captain's death"
  ([wiki, Bank](https://sunlessskies.fandom.com/wiki/Bank));
- the heir keeps more: "your legacy captain keeps your locomotive, bank account, some money, uncovered maps, and
  some of your achievements" ([review, 94 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197971880699/recommended/596970/));
- **prospects and bargains**: a port offers a good cheap and another port posts a wanted notice for it, so a trade
  run is a card you pick up, not a table you memorise (?);
- four regions joined by relay stations, each a separate map (?).

**Minute to minute.** Start: steer and strafe the engine through wreckage on port reports and one prospect at a
time. Middle: chain two or three prospects through the bank, buy the freighter, finish officers' stories. End: long
crossings between whole regions for an Ambition, with the level cap long reached.

**The screen at the end.** Looking down on a painted sky: brass rails, floating masonry, fungus, a green or blue
haze. The locomotive in the middle, an engine with a tender and a car or two, trailing steam. Bottom left, a
round brass dial cluster: hull, crew, fuel, supplies, terror, and a heat bar for the guns (?). A ring compass
round the engine points at known ports (?). Docked, the same story book as Sunless Sea, with a bank drawer under
the list of shops. The end screen differs from the first only in the engine's shape and a fuller bank.

**Praise.**

- "it has the weirdness and atmosphere of its predecessor without the frustrations or grindy elements." ([review, 141 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197983157173/recommended/596970/))
- "Your heir inherits your ship, so you don't start from scratch all the time" ([review, 26 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198041353132/recommended/596970/))

**Hate.**

- "There's no late game resource sink either, so there will come a time in this game where the player no longer engages in any way with it's gameplay." ([review, 101 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197998533452/recommended/596970/))
- "In the late game I had a lot of money and nothing to spend on" ([review, 70 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198099170856/recommended/596970/))
- "This gets worse when the map is largely traversed and running between ports becomes a sheer grinding in late game." ([review, 328 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198135671993/recommended/596970/))
- "25% increase from base speed by endgame is craaazy." ([review, not recommended, 57 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198022272502/recommended/596970/))
- "Your first captain can just stash a bunch of every item in the bank, and none of your subsequent captains will have to worry about items or money again." ([review, not recommended, 90 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198044161322/recommended/596970/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Kept in look and verbs; lost the same way as Sunless Sea, and one step further.
Because the heir keeps the engine and the bank, the second captain has no small-boat game at all. The generous
inheritance cured the first game's complaint (repeating the start) and removed the scarcity that made the start
good.

## 3. 80 Days (inkle, 2014)

**One to many: no, and it never wants to.** One valet, one master, one suitcase. Within a run you grow only in
funds and in the routes you have heard of. Between runs **the player** grows: you know that the airship from
Meteora exists. Bigger suitcases can be bought for more grid space (?). Running short of money is priced in days:
"being short on money will force you to wait several days for the bank transfer and even more if it's the
weekend." ([review, 27 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198027047516/recommended/381780/))

**Minute to minute.** The same loop from the first city to the last, about two to three hours a run: arrive;
explore to learn a route or two; glance at the market, where each item names the city that pays well for it; pick a
departure and its hour; read the journey as a short story with two or three choices; arrive. Start, middle and
end differ only in how tired Fogg is and how few days are left.

**The screen at the end.** A globe in black, white and one or two flat colours fills the screen and turns under
your finger. Cities are small labelled pins. From the city you stand in, two to four arcs curve out to the next
ones, each with a vehicle drawn on it, a price and a departure time. Top: the day ("Day 71"), the clock running,
the funds. Bottom: buttons for Explore, Market, Bank, Hotel, and the suitcase as a small grid. The makers found
this screen by taking things off it: "we locked all the routes on the globe", because with every route shown
"the market felt like a chore, and the choice of where to go next felt pointless as a result."
([inkle postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/postmortem-inkle-s-i-80-days-i-))

**Praise.**

- "If you play it excessively like me, it does start to get somewhat repetitive at a certain point, but there's so much content, so many side quests, and alteration in the market items that it really takes a long time to feel repetitive." ([review, 46 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198030866503/recommended/381780/))

**Hate.** There is little late game to hate, because a run ends at day 80 or so.

- "while some of the late-game stories (like the mutiny or the US train system) are a bit repetitive and bland" ([review, 9 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198039127913/recommended/381780/))
- "Feels repetitive and tedious." ([review, not recommended, 34 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198044861097/recommended/381780/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Kept whole. It does this by never growing the unit and by ending the run before a
road is travelled twice. Its lesson for a game that does grow: **a road should be hidden until heard of, and a run
should end.**

## 4. Dredge (Black Salt Games, 2023)

**One to many: no.** One boat, lent by the mayor against a small debt that is paid from your fish sales. She
grows in four hull stages at the dry dock (the wiki shows four boats), each bought with money and with timber,
scrap and cloth you dredge up yourself. Each hull gives a bigger cargo grid and more slots for engines, rods, nets
and lamps. Equipment is unlocked with research parts. The makers name the loop as "Go Out > Get Fish > Sell >
Upgrade > Go Out > Get Caught At Night", and say of the clock: "we still wanted players to feel the excitement of
being caught out at night, so that's why time became the resource instead."
([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trawling-in-the-deep-how-black-salt-games-made-spooky-fishing-rpg-i-dredge-i-))

**Minute to minute.** Start: a slow tub with a few squares of hold. You fish near the home island, fit the fish
into the grid, watch the sun, and creep home in fog with something behind you. Middle: faster engines, two lamps;
you cross to the other four island groups, each with its one trick and its one gear. End: a quick, well-lit boat
that outruns everything; you fill the last lines of the fish book and wait for spots to refill.

**The screen at the end.** A low, tilted view from behind and above the boat on teal water, islands in hard-edged
flat colour. The boat is now a broad trawler: a wheelhouse, a crane arm, a net boom astern, four lamps throwing
overlapping yellow circles. Top centre, a round clock with a sun and moon turning; beside it an eye that opens and
reddens as panic rises. Top right, the money. One key opens the cargo: a square grid of some sixty cells (?)
with engines, rods and lamps packed round its edge and fish shapes fitted in the middle, like a tidy jigsaw. In
the first hour the same grid had a dozen cells and one rod.

**Praise.**

- "The game is at its best when you're trying to get home for the night, slowly navigating a treacherous waterway in the dark while being hounded by some imaginary threat." ([review, 23 h; 416 found it helpful](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198015197386/recommended/1562430/))
- "The early game is where Dredge truly shines." ([review, 24 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198010794290/recommended/1562430/))

**Hate.**

- "By the end-game, dodging Lovecraftian horrors as you reel in your latest haul is routine, and Dredge is little more than an OK-ish fishing simulator with your sanity as just another resource to be managed." ([the same review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198010794290/recommended/1562430/))
- "I had completely upgraded my ship when I was only about 2/3 through the story, which took the proverbial wind out of the sails of the final 1/3 of the game." ([review, 14 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198002867677/recommended/1562430/))
- "I wish there was a late game money sink (I finished the game with ~50k saved up just by completing my pokedex)." ([review, 23 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198015197386/recommended/1562430/))
- "Once you are some what upgraded none of the "dangers" matter anymore killing all the tension you would get from exploring as well." ([review, not recommended, 13 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198093406642/recommended/1562430/))
- "An endgame prey to hunt that requires three yachts duct-taped together wouldn't have gone amiss." ([review, 28 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198057647272/recommended/1562430/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Lost, and by the upgrades themselves. Every hull and lamp buys a little of the fear
away, and the fear was the game. Dredge survives it by being twelve hours long. A game that runs to 1066 cannot.

## 5. FTL: Faster Than Light (Subset Games, 2012)

**One to many: no, by decision.** One ship for eight sectors, an hour or two. Scrap buys reactor power, system
levels, weapons, drones and crew. Between runs you unlock other ships to start in. The makers say they got there
by cutting: "By having one singular focus we were able to abandon everything else that didn't fit in line with
that vision." ([FTL postmortem](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-without-a-pitch---an-em-ftl-em-postmortem),
as read by the fetch tool; check the wording before quoting it on) Players asked for a fleet in the first week:
"why cant we take the ships we empty and have a sort of fleet to help us fight" (thread "Capturing Ships", 22
Sep 2012, found through [this forum search](https://steamcommunity.com/app/212680/discussions/search/?q=one+ship)).
It was never added.

**Minute to minute.** Start: three crew, two weapons, a jump, a text event with two choices, a short fight you
pause every few seconds. Middle: a shop, a choice between shields and a weapon you cannot afford both of. End:
the same verbs twice as fast: four weapons to time, boarders, fires, drones, a three-stage flagship.

**The screen at the end.** Black space. Left two thirds: your ship seen from above as a cutaway, fifteen or so
rooms in a grey hull, each with a system icon; eight small crew figures; a green shield bubble with four layers.
Right third: a window with the enemy ship, the same way, smaller. Top left: a long green hull bar, shield pips,
scrap, fuel, missiles, drone parts. Bottom left: a column of green reactor bars and a row of system icons with
power pips; next to them four weapon boxes with charge bars. Bottom right: drone boxes. In the first hour half the
rooms are empty and two weapon boxes are blank. It is the same picture, filled in.

**Praise.**

- "FTL manages to make all ships play and feel highly distinct, with their own advantages, disadvantages, and playstyles." ([review, 317 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198040718756/recommended/212680/))

**Hate.**

- "While this game is very fun to start, and without a doubt quite challenging, it does eventually get boring and repetitive." ([review, 20 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198066821688/recommended/212680/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Kept, because every run is a first hour, and the end is the first hour with every
slot full. The growth the player keeps is knowledge and a choice of starting hulls. It is the cleanest case here
for "many" meaning **many different boats to begin in**, not many at once.

## 6. Sailwind (Raw Lion Workshop, early access since 2021)

**One to many: hardly.** You begin with one small boat that depends on the home region you pick, and you sail her
by hand in first person: halyards, sheets, the wheel, a bucket. Money from carrying crates buys a bigger boat: the
sanbuq is 75 gold in one player's telling, the brig 110 gold at Fort Aestrin ([wiki, Brig](https://sailwind.fandom.com/wiki/Brig)),
the jong far more. A new boat arrives "completely empty, so relocating tools and supplies from your previous boat is
recommended", so the old one is not taken from you; you can own more than one, and they wait where you moored
them (?). There is no crew and no skipper to give one to (?). The gates are reputation (local missions, then
missions to another region) and nerve: the ocean crossing with a quadrant, a sun compass and a chronometer.

**Minute to minute.** Start: fifteen minutes to the next island with one crate, hand on the sheet, eye on the
telltale. Middle: a medium boat, three or four missions at once, a first night at sea, fishing off the stern for
supper. End: days at sea on the brig with eleven lines to tend, reefing before squalls, taking a noon sight,
bailing, then a harbour approach under one sail.

**The screen at the end.** No interface at all. You stand on a broad wooden deck in flat, bright low-poly colour.
Two masts with square and fore-and-aft sails, eleven belaying points each with a coiled line, a wheel aft. Below, a
cabin you furnished: a stove, a table with the paper chart held under a lamp, a bed, shelves of crates and water
barrels. The chart is an object in your hand; your position is a pencil mark you worked out. In the first hour the
same view had one mast, one sheet and a tiller. A second boat, if you kept her, is a hull at a pier a
day's sail astern (?).

**Praise.**

- "I enjoy the progression of sailing on the small ship you start with, to making enough money to buy a bigger ship, and eventually gaining the experience and confidence to sail across the vast ocean to a different archipelago." ([review, 215 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198802822350/recommended/1764530/))
- "This is my first play through and i just got the second ship. ahhhh space. worth all 75 gold." ([forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1764530/discussions/0/4134934813437369809/))

**Hate.**

- "It is, by the dev's own admission, 'a money sink for endgame'." (of hull damage that must be repaired at a yard; [review, not recommended, 166 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197960487331/recommended/1764530/))
- "Contracts pay noticeably too little, especially in the late game once you own a bigger ship with higher upkeep." ([review, 43 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198131231732/recommended/1764530/))
- "I have visited all the archipelagos, bought a Brig, fully upgraded it with the best equipment, and accumulated a lot of gold coins." (and then asks for businesses to own; [review, 124 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198183884357/recommended/1764530/))
- "you'll be playing for 20+ hours before you get a bigger ship." ([review, 3 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198151408244/recommended/1764530/))

On a wish for a steamship as late-game growth, another player answers for the game: "adding a steamship might go
against the core identity of the game." ([forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1764530/discussions/0/798967931812784813/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Kept better than any other game here, because the hands never leave the ropes and a
bigger boat is more ropes. Its late game runs dry for a different reason: there is nothing for gold to become but
another hull, and a sink added as a bill is resented by the very players who stayed 166 hours.

## 7. Uncharted Waters: New Horizons (Koei, 1993-94)

The Steam release is in Japanese only and its forum is mostly "Please release in English!", so there is little
English player talk to quote. The rules below are from the Koei wiki; the feel of the late game is from memory
and marked.

**One to many: yes, and this is the oldest and fullest model.**

- **The gate is a person.** Each extra ship needs a mate to be her captain: "Navigators are automatically put in
  this position once a new vessel has been added to the fleet." Mates are found in taverns and lodges, won over by
  treating them, paid a monthly wage each, and they leave if "constantly underpaid" or if you keep attacking the
  nation they favour. ([wiki, Navigator List](https://koei.fandom.com/wiki/Uncharted_Waters:_New_Horizons/Navigator_List))
- **The limit is ten ships.** ([wiki](https://koei.fandom.com/wiki/Uncharted_Waters:_New_Horizons))
- **Posts.** First Mate, Bookkeeper (who "can influence spending decisions and decide which port has the best price
  for certain goods"), Chief Navigator, Captain. A first mate with the celestial navigation skill unlocks **Auto
  Sail**: "Sail to a known port without manually steering the ship." Handing over the steering is gated by a
  man's skill and by the port being known.
- **The screens.** A Fleet menu (Fleet Info, Rearrange, Scrap), a Mate menu (Character Info, Wages, Appoint), a
  cargo menu (Cargo Info, Transfer between ships), and crew set by ship to three jobs: navigation, lookout, combat.
  ([wiki, Legend](https://koei.fandom.com/wiki/Uncharted_Waters:_New_Horizons/Legend))
- **The fleet is a convoy.** It sails as one sprite; in battle you command your flagship and give the others three
  two-way orders (Delegate or Target, Fight or Flee, Spread or Gather); "other navigators are directed by the AI".
- **What silver becomes.** Investment in a port's market and shipyard, which raises what it sells and builds and
  ties it to your flag (?); and three kinds of fame (trade, piracy, adventure), one of which each of the six
  characters must raise to move his story on.
- **An end.** "The game ends prematurely if the player's character is killed in battle, runs out of provisions, or
  reaches old age."

**Minute to minute.** Start: one small ship, a few casks of water and food counted in days, a hop along the coast
of Portugal with the wind arrow against you, a first cargo sold two ports on (?). Middle: three or four ships, a
bookkeeper telling you where sugar is dear, a circuit you repeat, a shipyard order that takes weeks to build. End:
ten ships on Auto Sail between ports you invested in, stopping only to load, with the story's last errands at the
far side of the world (?).

**The screen at the end** (SNES). A tile sea in two blues seen from straight above, coast in green and brown. One
small ship sprite in the middle stands for all ten. Down the right side a grey panel: the date and hour, a wind
arrow and strength, a current arrow, the fleet's speed in knots, food and water (?). Fleet Info opens a list over
the sea: ten rows, each a ship's name, type, captain, durability, crew and load. A port is a small town you walk
through as a figure, with a sign on each door (?).

**Praise and hate.**

- "Players can spend hours refining trade networks, hunting for rare discoveries, or building a formidable fleet, all while carving out a unique story shaped by their choices." ([review, 12 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198367471798/recommended/628170/))
- "Progress can feel slow, and the pacing demands a level of patience that not all players will be willing to invest." ([the same review](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198367471798/recommended/628170/))
- A print review of the day called the Genesis version an acquired taste for its bare presentation and constant money managing (GamePro, as reported on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncharted_Waters); not checked against the magazine).
- From memory, the common late-game complaint is that one good circuit with a full fleet makes gold meaningless long before the story ends (?).

**First hour kept or lost?** Mostly lost (D). The first hour is counting water casks against days; the last is a
list of ten rows that sails itself. What it gets right, and nothing else here does, is that **every added ship is
an added person** with a wage, a skill and a loyalty.

## 8. Sid Meier's Pirates! (MicroProse 1987; Firaxis 2004)

**One to many: yes, by capture, and the rules are worth reading closely** (all from the
[Pirates! wiki, Player's Fleet](https://sidmeierspirates.fandom.com/wiki/Player%27s_Fleet)).

- You begin with one ship. Board an enemy and you may "send a Prize crew": she joins you. The limit is **eight**.
- **Only the flagship fights.** "The rest of your fleet stays away from the action."
- **More hulls, more hold and more men:** in 2004 the fleet sets the most crew you may have.
- **Every hull costs men:** a skeleton crew each (8 in 1987; 6 to 24 by type in 2004). Short of that, "the entire
  fleet becomes slow and sluggish."
- **Every hull costs speed.** In 1987 the largest ship sets the fleet's sailing. In 2004 the ships follow in a V
  and a rubber band holds the flagship back: a lagging ship shows "Wait for me!".
- **Ships are lost one at a time:** to a hostile fort, to shoals, in a retreat, to a failed mutiny.
- **Dividing the plunder** ends a voyage: "you will immediately lose all Fleet ships except the largest one."
- **Age.** The captain slows with the years and is pressed to retire.

The wiki's own verdict: "many players choose to stick with one single ship for the majority of the game, adding
ships only for the purpose of selling them at the next visit to a Port."

**Minute to minute.** Start: one sloop, forty men, the wind on the nose, a first prize, a governor's letter of
marque. Middle: the loop at full stretch: sail, sight a sail, fight, board, duel, sell the prize, dance, promote.
End: the same loop with a name feared, an older and slower sword arm, the last lost relatives and the last named
pirates to tick off.

**The screen at the end** (2004). A bright toy Caribbean seen from above and behind: green islands, white surf,
towns as small clusters with a flag. Your flagship in the middle, drawn larger than true so she stands out; behind
her up to seven prizes in a V, each steering herself to keep station. One of them trails with "Wait for me!" over
her. A wind arrow; in a corner a small panel with gold, the count of men with a face for their mood, months of
food, the date (?). Fleet Status is a list of up to eight ships with type, damage, guns and upgrades; click one to
make her the flagship.

**Praise.**

- "It's still worth buying because those first few hours where you are learning the geography of the Caribbean and sailing around without any particular goal are a ton of fun." ([review, 13 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197961734538/recommended/3920/))
- "You keep your flagship, sell off the rest of your fleet and walk away with your share to start another crew." ([review, 76 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198075324976/recommended/3920/))

**Hate.**

- "So you spend hours hunting for the best frigate class ships and want to build a powerful fleet until you finally realise all you need and want is one Indian War Canoe." ([review, 86 h; spoiler tags removed](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198080645569/recommended/3920/))
- "And a fleet with six ships will sail slower than a fleet with two ships." ([forum, "What Ship to Use?"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3920/discussions/0/2673382428438313465/))
- "Watching your fleet and most of your goods disappear, leaving you with just a single ship, feels unfair and immersion breaking." ([review, 8 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198062506049/recommended/3920/))
- "Pirates is a pretty fun game, but - much like Sid Meier's other big hit Civilization - it kinda drags in the mid- and end-game." ([review, 13 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197961734538/recommended/3920/))
- A wish, eighteen years on: a new version that "allows a player to leverage their fleet in ship-to-ship combat." ([review, 45 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198032096578/recommended/3920/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Kept, by a trick: the game never really lets you leave it. The fleet is a purse with
sails, the voyage ends in a share-out that puts you back in one ship, and age ends the career. Players like the
reset when they choose it and resent it when it is done to them.

## 9. Nantucket (Picaresque Studio, 2018)

**One to many: no. The ship gets more boats.** "You start off in a small 3 man sloop and work your way up to a
bigger ship and hiring more crew." ([review, 18 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198203990337/recommended/621220/))
The unit of growth is the **whaleboat**: a bigger ship carries two, then three, and each boat is a team in the
whale fight. Gates: the price; a crew skill ("in order to do that, you often have to first fire experienced crew
members to get the ones with the necessary skills for the new ship", [review, 9 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198012014187/recommended/621220/));
and only some of the seven ports sell ships. Top ships carry nine men. Each man signs for a **lay**, a share of
what the oil sells for.

**Minute to minute.** Start: three men, one boat, a contract from the tavern, a slow line across a chart of the
Atlantic, a card event, a dice fight with a small whale, home to sell blubber. Middle: two boats, a surgeon, a
cooper, the story's first hard hunts. End: three boats and nine men, the Pacific, the white whale.

**The screen at the end.** A sepia sea chart of the world with rhumb lines and a compass rose. Your ship is an
engraved profile a centimetre long drawing a dotted wake; whaling grounds are hatched areas with a whale stamp;
seven ports are anchors. Top: the date (the 1820s on) and a monthly newspaper. A second screen is the ship cut
open in profile: the hold, the try-works, the quarters, slots for upgrades, and nine crew portraits with level
pips and a lay each. The fight: three columns for three whaleboats, a card for each man, dice rolled across the
top, the whale at the right.

**Praise.**

- "It's addictive when you want to go on just one more hunt so you can upgrade your ship, level your crew, or find that perfect surgeon to complement your hunters." ([review, 18 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198203990337/recommended/621220/))

**Hate.**

- "After you buy your second ship, which has 2 boats, therefore more firepower and a bigger hold you will start to make a big profit and the game starts being too easy ( like many games do)." ([review, 64 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198114595766/recommended/621220/))
- "From a quarter of the game onward money becomes just a number." ([review, not recommended, 27 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198006653285/recommended/621220/))
- "The late game is an absolute slog, you barely get any money for whaling because your crew's lay is so high, and if you even try to whale, there are abilities that some have to literally make an entire boat useless for a a turn." ([review, not recommended, 11 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198048465363/recommended/621220/))
- "It's actually too easy to make money. That wouldn't be a problem if there were more ways to spend it." ([forum, "Mid/Late Game Economy balancing needed"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/621220/discussions/0/1692659135920501483/))
- "End game turned into let me finish the main quest mostly because I am bored." ([review, 14 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198031754728/recommended/621220/))

And one player wrote out the late game he wanted, which is close to the first paper's: "Expanded ship roster,
fleets, expanded crew development, start your own whaling business, ports that upgrade as raw goods and cash go
through them, contracting out whale expeditions for a set price to other captains late game, warehousing, onshore
refinery, whaleboat upgrades." ([forum, "another dlc ?"](https://steamcommunity.com/app/621220/discussions/0/4792394847118557266/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Lost in the middle. The shares rule is true to history and is the best idea in the
game, but it was not balanced for growth: with nine skilled men the captain keeps so little of the oil that players
dump blubber and sell meat, on which no lay is paid. A share system that makes the main trade pointless at size is
worse than none.

## 10. Bounty Train (Corbie Games, 2017)

**One to many: no. The train gets longer.** One engine and the cars she can pull. Growth is a stronger engine (each
a historical type with a pulling weight), more and better cars (freight, passenger, later armoured, gatling,
mortar and hospital cars), a hired crew with skills, and track that costs money to open. Late in the campaign
come auctions for shares in your father's railway company (?) and "owning production sources (coalmines,cotton
plantation etc)". A bank pays interest.

**Minute to minute.** Start: Portland, 1860. A weak engine, two cars, a contract board at the town hall, coal
bought for each leg, a slider for speed, bandits you must pay. Middle: three contracts stacked on one run, a
better engine, armed men on the roofs, the war closing lines. End: a fast engine that crosses a state in a day,
gun cars that end every fight, and waiting for the next auction.

**The screen at the end.** A painted map of the eastern United States in browns and greens, cities as round
badges joined by rail lines, your train a small token on one of them, army camps and bandit marks beside the
track, the date at the top. In a station: a row of building buttons (town hall, market, depot, saloon, bank) over
a painting of the town. The train screen is a side view: the engine at the left, a tender, six or seven cars each
with slots, crew portraits standing on them, a weight bar that turns red when she is overloaded. Fights use the
same side view with the scenery sliding past.

**Praise.**

- "Every part of your train can be replaced or upgraded adding a fantastic amount of customization and allowing you to focus on whatever you feel makes money faster (smuggling goods, transporting passengers, raiding camps)." ([review, 56 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197991140811/recommended/371520/))
- "Some lategame features like auctions, owning production sources (coalmines,cotton plantation etc) and seige/medical carriages were nice suprises" ([review, 137 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198042209381/recommended/371520/))

**Hate.**

- "If you start putting your spare cash into the bank early it will begin to snowball and later in the game most of your money will come from going to the bank every 40 days to renew your investment as opposed to from your main activity." ([review, not recommended, 47 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198007134962/recommended/371520/))
- "I'm just driving around for no other reason but to pass the time till the next few auctions to finish the game." ([forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/371520/discussions/4/3145133271986463668/))
- "well while the game is fun until early mid game after that it becomes an incredible grindfest." ([review, not recommended, 14 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198065002006/recommended/371520/))
- "Towards the end game, the game feels lazy as all you gotta do is fast forward time until randomly the missions to complete end game become available." ([review, not recommended, 21 h](https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198101224639/recommended/371520/))
- An early-access player asked for what was missing: "allowing you to invest the fortunes you earn from running your train into something." ([forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/371520/discussions/4/520519112497357599/))

**First hour kept or lost?** Lost. The first hour is a hard sum of weight, coal, time and bribes. By the end coal
is too cheap to count, guns answer every card, and money that sits in a bank beats money that rides the rails.

## 12. Five lessons for Kapman, and one proposal each

### Lesson 1. Slow is loved until the road repeats. Hand the road over at that moment, not before and not after.

The evidence is the plainest in this paper. The same players who call Sunless Sea's first hours "wonderful" say
"it will start to feel slow once you start sailing the same routes over and over." Its makers refused to speed
the ship up, rightly, and had no other tool. Sunless Skies, Sailwind and Bounty Train repeat the complaint.
Uncharted Waters had the tool in 1993: Auto Sail, earned by a mate's skill, to a known port.

This is the first paper's three-crossings rule, and the evidence says it is the right rule in the right place. It
adds two things: the offer must **come to the player** at the moment the road goes stale, and the man must be one
who **was there**.

**Proposal: the offer in the reckoning line.** At the reckoning of the third end-to-end crossing of a route, one
extra line is written under the voyage's line: *"You know this road now. So does Folkmar."* From then on the
winter screen lists that route as one that can be given. The first skipper of the house is always one of the four
named men who was aboard for all three crossings; nobody can be hired from a tavern for a road he has not sailed
with you. Once a road is given, its cards stop coming to you and come home as his tale. No time warp is needed
or wanted: the player stops sailing the stale road because someone else now does.

### Lesson 2. Ships that follow you are a hold with a drag. A fleet is ships that are somewhere else.

Both fleet games in the sample give a convoy, and both sets of players undo it: "all you need and want is one
Indian War Canoe"; "many players choose to stick with one single ship." The extra hulls add hold and take speed,
and they ask no question. Uncharted Waters' ten ships are ten rows. Not one of the ten games dares the other
kind of fleet, where the second ship is away. Players have asked for it by name: "contracting out whale
expeditions for a set price to other captains late game."

**Proposal: no convoy; one consort for one leg.** A second boat of the house never follows the player as a
standing arrangement. She has her own road and her own skipper, and is known by letter (first paper, 4.2). The
single exception is an order called **In company**, chosen on the course card for one leg only, with one other
boat, where raiders are about: both sail at the slower boat's pace, both eat their stores, and the day stick
shows the cost before it is chosen. In a raider card, two sails count as two. At the far port they part. That is
the whole convoy game, and it is a decision each time it is used.

### Lesson 3. A bigger hull must close doors as well as open them, and must never buy off the fear.

Three games show three ways a bigger unit fails. Sunless Sea: more hold, more fuel, "back at square one", so
the step changed a number and no question. Dredge: every upgrade removed some dread, until "none of the dangers
matter anymore." Bounty Train and Nantucket: the bigger unit wins every fight and "the game starts being too
easy." Sailwind shows the way that works: a brig is not a safer cog, it is eleven lines where there were three.

`ships_roster.md` already gives each rung "what the step opens". The lesson is to print the other column too, and
to keep the boat that can still do what the big ship cannot. Sunless Sea makes you trade the old ship in; that is
the wrong rule for a house.

**Proposal: Opens and Closes on the ship card, and the first boat is never sold.** The card for buying or
building a ship has two short lists side by side. For the 14 m keel: *Opens: six tonnes at sea; Lundenwic as an
ordinary summer voyage. Closes: the creeks; the flats at low water; any beach without a hard.* For the knarr:
*Opens: the open sea, a gale ridden out. Closes: every port without a jetty, unless she tows her boat; and she is
seen from farther off.* The last item is the fear: each rung up adds one step to how soon raiders and king's men
make her out, shown as the same odds word the cards use. The Gislinge is kept on the house's hard for life, the
only hull that goes everywhere, and the player may take her out in any season instead of the big ship.

### Lesson 4. Silver must become people and places you choose. Never a bill, never interest, and never a share that eats the trade.

Seven of ten games end with a pile of money and a sentence like "nothing to spend on". The fixes that failed are
as useful as the gap. Sailwind added wear as "a money sink for endgame" and a 166-hour player turned against the
game over it. Bounty Train added a bank and found that "most of your money will come from going to the bank." 
Nantucket added the period's own share system and let it grow until captains threw the oil overboard. And the
players say what they want: mines, warehouses, a business, other captains, "something" to put a fortune into.

The first paper's list of sinks (4.5) is the right list. This lesson adds three rules to it.

**Proposal: three rules for silver, written into the winter screen.**

1. **Nothing earns while it sits.** No interest, no rent, no income from a plot or a share unless a ship came
   home with it this year. A storehouse holds goods; it does not make pennies.
2. **Every sink is chosen in winter and none is charged at sea.** Upkeep is one line at the winter reckoning
   (already 2.5 per cent a year in the roster), never a meter that drains on a voyage.
3. **A skipper's share is fixed when the partnership is made and does not rise with his skill.** One table,
   shown in the reckoning line of every ship that comes home (D, to be tuned): the house's hull and the house's
   cargo, two thirds to the house and one third to the skipper and his men; a partner's hull with the house's
   cargo, half and half; a share bought in another man's voyage, that share and no say. A better skipper is paid
   in what he asks for at the next winter (a share in the hull, a daughter's dowry, his own boat), which is a card
   and a choice, not a percentage creeping up.

### Lesson 5. The line of heirs is the real "many". Let the heir keep the hulls and lose the certainties.

The two Sunless games show both ends. In the first, the heir keeps one thing of five and repeats the start, and
players resent the repetition. In the second, the heir keeps the engine, the bank and the map, and "none of your
subsequent captains will have to worry about items or money again." Pirates! resets by rule at the share-out and
is forgiven when the player chose the moment and not when he did not. FTL and 80 Days are loved because a run
ends. Kapman has a run that ends (the year) and a house that goes on; the question is what a death takes.

**Proposal: the oath-winter.** When the head of the house dies, the heir keeps every hull, every plot, the chart
and the chest (as decided on 4 October). What he does not keep for nothing is the people. The winter after a death
has one extra page: each skipper, factor and partner comes with a card. One renews his oath as it stood. One asks
for more: a larger share, a boat of his own, a marriage. One says he sailed for the father, not the son, and goes,
and takes his road's goodwill with him. Charters and toll freedoms held from a lord must be asked for again with
a gift. So every generation begins with a smaller, shakier house than the one that ended, and with a reason to
take the small boat out and sail a road three times: the first hour comes back, by people and not by
confiscation.

## 13. What this changes in the first paper

| First paper | After this pass |
|---|---|
| 4.1 The three-crossings rule | Kept, with the offer written into the third reckoning and the first skipper drawn from the men aboard. Strongest support of any rule. |
| 4.2 Letters, not telepathy | Kept. No game in this sample tries it, so there is no proof it is fun; it is the one real risk and should be built and played early with one boat. |
| 4.5 Sinks | Kept, with the three rules of Lesson 4. Drop any thought of a bank or of rent. |
| 4.6 Dangers grow | Kept, and tied to the ship card: the size of the hull is how far off she is seen. |
| 4.7 You always have a boat | Kept and sharpened: it is always the Gislinge that is kept, and she is never sold. |
| 7.3 A cap of eight keels | Pirates! caps at eight and Uncharted Waters at ten; in both, players say it is too many to care about. A lower cap (five or six named skippers) is worth testing (D). |
| Not in the first paper | In company, for one leg. The oath-winter at a succession. Opens and Closes on the ship card. |
