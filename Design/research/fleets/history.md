# How real merchant houses grew, 700 to 1100

**The question:** how did a merchant of the North Sea and Baltic really grow from one boat to many?

**The short answer.** He did not grow into a fleet. He grew into **shares, kin and friends**. Nothing in the sources of 700 to 1100 shows a private northern merchant owning several whole ships. The best recorded career, Godric of Finchale's, runs from a pack on his back to **half of one ship and a quarter of another** after about twenty years. What grew was not the count of hulls but the web around the man: partners who laid goods together, kinsmen at the steering oar, a host in each port who answered for him, a plot among his countrymen abroad, sworn brothers who would bury him and pay for his losses, and a lord's letter that got him past the toll. Fleets belonged to kings, to abbeys and to princes' retinues. A merchant who wanted more than his own hold could carry bought a part of someone else's risk.

That is good news for the game. The period's own way of growing is already "people, not units" and "letters, not telepathy". It only asks that the late game count **shares and men**, not keels.

---

## 1. What the evidence is, and what it is not

| Kind | What it gives | Its limit |
|---|---|---|
| Rune stones (c. 970 to 1100) | The words *félagi* (partner), *gildi* (guild-brother), *styrimaðr* (steersman), a ship owned jointly, a cargo ship lost | A dozen words each. Denmark and Sweden only, and only the last hundred years of the period. |
| Laws and treaties | Hosts, witnesses, wreck, debt, the merchant's rank; the Rus treaties of 907, 911 and 944 | Rules say what should happen, not what did. |
| Royal and church writing | Louis the Pious's merchants (828), toll charters, the bans on sworn guilds (779 on), the Frisians at Mainz and Worms | Written by clerks about people they taxed or distrusted. |
| Saints' lives | Anskar's merchants and pirates, Liudger's Frisians at York, Godric | A merchant appears only when a saint needs him. |
| A hostile monk | Alpert of Metz on the men of Tiel, c. 1021 to 1024 | He dislikes them; that is why he is useful. |
| Travellers | Ohthere and Wulfstan at Alfred's court (c. 890); Ibn Fadlan on the Volga (922); Constantine VII on the Rus convoys (c. 950) | Outsiders, one visit each. |
| The ground | Ships (Klåstad, Äskekärr, Hedeby 3, Skuldelev 1 and 3), plots, scales and weights, seals, rune sticks, birch bark | No names, no owners. |

**There is not one account book, contract or merchant's letter from the North Sea before 1100.** Everything about how a house was run is read sideways from these. Where this report says "probably", that is why.

## 2. The rungs, as the sources show them

### 2.1 One man, his own goods, somebody's boat

The first rung is not a boat at all.

- **Godric** (born c. 1065 to 1070 in Norfolk, died 21 May 1170) "lived as a chapman for four years in Lincolnshire, going on foot and carrying the smallest wares". Only then did he go to sea, and at first as one of a company: "having formed a familiar friendship with certain other young men who were eager for merchandise, he began to launch upon bolder courses, and to coast frequently by sea". (Reginald of Durham, *Life of St Godric*, tr. G. G. Coulton, *Social Life in Britain from the Conquest to the Reformation*, Cambridge 1918, pp. 415-420; public domain by the brief's rule.)
- **The Frisian of London, 679.** Bede's captive thegn Imma is sold in London to a Frisian, who cannot keep him bound and lets him ransom himself (Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* IV.22). One man, one deal, a foreign town. This is the oldest named kind of North Sea trader: a Frisian dealing in people.
- **The Frisian on the Rhine, c. 839.** In the miracles of St Goar (Wandalbert of Prüm) a Frisian merchant comes upriver with his boat hauled on a rope by his men from the bank (?). A boat on the Rhine upstream is towed, not sailed.

A man could trade for years as a passenger with a bale, paying freight in a share of the goods (the saga word for a passenger's place is *far*; saga evidence, 13th century) (?).

### 2.2 The partnership: goods laid together

Old Norse *félag* is *fé* (cattle, wealth) plus the root of "lay": property laid together. A *félagi* is the root of English "fellow". (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9lag)

About twenty-two rune stones name a *félagi*. The ones that matter here:

| Stone | Where, when | What it says (paraphrase) | What it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| DR 68 | Aarhus, c. 970 to 1020 | Three men raised it for Assur Saxe, their partner, a very good man; "he owned a ship with Arne". | A ship owned jointly by two, and a partnership of four that is wider than the ship. |
| DR 1 (the Stone of Eric) | Hedeby, c. 982 to 995 | Thorulf, King Svein's man, raised it for Erik his partner, who died when men besieged Hedeby; "he was a steersman, a very good man". | Partner and steersman are the same person. Also: the same men fought. |
| DR 66 | Aarhus, same years | Four men for Ful, their partner, "who died when kings fought". | *Félag* is also the word for a war-band's fellowship. |
| The Berezan stone | The island at the mouth of the Dnieper, 11th century | "Grani made this vault for Karl, his partner." | Two partners (thought to be Gotlanders) on the road to Constantinople; one buried the other. |
| U 391 | Sigtuna, 11th century | The Frisian guild-brothers had the runes cut for Albod, "Slode's partner" (?). | A man could be in a guild and in a two-man partnership at once. |

Three things to hold on to:

1. **A *félag* is small.** Two to four names. It is a venture between men who know each other, not a company.
2. **It is by the voyage or the ship**, not for life. The later Norwegian town law (the Bjarkey law, written down in the 12th and 13th centuries) treats it as goods put in, shares of gain and loss by what each put in, and an end when the venture is reckoned (?).
3. **The word covers trade and war alike.** The same crew that sold whetstones could stand in a shield wall. The stones do not let us sort merchants from warriors, and the period did not try.

The stones are all late (after c. 970) and all Danish, Swedish or Gotlandic. For a Frisian of 793 there is no word and no text. That Frisians shared ventures is likely (they sailed in companies: section 2.6) but unproven.

### 2.3 Shares in a ship

- DR 68: a ship owned by two.
- Godric: "he purchased the half of a merchant-ship with certain of his partners in the trade; and again by his prudence he bought the fourth part of another ship." Note the wording: the **half** itself was bought "with partners". He held a part of a half, then a quarter of another.
- The sagas of Icelanders, written 150 to 300 years later, are full of men who "owned half the ship" with a Norwegian (?). Later still, northern law divides ships into halves, quarters and eighths (?).

A share spreads the worst loss. One hull lost takes a quarter of a man's stake, not all of it. This is the period's only insurance apart from the guild (section 2.8).

### 2.4 The steersman

*Styrimaðr* is the man who commands and usually owns, or part-owns, the ship (DR 1). Godric "by his skill in navigation, wherein he excelled all his fellows, earned promotion to the post of steersman": among partners the steering oar went to the best seaman, and he was **chosen by the others**. There is no sign anywhere in the period of a hired captain who is a stranger to the owners. The man in command is an owner, a partner, or kin.

### 2.5 Kin

The sources say less than one expects, and it is all indirect:

- Rune stones for traders are raised by widows, sons and brothers: Sigrid for Svein, who "often sailed a valued cargo ship to Semgallen, round Domesnäs" (Sö 198, Mervalla); Ingeborg for her husband, who "drowned in Holm's sea; his cargo ship went to the bottom; only three came out alive" (U 214, Vallentuna, c. 1100). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_area_runestones)
- Rimbert's rich widow **Frideburg** of Birka (c. 850) keeps her wealth herself and tells her daughter **Catla** to carry it to Dorestad after her death and give it to the churches and the poor there; Catla makes the voyage and does it (*Life of Anskar* ch. 20, tr. Robinson 1921) (?). A Birka woman's fortune goes to Dorestad by her daughter's hand: kin as the trusted carrier, and a Swedish house that knows where Dorestad's holy places are.
- A dead partner's goods: the Rus treaty of 911 says the goods of a Rus who dies in Greek service with no will go to his nearest kin in Rus, carried home by Rus who trade there (*Primary Chronicle*, tr. S. H. Cross 1930) (?).

So: the wife or widow holds the house's wealth at home, sons and brothers sail, and countrymen carry a dead man's goods to his heirs. That is the family firm of the age.

### 2.6 The host, and sailing in company

A stranger in a port had no kin there and therefore no law. Somebody had to answer for him.

- **Kent, c. 680.** If a man shelters a stranger for three nights in his own home, "a trader or any other man who has come over the border", and feeds him, and the stranger then harms someone, the host must bring him to justice or pay (Laws of Hlothhere and Eadric; the clause numbering differs by edition) (?). The same laws: a Kentishman buying in London needs two or three honest freemen or the king's wic-reeve as witness. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Hlothhere_and_Eadric)
- **Wessex, c. 890.** Merchants must bring the men they mean to take up country before the king's reeve at the folk-moot and say how many they are; they answer for them (Alfred 34) (?). Ine 25 (c. 690): a merchant trading up country does it before witnesses (?).
- **England, 10th century.** No buying over 20 pence outside a town, and every sale before the port-reeve or other trusted men (I Edward 1; II Athelstan 12) (?).
- **Birka, c. 830 to 850.** The king's prefect Hergeir is the man Anskar deals with; the town has an assembly and a prefect, and foreign merchants are in the town when it is attacked (*Life of Anskar* ch. 11, 19) (?).
- **Constantinople, 907 and 944.** The Rus are lodged in one quarter (St Mamas), their names written down by imperial officers, let in by one gate, unarmed, fifty at a time, with an imperial guard, and fed by the month for six months. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rus%27%E2%80%93Byzantine_Treaty_(907))

The host is where a "factor" comes from. But in this period he is **a local man who vouches for you and stores your goods**, or a countryman settled there. A salaried agent who buys to written standing orders is a thing of the 13th century.

Merchants also moved in company. Anskar sailed for Birka in 829 or 830 with merchants; pirates took the ships and goods, and the missionaries lost nearly forty books (ch. 10, tr. Robinson). Constantine VII describes the Rus leaving Kiev each June in a convoy of dugouts, hauling them past the Dnieper rapids with their slaves in chains and a guard posted against the Pechenegs (*De administrando imperio* ch. 9, c. 950) (?). A Trier miracle story has the Frisian Ibbo joining a fleet of six ships for a voyage overseas (?). **Several boats sailing together were several owners, not one man's fleet.**

### 2.7 Countrymen abroad: the quarters

| Place | When | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| London | 679 | A Frisian buys a slave (Bede IV.22). |
| Saint-Denis fair | 753 | Pippin's charter names Saxons and Frisians among those who come (?). |
| York | c. 773 | A Frisian merchant kills a count's son; **all** the Frisians leave York for fear of the kin's revenge, and Liudger goes home with them (Altfrid, *Life of Liudger* I.11-12) (?). A colony, and collective blame. |
| Strasbourg and the upper Rhine | c. 826 | Ermoldus Nigellus has the Rhine boast that Frisians carry Alsace's wine and corn away and bring bright cloaks (?). |
| Birka | c. 850 | Frideburg and Catla; people there know Dorestad (*Life of Anskar* ch. 20, 27) (?). |
| Hedeby | c. 850 | When a church is allowed at Sliaswich, merchants "both from here and from Dorestad" come freely (ch. 24) (?). |
| Mainz | 886 | A fire burns "the best part of the city, where the Frisians lived" (*Annals of Fulda*) (?). |
| Duisburg | 893 | Frisians pay dues there (the Prüm polyptych) (?). |
| Worms | c. 900 | The wall-building order gives the Frisians their own stretch of wall to keep, from "the Frisians' point" to the Rhine (?). |
| Sigtuna | 11th century | Two stones raised by "the Frisian guild-brothers" (U 379, U 391). |

**A caution on Sigtuna.** The stones are in Old Norse and the names (Thorkel, Thorbjorn, Slode) are Scandinavian, Albod perhaps excepted. The guild was probably Swedes who traded to Frisia, or "Frisian" had come to mean "merchant". It is not good evidence for Frisians living in Sigtuna. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrkog%C3%A5rden_Runestones)

What a quarter was: a row of plots by the water where one people lived together, with a duty to the town (a length of wall at Worms) and no walls of its own. The ground agrees. Ribe was laid out in narrow plots soon after 700; Dorestad's harbour was long strips running to the river, each with its own causeway; York's Coppergate tenements were set out c. 930 to 935 at about 5.5 m wide and kept their lines for a thousand years; Sigtuna's were laid out c. 980 (?). **A plot was what a merchant owned abroad.** There was no shared trading hall and no factory in the Hanse's sense.

### 2.8 Sworn brothers: the guilds

The thing is older than the name's good repute, and for two hundred years the law is against it.

- **779, Herstal.** Charlemagne forbids men to bind themselves by oath in guilds (*gildonia*). They may still agree to help each other "in alms, in fire and in shipwreck", so long as no one swears (capitulary of Herstal, c. 16) (?). **Mutual aid against shipwreck among Frankish subjects, fourteen years before the game begins**, and a ban on the oath that made it hold.
- **852.** Hincmar of Reims rules on what priests may do at the meetings "which they call guilds or brotherhoods": candles, alms, burials yes; feasting and quarrels no (?).
- **c. 930 to 940, London.** The peace-guild of the bishops and reeves (VI Athelstan): a common purse of fourpence a member, payment for stolen cattle from it, the hunting of thieves together, and a monthly meeting with a meal (?).
- **c. 1000, Cambridge, the thegns' guild.** The rules survive in a gospel book: an oath on relics of true loyalty; the guild fetches a sick or dead brother home and buries him where he wished; if a brother is killed the guild takes eight pounds for him and backs the feud; if a brother kills in need, all help pay; an insult costs a measure of honey (tr. B. Thorpe, *Diplomatarium Anglicum*, 1865, pp. 610-613) (?). These are landed men, not merchants. Sister rules survive from Exeter (each brother pays a penny when another's house burns, fivepence when one goes on pilgrimage), Bedwyn and Abbotsbury (?).
- **c. 1000 to 1100, Sweden.** Four stones name guild-brothers: U 379 and U 391 at Sigtuna, and two in Östergötland, one with a ship cut on it (Bjälbo, Törnevalla) (?).
- **c. 1021 to 1024, Tiel.** Alpert of Metz (*De diversitate temporum* II.20): the merchants of Tiel are hard men who judge by their own will and not by law, and say the emperor gave them this right by charter; a man may clear himself of a debt by his oath alone; they begin drinking at dawn; and they put money together in a common fund, lay it out for gain, and from the gain hold feasts at set times of the year (?). In 1018 these same men had complained to Henry II that Count Dirk III was taking a heavy toll from ships on the Merwede and ruining the trade with England; the emperor's army went and was destroyed at Vlaardingen on 29 July 1018. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vlaardingen ; https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiel)
- **c. 1050 to 1070, Valenciennes; before 1127, Saint-Omer.** The first merchant-guild rules that survive. At Saint-Omer: a merchant outside the guild who is robbed on the road gets no help; a brother may claim a part in another brother's bargain at the price paid, if he is present; no weapons in the guild hall; the leftover of the drinking goes to the streets, the gates and the lepers (?).

What a guild gave: a burial, an avenger, a witness, an oath-helper in a debt case, a share in a good bargain, a common purse, and, at Tiel, a voice the emperor heard. What it did not give: ships. **A guild owned no fleet and ran no trade of its own.**

### 2.9 A lord's letter

- **828, the merchants of the palace.** Louis the Pious's model letter (*Praeceptum negotiatorum*, *Formulae imperiales* 37): named merchants serve the palace faithfully; they come to the palace in the middle of May, every year or every second year, to answer to the chamber; they are free of army service and their ships may not be taken for the king's use; they may add to their ships; they pay no toll anywhere **except at Quentovic, Dorestad and the Alpine passes, where the tenth is taken**; a case that cannot be settled where it arises waits for the emperor or for the master he has set over the merchants (?).
- **Abbeys.** From 753 on, kings grant abbeys freedom from toll for a stated number of ships: commonly two to six, on a named river (?). The abbey's men who sail them are *negotiatores* of the saint. This is the commonest way a Frankish trader of 793 sailed toll-free: **as an abbey's man, for a share**.
- **796.** Charlemagne writes to Offa: merchants shall have protection in his realm "by our command", and if they are wronged they may appeal to him or his judges; and the same for his own in Mercia (letter in Alcuin's collection) (?).
- **944.** Each Rus ship is to bear a charter of the prince of Kiev; twenty-six merchants are named in the treaty beside the envoys. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rus%27%E2%80%93Byzantine_Treaty_(945)) In the text as Cross translates it, as I recall: until then envoys had carried gold seals and merchants silver ones; now the prince sends a certificate saying how many ships he has dispatched, and Rus who come without it are held; a Rus may buy silk only to the worth of fifty gold pieces, and an officer stamps it (?).
- **c. 1000, London.** Æthelred's toll list (IV Æthelred): the men of Rouen, Flanders, Ponthieu, Normandy and France, of Huy, Liège and Nivelles each have their terms; "the men of the Emperor who came in their ships" are worthy of good laws like the English and pay at Christmas and Easter in cloth, pepper, gloves and vinegar (?). Tiel's men are among these.

The pattern: **the right to trade abroad is held by a group under a lord and proved by a thing you carry** (a letter, a seal, a charter). A lone man with no lord pays everything and has no judge.

### 2.10 Rank

The English text on status, *Geþyncðo*, is the work of Archbishop Wulfstan of York, 1002 to 1023 (Wormald: 1008 to 1014). The clause that a merchant who crossed the wide sea three times at his own cost was worthy of a thegn's rights is **only in the southern copy in the *Textus Roffensis***, not in the text as first written. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge%C3%BEync%C3%B0o) Thorpe's 1840 translation reads, as far as I recall it: "And if a merchant thrived, so that he fared thrice over the wide sea by his own means, then was he thenceforth of thane-right worthy" (check the wording against the book before it is quoted) (?).

"By his own means" is the point. Three crossings as another man's partner did not count. It rewards exactly the thing that was rare: owning the whole venture.

## 3. Two lives

### 3.1 Ohthere, c. 890: a rich man who is not a house

Ohthere of Hålogaland told King Alfred that he lived furthest north of all Northmen. He had 600 tame reindeer unsold, six of them decoys; but only twenty cattle, twenty sheep and twenty pigs, and ploughed a little with horses. His wealth was the **tribute of the Finnas**: the highest-born paid fifteen marten skins, five reindeer skins, a bear skin, ten measures of feathers, a coat of bear or otter skin, and two ship's ropes sixty ells long of whale or seal hide. He sailed north round the cape to see how far the land went and "for the walrus", brought Alfred walrus teeth, and sailed south a month along the coast to Sciringes heal and five days more to Hedeby. (Old English Orosius; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohthere_of_H%C3%A5logaland ; public-domain translations: Bosworth 1855, Thorpe 1853.)

What he shows: the richest kind of trader in the north was **a chieftain selling his own tribute in his own ship**. One ship, one voyage south a year, the goods his by right and not by purchase. No partners are mentioned, no agents, no second vessel.

### 3.2 Godric, c. 1085 to 1105: the whole ladder in one man

| Step | Reginald's words (Coulton 1918) | Years |
|---|---|---|
| Pedlar | "a chapman for four years in Lincolnshire, going on foot and carrying the smallest wares" | 4 |
| Markets | Then "he gradually associated himself by compact with city merchants" | |
| A company at sea | "a familiar friendship with certain other young men who were eager for merchandise ... to coast frequently by sea" | |
| The routes | Between England, Scotland, Denmark and Flanders (and pilgrimages to Rome, St Andrews, Jerusalem) (?) | |
| A share | "the half of a merchant-ship with certain of his partners" | |
| A second share | "the fourth part of another ship" | |
| Steersman | "by his skill in navigation, wherein he excelled all his fellows" | |
| The end | "he had lived sixteen years as a merchant, and began to think of spending on charity" | 16 as a merchant |

He then gave it all away and lived about sixty years as a hermit at Finchale. He is just outside the game's years, but nothing in his rise needs anything that did not exist in 1000. **Twenty years of skill and luck bought less than three quarters of one ship's worth, spread over two hulls.** That is the measured scale of success.

(A "Guderic, a pirate from England" carried King Baldwin I to Jaffa in 1102; that this was the same man is a guess, not a fact.)

## 4. How word travelled

**At the speed of a ship, and mostly in a man's mouth.**

| Means | Evidence in the period | Use in the game |
|---|---|---|
| **A spoken message by a known man** | The normal way everywhere. A message is as good as its bearer. | The default. A skipper or a guild-brother "has word for you". |
| **A token** | A thing sent with the messenger to prove who sent him: a ring, a knife, a marked stick. Norse *jartegn*; the sagas use it constantly, but they are 13th century (?). In England a seal (*insegl*) proves a lord's word from the 10th century; the ivory seal of "Godwine the thegn", c. 1040, survives (?). | A token is an item. Lose it and your word is not believed. |
| **A lord's letter under seal** | 796, 828, 944 (section 2.9). Lead seals of a Byzantine official named Theodosios, c. 840, have been found at Hedeby, Ribe and Tissø: sealed things did travel to the northern markets (?). | A charter in the chest. |
| **A clerk's letter** | Latin letters on parchment between churchmen all through the period (Alcuin, Lupus of Ferrières), carried by whoever was going. A merchant could **carry** one; to **send** one he needed a priest. No merchant's letter survives. | A scribe is a thing the church gives (the scale document already has this). |
| **Rune sticks** | Writing on wood is old: a stick with verse from Staraya Ladoga, c. 800; rune sticks from Hedeby, 9th century, one perhaps a message (?). But the business letters and owner's tags of Bergen's wharf (about 670 inscriptions) are later than c. 1170 (?); the letter of Thorir the Fair to his partner Hafgrim asking for help is early 14th century. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryggen_inscriptions ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9lag) | A Northman's short message on a stick is fair for the whole period. Long business letters on sticks are not proven before 1100: keep them to a line. |
| **Owner's tags** | A slip of wood tied to a bale, cut "X owns me" (Bergen, Trondheim, 12th century on) (?). In Novgorod from the 970s, wooden cylinder locks closed the sacks of tribute and were cut with the collector's sign and the owner's (?). | Marked bales from c. 1000 in the north-east; before that, a knot and a witness. |
| **Birch bark** | Novgorod, 11th to 15th century; 1,113 found there by 2018, many about debts and trade. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch_bark_manuscript) One of the oldest (no. 246, c. 1025 to 1050), as I recall it: Zhirovit tells Stoyan that it is the ninth year since Stoyan swore on the cross and still has not sent the four and a half grivnas, and that if he does not, Zhirovit will seize the goods of a leading Novgorodian on Stoyan's account (?). | The Rus river end of the map, after c. 1030. A dunning letter is the oldest merchant's letter there is. |
| **Tally sticks** | A notched stick, sometimes split so each party keeps half, is the common debt record of unlettered Europe; split tallies are certain in England by the 12th century and notched sticks are found in the northern towns (?). No dated example can be named here for 793. | Debts as tallies in the chest: fair, but mark it as likely, not proven, before 1000. |
| **Scales and weights** | Folding scales and standard weights spread across the north from c. 870 (?). More than a hundred scales from Birka. The Sigtuna box (early 11th century) held a scale and says "Djarf got these scales from a man of Samland (or Semgallen)". Silver is nicked and pecked to test it. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigtuna_box) | Already in the game (weigh_silver, test_silver). Trust between strangers is the scale, not the letter. |

**How fast.** Ohthere: a month from Hålogaland to Sciringes heal, sleeping ashore, and five days on to Hedeby. Wulfstan: seven days and nights from Hedeby to Truso under sail. Adam of Bremen (c. 1075): Ribe to the Flemish coast two days and nights; Birka to Russia five days; Wolin to Novgorod fourteen (?). Add the wait for a ship going that way, and no ships at all from Martinmas to Easter. **A question sent from Dorestad to Birka in June was answered in September, or next year.** Orders therefore could not be managed: they were given once, to a man trusted to change them.

## 5. Credit and debt

- **The stake is usually a lord's.** The palace's merchants, the abbey's men, Ohthere's tribute, the Rus prince's ships. A man starting with nothing started with someone else's goods. The game's 480 pennies owed to the count is true to this.
- **Lending at interest is forbidden to Christians** in Frankish law (the General Admonition of 789; the Nijmegen capitulary of 806, which defines usury as taking back more than was given) (?). So gain on a loan is dressed as a share: "I put in goods, you sail, we part the gain". That is a *félag* with a sleeping partner. In the south the same thing is written down as the Venetian *colleganza* (first surviving contract 1073: the stay-at-home puts in two thirds, the traveller one third and his labour, gain halved) (?); the north has the practice without the parchment.
- **Common funds.** Tiel's merchants pool money and lay it out for gain (c. 1021). London's peace-guild pays losses from a purse (c. 935).
- **Proof is men, not paper.** Witnesses at the sale (the English laws); oath-helpers at the claim; the debtor's own oath clears him at Tiel, which Alpert thought a scandal. A debt was only as good as the creditor's standing and friends.
- **Countrymen pay for each other.** Zhirovit threatens to take a third man's goods for Stoyan's debt. The Frisians of York all flee for one man's killing. When Offa and Charlemagne fell out, c. 790, each closed his ports to all the other's merchants (*Deeds of the Abbots of Fontenelle*; Alcuin's letters) (?). **Your name abroad was your people's name, and theirs was yours.**
- **Silver by weight** in the north until local coin (Hedeby c. 825, then widely from c. 995); pennies in Francia and England. A debt in the north is in marks and ores of weighed silver.
- **What is not there:** bills of exchange, banks, insurance contracts, sea loans, written partnership deeds, account books. All are Mediterranean or later.

## 6. What could go wrong

| What | A dated case |
|---|---|
| **Wreck** | U 214: the cargo ship went down in "Holm's sea", three men lived (c. 1100). Sö 198 praises a man for often rounding Domesnäs, where the reef is. |
| **Wreck, then the shore** | Wrecked goods fell to whoever held the shore (?). The treaties try to stop it: the Rus and Greeks promise in 911 to help each other's stranded ships and bring the cargo on (?); Æthelred's peace with Olaf's army (994) gives a merchant ship peace in any river mouth, and her men peace if they are driven ashore and reach a town (II Æthelred 2-3) (?). You needed a treaty to be safe from rescue. |
| **Raid at sea** | Anskar's merchants, 829 or 830: ships and goods lost, the men put ashore to walk (*Life of Anskar* 10). |
| **Raid on the port** | Dorestad 834, 835, 836, 837, 846, 847, 857, 863 (?). In 863 the Danes also took a place where Frisian merchants had fled and killed many (*Annals of St Bertin*) (?). Mainz's Frisian quarter burned 886 (?). Hedeby burned c. 1050 and 1066 (?). |
| **A king moves the market** | 808: King Godfred destroys Reric and carries its merchants to Hedeby (*Royal Frankish Annals*). |
| **A king shuts the sea** | c. 790: the Channel ports closed between Francia and Mercia (?). |
| **A king's ban** | 805, Thionville: no arms or mail to the Slavs, trade only at named frontier posts under named officers; a man caught loses **all** his goods, half to the palace (?). |
| **A new toll** | 1018: Dirk III on the Merwede. The emperor sided with the merchants and lost the battle; Tiel lost its protector and declined. |
| **A faithless partner** | No dated northern case before 1100 can be named. The remedies show the fear: witnesses, oaths, the guild's judgment, Saint-Omer's rule that a brother may claim his part in a bargain. The sagas are full of quarrels over the reckoning (?). |
| **A killing** | York c. 773: one man's blow emptied a colony. |
| **A debt called from the wrong man** | Novgorod no. 246. |
| **Dying abroad** | The Berezan vault; the 911 clause on a dead man's goods; the guild's first duty everywhere is burial. |
| **A change of heart** | Godric gave it all away. So, in a way, did Frideburg. |

Notice what is missing: **the dishonest hired skipper**. The period did not have him, because it did not hire strangers.

And the hulls themselves grew: Äskekärr (c. 930, about 16 m, perhaps 20 tons) (?); Klåstad (c. 990, about 21 m, perhaps 13 tons, a cargo of whetstones) (?); **Hedeby 3 (c. 1025, about 22 m, about 60 tons)** (?); Skuldelev 1 (c. 1030, built in Sogn, 15.8 m, 6 to 8 men, about 24 tons (?)); Skuldelev 3 (c. 1040, 14 m, 5 to 8 men, 4 to 5 tons). (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skuldelev_ships) A sixty-ton ship in 1025 carried what a dozen or more Skuldelev 3s did. **The period's way to get bigger was one larger hull with more owners, not more hulls.** Nobody knows who owned Hedeby 3; a ship that size is a magnate's or a king's, or many men's.

Other measures of a great merchant: Ibn Fadlan's Rus on the Volga (922) give a wife a neck-ring for every ten thousand dirhams the man is worth, and some women wear several (?); Gotland's Spillings hoard (buried after 870) holds 67 kg of silver (?); Frideburg's alms were worth a voyage to Dorestad.

**An honest ceiling for a private house, 793 to 1066:** shares that add up to two or three hulls, spread over four to six; two or three plots abroad; a dozen men who are kin, partners or sworn; a seat in a guild after 1000; a lord's letter. Anything beyond that is an abbey, a bishop, an earl or a king. The scale document's "a great house had a handful" is right only if a handful means **shares in** a handful. Its proposed cap of eight keels is more than any known merchant owned outright.

## 9. What the game should and should not let a player do

### Should

1. **Grow by shares, not hulls.** Let the player buy a half, a quarter, later an eighth of a ship, and sell them. Count the house's size in shares. A wholly owned second ship should be rare, dear and late; a third is a magnate's mark. Recommended ceiling: shares in up to six or eight hulls, never worth more than about three whole ones. (Changes decision 3 of the scale document.)
2. **Start below the boat, or beside it.** The player's first venture with another man should be a bale in his hold or a bale of his in yours, long before a second keel. Godric's ladder (pedlar, company, part-share, quarter, steersman) is the model for the first ten hours.
3. **Make every partnership a venture with an end.** Goods laid together for one voyage or one season, reckoned on the quay before witnesses, then renewed or not. The reckoning is a card. It is where trust is won and where quarrels start.
4. **Let the partners choose the steersman.** Skill at the oar earns command, as it did for Godric. A kinsman may have the right and not the skill: that is the choice the scale document already wants.
5. **Only kin, partners and sworn men command.** No hiring a stranger as skipper. A man becomes sendable by sailing with you, by marrying in, by fostering, or by oath.
6. **A host before a factor.** In each foreign port the player first needs a man who answers for him: three nights under his roof and the host is liable (Kent, c. 680). He stores goods, stands witness and passes word. Only late, and only among countrymen in a quarter, does he buy for you; and then on a few spoken words, not a list.
7. **A plot among your own.** Storehouses are narrow waterside plots in the Frisian row at York, Mainz, Worms, Hedeby, Birka. The quarter carries a duty (a stretch of wall, a share of the watch) and a shared name: when one Frisian kills a count's son, every Frisian pays or leaves.
8. **A lord's letter as the key that scales.** Sail as an abbey's man or a palace merchant: toll-free everywhere but Dorestad, Quentovic and the passes (a tenth there), ships safe from the king's requisition, a judge to appeal to; in return, a share, and an account given at the palace in mid-May. This fits the count's debt and gives the "house" stage its real engine. It can be lost with the lord.
9. **The sworn fellowship, in its right years.** Before c. 1000: countrymen who drink and help each other in fire and wreck, with a card where the count's man reminds them that the oath is forbidden (779). From c. 1000: the guild by name at Tiel and Sigtuna, with what it truly gave: burial, an avenger, oath-helpers in a debt case, a part in a brother's bargain, a common purse laid out for gain, and a complaint the emperor might hear (and lose a battle over, 1018).
10. **Word at ship speed, in a man's mouth.** The scale document's rule stands and is the truest thing in it. Sharpen it: most word is spoken and is worth what its bearer is worth; a token proves it; a written letter needs a clerk (west) or is one line on a stick (north); long dunning letters on bark belong to Novgorod after c. 1030.
11. **Orders given once, to a man free to break them.** With a season between question and answer, a skipper must be trusted to change the plan. The four-line order of the scale document is right; make "he did otherwise, and here is why" a normal homecoming, not a fault.
12. **Debts as tallies and oaths.** A debt is a notched stick, a witness and a day. It can be denied on oath where the guild's law runs. It can be collected from your countrymen if you do not pay, and yours from theirs.
13. **Loss by the period's own list:** wreck and the shore-lord's claim on the wreck; raid on the convoy; raid on the port where the plot is (834 and after); a king's ban, toll, or moved market; an embargo for another man's quarrel; a partner's death abroad and the carrying home of his goods to his heirs.
14. **One bigger hull as the late prize.** From c. 1000 a 24-ton ship, from c. 1025 a 60-ton one, owned by several. Building one with partners is the period's "fleet".
15. **Sail in company.** Let the player join or call a sailing of several owners' boats for a dangerous stretch. Safety in numbers, and prices fall when six boats land the same wine.
16. **Rank as an end.** Three crossings of the wide sea **at your own cost** (not on a partner's stake) for a thegn's right in England, from c. 1010. A widow who holds the house and sends her last wealth to the churches of Dorestad is as true an ending as a rich heir.
17. **Women hold the house.** The wife or widow keeps the silver and the keys at home, raises the stone, and can be the head of the house between heirs (Frideburg, Sigrid, Ingeborg).

### Should not

1. **No fleet of wholly owned ships** on standing routes. It is the one thing the period certainly lacked.
2. **No hired captains, no wages for skippers.** Shares and kinship only.
3. **No salaried factor with written standing orders** before the very end, and never outside your own people's quarter.
4. **No ledgers, contracts, bills, insurance, banks or interest named as interest.** Gain on lent silver is a share in a venture. Insurance is the guild's purse and the split hull.
5. **No guild by that name before c. 1000,** and never a guild that owns ships or trades as one body. (BRIEF rule 2 already says the first.)
6. **No company name, flag or merchant's mark** as a thing in the world. The house colour on the chart is the player's tool, like the chart itself. In the world a house is "Radbod's sons" or "the men of Tiel".
7. **No live sight of your ships, and no answer faster than a ship.** No orders changed in mid-voyage.
8. **No trading as a lone man where a lord's letter is the rule,** without paying for it: the tenth at Dorestad and Quentovic stays even for the palace's own merchants.
9. **No Frisian quarter at Sigtuna or Birka as a fact.** At Birka, Dorestad is known and visited; at Sigtuna the "Frisian guild" is most likely Swedes. A Frisian there is a guest with a host.
10. **No monopoly, no buying a town, no private war.** A merchant's power is a complaint to a lord, and 1018 shows what that was worth.
11. **No rune-stick business correspondence as a system** before 1100. One cut line, a tag, a token.
12. **No safety in size.** The storehouse is what the raid comes for; the name is what a creditor seizes on.

### One thing for the user to decide

**Slaves.** The oldest named Frisian trader sells a man (679); the Rus convoys march theirs past the rapids in chains; the Radhanites carry "eunuchs, female slaves, boys" among their goods (Ibn Khordadbeh, c. 870; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radhanite). A history of how houses grew cannot leave it out. Whether the player may deal in them, only meets it, or works against it (ransom is already in the scale document) is a design choice, not a research one.

### Where this changes the scale document

| There | The evidence says |
|---|---|
| "A host or factor ... who writes or sends word" | A host who vouches, stores and speaks. Writing needs a clerk or is one line; buying to orders is late. |
| "A great house had a handful. I would cap at eight." | Shares in a handful. No known merchant owned two whole ships. Cap by shares. |
| Three crossings made a merchant a thegn | True from c. 1010, in one copy only, and only "by his own means". |
| "The Frisian guild-brothers" at Sigtuna | Probably Swedes in the Frisia trade. |
| Stage 3, "a senior partner with two or three boats" | A senior partner with parts of two or three boats, each steered by a part-owner. |
| The winter council | Keep it. It is the guild's set feast and the reckoning between partners in one evening; the palace's merchants had theirs in mid-May. |
