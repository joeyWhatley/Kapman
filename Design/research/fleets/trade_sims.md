# Trade sims: how they grow from one ship to many

**The question (4 Oct):** "research a way for the game to scale from slow and methodical at the start to managing fleets
of traders, and how that end game will look from a gameplay look standpoint."

This paper looks at ten games that all make the same journey: Patrician II and III, Patrician IV, Port Royale 2, 3 and 4,
Rise of Venice, Merchant Prince (and its remake, Machiavelli the Prince), East India Company and Winds of Trade.

**What they have in common, in five lines.**

1. All start with one ship (or one convoy) and one house in one town, and all are good in that hour.
2. All grow by the same three steps: a hired captain, an automatic route, a house in a second town.
3. All end on the same screen: a map at the highest speed, with lists laid over it.
4. The moment players love most is **setting up** a route. The thing they hate most is what comes after: either the route runs for ever and the game "plays itself", or it jams quietly and has to be nursed.
5. The ones remembered kindly after twenty or thirty years (Patrician III, Merchant Prince) are the ones where money turns into something other people grant: an office, a vote, a town that prospers.

| Game | Year | Start | The gate to ship two | The gate to a fleet | The end goal | Players, in short |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Patrician II / III | 2000 / 2003 | 1 or 2 small ships, an office, some gold | Gold | A **captain** found in a tavern, one per route or convoy | Lord Mayor, then Alderman of the Hanse | Loved. 81% of 896 on Steam |
| Patrician IV | 2010 | 1 ship, a counting house | Gold | Captain, plus a rank ladder that counts your ships | Alderman | Split. 59% of 585 |
| Port Royale 2 | 2004 | 1 small ship, a warehouse, a purse | Gold | **Rank** by capital: each rank allows more captains and more building permits | Your own towns, granted by a viceroy | Liked. 71% of 662 |
| Port Royale 3 | 2012 | 1 convoy | Gold | **Level** by net worth lifts the limits on fleets and permits | Own the Caribbean | 72% of 1,206, but "AFK Simulator" |
| Port Royale 4 | 2020 | A governor with a small town and a convoy | Gold | **Fame** points buy concessions; licences town by town | Supremacy for your nation | Split. 56% of 1,409 |
| Rise of Venice | 2013 | 1 convoy in Venice | Gold | Rank needs counts (convoys, men) **and a vote** of the council's families | Doge | Split. 62% of 334 |
| Merchant Prince | 1993 | A handful of units, a fogged map | Florins | A **hard cap of 15 units** | A million florins, or the richest in 1492; doge and pope on the way | Loved for thirty years |
| East India Company | 2009 | A few small ships, a home port | Gold | Fleets under a commander; ports taken by force | Beat the rival companies | Split. 52% of 190 |
| Winds of Trade | 2017 | 1 ship, 1 trade office | Gold | Automatic routes unlock at **three ships** | Out-earn the rival companies | Split. 61% of 154 |

## 1. Patrician II and III (Ascaron, 2000 and 2003)

Patrician III is Patrician II with its add-on, sold under a new number in English. The same game for this purpose.

### From one to many

- **Start.** "One or two ships of small size, a trade office in the town of player's choosing, and some money" (Wikipedia). You are a Shopkeeper.
- **The rank ladder**, eight rungs: Shopkeeper, Trader, Merchant, Travelling Merchant, Councillor, Patrician, Lord Mayor, Alderman. You rise by wealth **and** by standing in your town: feasts, gifts to the church, public buildings paid for, the town defended (Wikipedia).
- **Ship two** costs only gold and a berth at a shipyard. Nothing else gates it.
- **The real gate is a man.** A ship does nothing by herself. To give her an automatic route, or to make her the armed lead of a convoy, she needs a **captain**, and captains are found one at a time, by chance, sitting in a tavern (?). Players plan round this: "find a captain before you hire an administrator. as you can use him in every town" (forum, macbraveheart68). A captain has three skills, trade, sailing and fighting; a forum post gives the effects as 2% off costs for each money bag, weapon strength for each sword, speed for each sail.
- **The automatic route.** A screen for one ship or convoy: a list of towns in order, and under each town a list of goods, each with buy or sell, how many, and a price limit. A player: "Once you build up a set routine route, you can even just automate the route, so that the ship sails between up to around a dozen ports buying and selling the same things over and over for a profit." Routes can be saved, loaded and reused in a later game (forum, AlP).
- **A house in a second town.** A trading office elsewhere needs a building permit there, which needs rank and local standing (?). In it you can place an **administrator** who buys or sells for you at price limits while you are away. Players wish he were cleverer: "Administrators can either buy or sell, not both" (forum, AlP).
- **Convoys.** "You have to have an armed ship as the leader" (forum, jynx), with a captain. Other ships then sail under her guns.
- **After that:** workshops and farms, houses to let, a seat on the council, the mayor's chain (walls, the watch, sieges), and missions for the Hanse that make you Alderman.

### Minute to minute

- **Start (hours 1 to 5).** One ship, your own hand on every sale. Open a town's market, read twenty prices, buy what is cheap, sail, sell. You learn which towns make what. It is slow: one player gave up when "it takes a projected 2 hours to get through the tutorial cause you're waiting for it to sail".
- **Middle (hours 5 to 30).** Three to ten ships. You find a captain, write your first route, watch it for two rounds, fix a price limit, and leave it. Then a second office and an administrator. This is the part players call the best: "When you reach the stage in the game where you unlock trading offices in other towns, the game becomes even better!"
- **End (hours 30 to hundreds).** Dozens of ships in convoys on saved routes, an office in most towns. You work in lists: the ship list, the office stock, the balance sheet. Your hands are on town building, elections and the odd pirate. One player with thousands of hours: "I only have 2 saved games- that is how much of a marathon this game is."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A fixed-angle painted town fills the left three quarters: quay at the bottom with your ships tied up, your office, rows of your own workshops and houses behind, the wall you paid for round it. Down the right edge runs a narrow wooden panel: at its top a small sea chart of the North Sea and Baltic with white sail marks; under it the date, your rank and your gold; under that a scrolling list of ship names with small ship pictures (?). Over the town lies a parchment dialog: the route table, a column of town names on the left, and for the chosen town a grid of goods with a buy or sell mark, a number and a price in each row. The town behind it has not been looked at for an hour.

### What players say

Praise:

- "The beauty of Patrician 3 is in it's long term gameplay which has you slowly work your way from a humble trading shopkeeper to a master merchant." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198007233448/recommended/33570/
- "You become a builder of prosperity." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198061966626/recommended/33570/
- "Most of that becomes automated away as you start to get a fleet of ships etc." (said as praise) https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197960420790/recommended/33570/
- "there is a ton of useful automation in the game (auto trading ftw)" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198070542305/recommended/33570/
- On GOG: "You can hire a captain for any of your ships and once you do, you can set up trade routes where you have the ship hit specific cities and trade in whatever goods you want." https://www.gog.com/en/game/patrician_3

Hate:

- "The trading system feels fine, as long as you just have 2 or 3 ships...But if you want to start automating the selling process, it will become quite a bit more complicated, a messy quagmire" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197996691164/recommended/33570/
- "The whole gameplay is just about looking at prices, prices, prices" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198041856540/recommended/33570/
- "It feels way too tedious to manage basic routes without pulling your hair out." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199026195605/recommended/33570/

### The first hour's feel

Mostly lost, and nobody minds much, because the thing that replaces it is good: the town. The player stops being a skipper and becomes a townsman whose money shows in stone. The hand-trading verb is still there (any ship can be taken off its route and sailed by hand) but there is never a reason to. What the game keeps from the first hour is its **knowledge**: a late player still knows that Reval has skins and Lübeck iron goods, because he wrote that into every route himself.

## 2. Patrician IV (Gaming Minds, 2010)

### From one to many

- **Start.** "Starting with a single ship and counting house, your goal is to make your fortune across the many ports" (player). The sea chart is now the main screen; towns are 3D views you step into.
- **Captain and route** as before, but the route screen offers ready-made choices so a route can be made in a few clicks (?).
- **The ladder counts things.** Each rank asks for wealth, standing and a number of ships and works (?). A player who loved Patrician III names the cost: "In P3 I took pride in making my routes as efficient and profitable as possible using as few ships, but in P4 I had fleets just sitting there because you have to have ships to advance for no reason."
- **The end** is the same: mayor, alderman, founding towns.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Hand trading between three or four towns, with a row of dots on each good showing how the sale moves the town's stock: "A helpful four-dot display also shows you the effect of your trade on the city's overall supply".
- **Middle.** Routes and convoys: "how to best set up your routes/convoys will be what you spend most of your time doing."
- **End.** Interruptions. Pirates find your convoys, and each fight pulls you off the work: "You would play the game for ten minutes and do what you wanted to do, build up your empire, and then have to spend the next 20 minutes fighting off pirates."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A 3D sea chart of northern Europe in soft colours, seen from above and a little aslant; towns as small models with name plates; your convoys as single ship models with a banner. A bar across the top with gold, date and rank. A panel at one side with a list of convoys, each a line with a status word (sailing, trading, fighting) (?). A red sword mark blinks over one of them.

### What players say

- Praise: "A perfect balance of complexity and fun" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197970314107/recommended/57620/
- "Why should I spend 3 hours building out an intricate fragile automated trade loop which can break at any time due to a single misclick for 10% margin" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561199265849554/recommended/57620/
- "This game feels like you're sitting in an 8 hour meeting with middle managers" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198019258564/recommended/57620/
- "Its pretty much impossible to go bankrupt in this title, the economy has simplified to boring" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198001816907/recommended/57620/
- The press agreed: "fun to play once" (IGN), a "very dry affair" (CGMagazine), 66 on Metacritic (Wikipedia).

### The first hour's feel

Lost, and replaced by less than in Patrician III. Three things did it: nobody can go broke, so the first hour's care with a small purse has no later form; the ladder asks for ships as a number, so ships stop meaning anything; and the fleet interrupts the player instead of serving him.

## 3. Port Royale 2 (Ascaron, 2004)

### From one to many

- **Start.** "You start as a small time trader with a small ship, a warehouse, and a purse of gold."
- **The gate is rank, and rank is capital.** "Once you obtain enough capital you increase in rank. Rank increases allow you to hire more captains to have separate fleets, and obtain more building permits in different ports." (same player). So the number of fleets is capped by a number you can read.
- **A convoy** is up to ten ships under one captain hired in a tavern (German Wikipedia). The captain learns: "once your captain reaches a high enough experience level, you can send him off to do trading while you go do something else" (GOG review). The route tool works by rules the player sets (German Wikipedia: "Durch eine regelbasierte Handelsrouten-Automatik kann der Spieler die Kontrolle der Schiffe delegieren").
- **A permit** in a town lets you build a warehouse, then works.
- **The top:** missions for a viceroy earn land, and on it your own town, "up to 16 apparently".

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** One ship between the nearest five towns, learning the two kinds of town that need each other.
- **Middle.** Three or four convoys on routes; a first works; a military convoy for pirates. A player sums the climb up: "From being a small shipowner who trades between provinces, to a medium businessman who not only controls good amount of trade routes but also have some influence over the military aspects to the final 'endgame' aspects where we control the entire trade routes, own almost all businesses".
- **End.** Supplying your own towns with everything, and fighting for more land: "a lot of military convoy grinding, annexing a town so you'll be rewarded more land, and restarting the military convoy grind."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A 3D sea, blue-green, the Caribbean from above, sixty small towns with flags of four nations, little ship models with wakes. A convoy list down one side. Over it a route window: towns in a column, goods in rows, numbers in boxes. And beside the keyboard, in one player's case, real paper: "It's not a relaxing game; I resorted to graph paper to map out my major supply routes."

### What players say

- "This is simply the best version of Port Royale...none of the successors of this game really addressed these issues." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197978980746/recommended/12470/
- The graph paper, and the 16 towns: https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198034076507/recommended/12470/
- The climb in one sentence: https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198212764989/recommended/12470/
- The press: "passes the time, but gets old fast" (PC Format); "somewhat slow to start" (Computer Gaming World); 75 on Metacritic (Wikipedia).

### The first hour's feel

Half kept. The captain who learns is the best idea in the series: the man you hand a convoy to is one you have watched grow. The graph paper is the other half: the plan is the player's own, worked out by hand, and the game is where he tests it. What is lost is the single ship; by the end she is one line among forty.

## 4. Port Royale 3 (Gaming Minds, 2012)

### From one to many

- **Limits lifted by level.** "Initial operating constraints limit fleet count and town building permits. As players gain levels based on net worth, these restrictions lift" (Wikipedia). A player: "Control over multiple fleets (based on rank), each having one or more ships, they can be set on automated traderoutes".
- **Routes from the first day.** "you can simply start out with a strong trade route on the first day".
- **Two campaigns,** Trader and Adventurer, so the fighting can be left out altogether.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Read the icons on the map (each town shows what it lacks), make one route.
- **Middle.** "Buy some ships and tell them where to go trade stuff. Watch the money go up until you can afford more ships."
- **End.** The same sentence, with larger numbers. Combat is left to the machine: "boat combat is best left on autopilot, as is most of your trade empire."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

The 3D Caribbean again, brighter. Each town carries a small round icon of the good it wants most. Convoys sail lines you no longer look at. The gold counter in the top bar ticks upward by itself. One reviewer of the press found the map "looks like you are playing a much older game" (Midlife Gamer, via Wikipedia).

### What players say

- "very easy to set up a profitable one, once you've done it it's AFK Simulator 2000" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197983740996/recommended/205610/
- "Watch the money go up until you can afford more ships. That's about it." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198051630173/recommended/205610/
- "honestly just a grind for money to build things to make you more money" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198068370015/recommended/205610/
- "you'll love it for the first four, maybe five hours but then the disappointment" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197994640069/recommended/205610/
- Kinder: "the challenge lies in learning the somewhat mystifying AI, fanaggling trade routes to suit the needs of the economy" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198025059416/recommended/205610/
- The press: "none of it was much fun" (GameSpot, 5/10, via Wikipedia).

### The first hour's feel

Gone by the fifth hour, by the players' own count. The route asks for no knowledge the player had to earn, so writing it is not a reward, and nothing ever goes wrong with it, so there is nothing to come back to.

## 5. Port Royale 4 (Gaming Minds, 2020)

### From one to many

- **Start.** You are the governor of one small town with one convoy. The store page's own summary of the game is the ladder: "managing production chains, creating complex trade routes across the Caribbean, and fulfilling tasks for your nation's viceroy to earn fame and unlock new buildings and ships."
- **Two currencies.** Gold buys ships and works. **Fame**, earned by the viceroy's tasks and by growing towns, buys concessions: ship types, buildings, captains' tricks (?). Trade **licences** are bought town by town. A player on the curve: "Favour points requirements increases exponentially, but the tasks that give you favour don't".
- **The route tool is the star.** You draw the line on the chart yourself, round shoals and with the wind and current, and players who stay praise exactly that: "Wind & current was ignored in previous games". But the buying can be left to one button: "Simply chose to create a new trade route, ctrl-click cities, and bam, your ship will automatically buy only what can be sold at a profit."
- **The clock is tied to the zoom:** "The more zoomed-out you are, the faster it goes."
- **Battles** are turn-based on a grid, up to eight ships a side.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** One convoy, one route, the auto button.
- **Middle.** Works in your town, more convoys, licences, the viceroy's errands.
- **End.** Either war ("The economic game ends aproximately 6 months in when war starts and you are constantly at war") or nursing jammed convoys ("your automated convoys inevitably end up stuffed to the gunwales with cargo, sailing from port to port unable to trade").

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

One seamless picture. Pulled right back: the whole Caribbean as a sea chart, pale water, green islands, dotted route lines in your colour looping between sixty towns, convoy markers sliding along them fast because the clock runs with the zoom. Pushed in: one town in full 3D, plots in a grid, your plantations in rows. On the right a route panel: the stops in order, each with a small table of goods and an "automatic" tick. The player sits pulled back.

### What players say

- "Now you just buy a ship, ham fist the automatic button on every city in your trade route, and turn on the money faucet. Making money is trivially easy without any user interaction." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197975309551/recommended/1024650/
- "After about 3-4 hours, I had 15 millions, and no way to spend it meaningfully." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198009925619/recommended/1024650/
- "setting up a trade route is tiring, and commanding a convoy your self is pointless" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198130078996/recommended/1024650/
- "As a city builder it's awful, as a combat game it's basic, as a trading game it's boring." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197969467659/recommended/1024650/
- "No more 'warehouse manager' to automatically sell to the town at best prices." (577 hours played) https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198031403745/recommended/1024650/
- Praise: "This game is simply relaxing, time passer and a lot of fun to play. Best of all, I control the speed of time." (344 hours) https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197963428726/recommended/1024650/
- Praise: "If you like transporting goods around and seeing cities grow, sometimes as direct consequence of your actions." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198013132245/recommended/1024650/

### The first hour's feel

There is no first hour of the old kind: the automatic button is there from the first minute, so the player never trades by hand long enough to know anything. What the game does keep, and it is worth stealing, is **the chart as a place**: wind, current and shoals make the drawing of a line a real decision, and that decision is the same in hour one and hour one hundred.

## 6. Rise of Venice (Gaming Minds, 2013)

### From one to many

- **Start.** One convoy at Venice. "Everything takes place in the world map": no town views to load.
- **The gate is a vote.** "Ranking up has been changed. You now need to have a certain amount of convoys, sailors/workers, etc AND you need to get permission of the council of Venice." The council is the city's great families; each has a view of you that you move by favours, missions and money.
- **A licence** is bought for each city before you may trade there freely (?).
- **A family tree:** kin can be sent on errands ("Family members can actually be send on missions").
- **The top:** Doge.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Venice and the near Adriatic by hand.
- **Middle.** Routes. A player with 547 hours gives the whole method: "The key to success in RoV is the trade routes...buy at 120%, sell at 140% (of production cost)".
- **End.** Courting the families. The same player: "The true game isn't to make a profit. The game is to appease the Council so you can rise in rank."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

The Mediterranean as a warm painted chart, Venice at the top, Alexandria and Constantinople at the edges. Convoys as galley models. In the middle a council window: a row of family crests, each with a face and a small bar for how they stand toward you, and a ballot under them. Behind it the route lines run by themselves.

### What players say

- The council as the real game: https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197974196784/recommended/227020/
- "a nice politics/intrigue game mechanic that fits the setting along with a nice sense of progression" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198007293796/recommended/227020/
- "The game is thus reduced to programming your obvious trade routes and hoping for a mission." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198009289636/recommended/227020/
- "a few items to trade the game becomes nothing of a challenge after 3 hours or so" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197961042609/recommended/227020/
- "The city building and trade route management was better in the now MUCH cheaper Patrician III" (199 hours) https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197971948490/recommended/227020/
- One finished it: "I finally accomplished my end-goal of becoming Doge, I had clocked in about sixteen hours" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197963375232/recommended/227020/

### The first hour's feel

Lost on the trading side as in Port Royale 3. But the vote is the one new idea in the family after 2004, and the players who stayed for hundreds of hours stayed for it: the gate is the opinion of named people, not a sum.

## 7. Merchant Prince (QQP, 1993) and Machiavelli the Prince (1995)

### From one to many

- **Turn-based**, Venice, 1300 to 1492. You begin with a few units (?) and a map "resembling period cartography": true near Venice, guesswork far off. Exploring it is half the early game.
- **A hard cap.** "Players command up to 15 trading units simultaneously" (Wikipedia): galleys (fast, frail), cogs (slow, sound in storms), donkey trains, camel caravans. There is no sixteenth.
- **Routes** can be set for fleets and caravans (GOG page of the 1995 version).
- **So growth goes elsewhere.** Bribe the Council of Ten for its offices (head, general, admiral, builder). Be elected doge. Buy church livings to steer the conclave, and the papacy. Burn a rival's warehouse, spread a rumour, hire a knife. "First to reach one million florins wins, or highest net worth at game's end."

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Move one galley into the fog. Find a city. See what it sells.
- **Middle.** Ten units, each moved or sent on its route each turn; the first bribes.
- **End.** The same fifteen units, mostly on routes. The turn is spent in Venice and Rome: who holds which seat, who can be bought, who must be stopped.

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A parchment map of Europe, Africa and Asia, drawn as a mapmaker of 1300 would, the far lands now filled in where your units have been. Fifteen small unit marks, no more. Along the edge a strip of buttons that open rooms: the council chamber with ten senators' faces and a price under each; the conclave with cardinals' hats in your colour and your rivals'. The map has not grown busier since turn thirty. The rooms have.

### What players say

- "a finely balanced, well presented, and intensely enjoyable game" with "considerable replay value" (Tim Carter, Computer Gaming World, April 1994, via Wikipedia).
- It "captures the greed and treachery of medieval Italy even more than the classic Machiavelli boardgame" (CGW, June 1994, via Wikipedia).
- On GOG, thirty years on: "you can lose hours to just telling yourself: just one more turn." And: "you're competing with your AI opponents...in multiple arenas. Besides amassing a fortune via trade, you're also making waves back in Rome by trying to become the Doge or even Pope." And: "the best trading game I have ever played". https://www.gog.com/en/game/machiavelli_the_prince

### The first hour's feel

Kept better than in any other game here, for two plain reasons. The number of units never grows past what one person can hold in the head, so a galley in 1480 is moved with the same care as one in 1300. And the map goes on giving: there is fog to lift for most of the game.

## 8. East India Company (Nitro Games, 2009)

### From one to many

- **Start.** A company of one of eight nations, a home port in Europe, a few small ships and a sum of money. The trade is a long line, not a web: out to India round Africa, and home.
- **Fleets.** Ships are grouped in fleets, each under a commander who gains skills; a fleet holds a handful of ships (five (?)) and can be told to run a route by itself (?).
- **Ports** in Africa and India are taken by force or treaty and built up.
- **The goal** is set by the company's board: bring home so much of the main goods, hold so many ports, outlast the rivals.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Budgeting: "the way you have to budget your money and buy more ships to trade more goods over time to make more money". Each fleet out and back is a year's bet.
- **Middle.** Three or four fleets passing each other on the same line; the first port taken.
- **End.** Waiting, with the clock pushed up. "Buy stuff at home port, and sell them in India, allowing you to buy items in India and selling them at home port. Back and forth".

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A 3D map in the manner of a painted globe laid flat: Europe at the top left, Africa down the middle, India at the right. Ports as flagged markers along the coasts. Six or eight fleets as single ship models strung along one long curve round the Cape. A speed control with five steps at the top, set to four. A fleet panel with five ship cards and a commander's portrait.

### What players say

- "The game plays so slow. It gives you five times speeds... number 4 Why isn't this speed the first number?" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198039823178/recommended/25930/
- "You really don't buy anything but the big ticket items and sell them at your home port. Less of a commerce simulator, more of an importation simulator." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198022152471/recommended/25930/
- "There is too much automation, most especially with the combat. When you have the computer auto-fight a battle for you, the results are always far better than you could ever do manually." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197991137016/recommended/25930/
- "Late game breaks due to it taking forever to bankrupt competitors" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197985260138/recommended/25930/
- Praise: "If you expand too fast, you die. If you expand too slowly or in the wrong ways, you haven't a chance." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198146523218/recommended/25930/
- Praise: "Big advantage is the fact that you can dirrectly command each one of your ships." (395 hours) https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198028589875/recommended/25930/
- The press: "the appeal wears thin long before it should have" (IGN, via Wikipedia).

### The first hour's feel

Kept in one way and lost in another. The risk stays real: growing too fast can still sink the company late on, which none of the Kalypso games can say. But a voyage that long with nothing on it is only a wait, and a wait is what the speed button is for. Once the player's hand lives on the speed button the first hour is over.

## 9. Winds of Trade (Rhombico Games, 2017)

One developer's game. Worth a look because it is the nearest in size to Kapman, and because it shows the middle of the ladder with a rung missing.

### From one to many

- **Start.** "You start with a ship and trading office in one city and aim to build a vast merchant empire." The world is made fresh each game: "Randomly generated world with 20 cities, spread across a few islands with multiple countries."
- **An office before trade.** "in order to trade in a city, first you need to build a trade office there."
- **Routes unlock by count.** "Automated trade route system, which unlocks once you have 3 ships."
- **No fleets at first.** "There are unfortunately no fleets in the game, yet, but you can copy orders from one ship to another". A later player found convoys had come.
- **Beside it:** a stock market, loans, smuggling, colonies, turn-based fights.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** "In the beginning I started to go back and forth between an handful of cities just to win little money". And the clock does not wait: "the timer doesn't stop when you arrive in a port though, this is sad."
- **Middle.** Three ships, routes written as scripts.
- **End.** "once you establish a few profitable trade routes and manage to avoid significant losses from pirates early on, you essentially dominate the game."

### The end screen, so it could be drawn

A flat painted chart of a few islands, twenty towns, ships as small top-down marks each with its own thin line. No grouping: thirty marks, thirty lines. Windows over it for a ship's orders, the stock market's graph, a city's price list.

### What players say

- "When loading and unloading ships you have to do it manually for each ship; I'm not against this being an option but when you have a fleet of ships you should be able to load and unload the whole fleet at once." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198004368051/recommended/576260/

- Praise: "This PUZZLE game is all about automatic trading. It is fun watching ships sail into port" https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561198110425220/recommended/576260/
- Praise: "The political and stock market mechanics are well-implemented and offers multiple approaches to the game." https://steamcommunity.com/profiles/76561197995191239/recommended/576260/

### The first hour's feel

The first hour is the weak one (no pause in port, prices to be held in the head), and the last is a won game. The good part is a narrow band in the middle. The lesson is about order: this game gave the player many ships before it gave him a way to hold many ships.

## 10. What the ten say together

**The ladder is always the same three rungs.** A man (captain), a rule (route), a house (office). No game here found a fourth. Kapman's first answer already has all three: a skipper, orders in words, a host or factor.

**What the gate is made of decides how the late game feels.**

| The gate | Games | How it feels late |
|---|---|---|
| A sum of money or net worth | Port Royale 3, Winds of Trade | Nothing. The gate opens by itself while you watch |
| A count of things owned | Patrician IV, Rise of Venice (half) | Ships bought to tick a box: "fleets just sitting there" |
| A second currency | Port Royale 4 (fame) | A grind when its price climbs faster than its supply |
| A person found, kept and grown | Patrician III, Port Royale 2 | Good. A captain is a find, and a loss |
| Other people's opinion | Rise of Venice (the vote), Merchant Prince (the council), Patrician III (mayor) | The part long-time players name as "the true game" |
| A hard cap | Merchant Prince (15) | Care for each unit to the end |

**The route is loved once and resented after.** Every quote in praise is about making one. Every quote in anger is about a route that needs no thought ("turn on the money faucet", "AFK Simulator 2000") or about one that breaks without a word ("fragile", "stuffed to the gunwales"). None of these games has a route that ends, or a route that comes back and tells you how it went.

**Money has nowhere to go.** "15 millions, and no way to spend it meaningfully." Where it does have somewhere to go, it is always toward people: a town that grows, a council that votes, a conclave.

**The clock is the tell.** At the start nobody touches the speed. At the end everybody plays at the top speed (East India Company's "Why isn't this speed the first number?"; Port Royale 4 ties speed to zooming out, which makes the far view the fast view and so the default one). When the game is played at top speed, its world has stopped mattering.

**The fleet turns on its owner.** In Patrician IV the fleet is where the pirates find you; in Port Royale 4, where the jams are. The player's time goes to whichever ship is in trouble, never to the one he would choose.

## 11. Five lessons for Kapman, and a proposal for each

These build on `Design/research_scale_to_fleets.md`. They do not replace its rules (three crossings, letters, orders in words, the winter council, one boat always yours); they sharpen five of them against what these games show.

### Lesson 1. Make the gate a man you have sailed with, not a sum

Patrician III and Port Royale 2 gate the fleet on a captain, and it is the only gate in this family that nobody complains about. Patrician IV gates on a count of ships and is hated for it. Port Royale 2's captain also learns, so the man you trust with a convoy is one you watched.

**Proposal: the steersman's season.** A boat can be given only to a man who has sailed in your own boat for one whole season, Easter to Martinmas, and has taken the steering oar on at least three legs (a card offers it: "Let Folkmar bring her in"). Together with the three-crossings rule this makes two keys for every new keel: a route you know and a man you know. The number of keels is therefore the number of such men, and no rank, level or purse opens it. On the House tab each man shows three plain words learned from his own tallies, not numbers: how he is at sea, at the scales, and with the truth (each: poor, fair, good, or "not yet known").

### Lesson 2. A route is a decision: let it end, and let it speak

The best minute in these games is writing a route; the worst hours are living with one. A route that runs for ever is a faucet. A route that jams in silence is a chore. Both come from the route being a machine.

**Proposal: orders last one season and come home as a tale.** Orders are given at the winter council and end at Martinmas. They are never a table of stops and prices: they stay the earlier paper's one sentence and one rule. Because the skipper is a man with the world's own trader mind, he cannot jam: if the wine will not sell at Lundenwic he does what his nature and your rule allow, and tells you. At the season's end each boat gives one reckoning card: silver brought home, what he did where your orders ran out, one thing he learned (a price word you now hold, with his name and its age on it), and one thing he wants. Then you choose again: the same orders, new ones, or another man. With eight keels that is eight decisions a winter, each with a face. Nothing can be saved as a template and nothing renews itself.

### Lesson 3. Silver must be turned into standing that named people grant

"15 millions, and no way to spend it." The long-lived games send money toward people: "The true game isn't to make a profit. The game is to appease the Council"; "You become a builder of prosperity"; doge and pope in Merchant Prince. A rank that the game hands out at a net worth is worth nothing; a rank that somebody gives is a story.

**Proposal: a ladder of five grants, each with a giver and a yearly due.** No rung is reached by wealth alone; each is given by someone in the world who can also take it back, and each costs something every year, so the drain grows with the house.

| Rung | Who gives it | For what | The yearly due |
|---|---|---|---|
| A berth of your own | The reeve of your home port | The debt paid on its day | The toll, paid without quarrel |
| A partner (félag) | Another merchant | A season sailed beside him without a broken word | His share |
| A lord's protection | An abbot or a count | A gift, and a cargo carried for him | A cargo carried each year at his price |
| A seat among the sworn brothers | The merchants of your port who "swear and drink together" (Tiel, 1020; before that, an oath among men who sail together) | Two members speak for you | The feast, and the dead man's share when a brother is lost |
| The king's peace | A king or his reeve | Three crossings of the wide sea at your own cost (the English rule of the early eleventh century, where it holds), or a great gift | A ship for his service when he calls (?) |

Each giver is a person in the places files with a memory, so each rung can be lost by a deed. The winter council shows the ladder as five lines with a name beside each. (The king's due needs a source for each realm and date before it is written into the game.)

### Lesson 4. Keep the fleet small and keep the same screen

Merchant Prince stops at fifteen units and is still being played. Winds of Trade let ships multiply before it could hold them. Every game that lets the fleet grow without end finishes in a ship list at top speed, and a Patrician III player's pride was in "as few ships" as would do the work.

**Proposal: eight keels, no list, no faster clock.**

- **Eight keels at the most**, your own boat among them (the earlier paper's cap; these games are the evidence for it). Growth past eight is **shares**: a third of another man's boat is one line on the tally that pays or fails at Martinmas and asks nothing in between.
- **No screen in the game holds a list longer than eight**, and none is a table of numbers. The House tab is eight cards: a boat's small picture, her skipper's name, her orders as their sentence, and her last word with its age.
- **The chart stays the screen.** The end game adds marks to it and takes nothing away: seven marks in the house's colour, solid, hollow or faint by the age of the word; seven thin threads for the ordered routes; a second row of notches on the day stick for when each is due.
- **The clock keeps 1x to 4x for the whole game**, and the far view is never the fast view. A year passes because the player chose to sail or to wait.

How it would be drawn, late in the game: the same pixel chart as in the first hour, more of its fog lifted. Her own mark, solid, off the Jutland coast. Three hollow marks with small ages beside them ("9 d", "21 d", "40 d") at Lundenwic, Quentovic and Ribe. Two faint marks at sea where their orders should have them. One thread ends in a red notch on the day stick. At the bottom left the tally, with silver in four small heaps: aboard, in store, lent, owed. Nothing on the screen is a table.

### Lesson 5. The master's boat does what no other can, and the fleet may not call her away

In Port Royale 4 "commanding a convoy your self is pointless". In Patrician IV the fleet takes two thirds of the player's time for its troubles. In East India Company the long voyage is a wait. The games that hold their players give the hand something only the hand can do: Port Royale 4's drawn sea lines, Merchant Prince's fog, East India Company's "you can dirrectly command each one of your ships".

**Proposal: four things only she can do, and a rule of silence.**

Only the boat you sail yourself can:

1. **Lift fog.** No skipper adds to the chart. Where you have not been, nobody can be sent.
2. **Open a route.** The three crossings are yours to make.
3. **Hear the word first-hand.** A skipper brings home one price word a season; you hear three talks at every call.
4. **Carry the house's name.** A grant on the ladder (Lesson 3) is asked for in person: the gift to the abbot, the oath at the feast, the king's hall.

And the rule of silence: **word from the fleet reaches you only in port.** A letter waits with your host or factor at the ports where you have one, or comes aboard with a skipper who lies at the same quay. It is read in the Hear beat, with the clock held. Nothing about another boat ever opens a card while yours is under way, and no trouble of theirs can be mended from where you stand except by a reply that sails. A boat lost is learned of weeks later, as news, which is how it was.
