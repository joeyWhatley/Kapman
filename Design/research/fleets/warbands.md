# From one unit to many: seven warband and caravan games

**The ask (4 Oct):** "research a way for the game to scale from slow and methodical at the start to managing fleets of
traders, and how that end game will look from a gameplay look standpoint."

This file goes deeper on the games where you begin as one person or one small band and end with many: Mount & Blade:
Warband, Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord, Kenshi, Battle Brothers, Wartales, Vagrus and Caravaneer 2. It keeps the first
answer's three rules (hand over only what you have done; ships are people you sent, known by letter; you never stop
sailing) and tests them against what these games' own players say.

## The short answer

1. **None of the seven changes its screen as it grows.** The end game is the first hour's screen with more on it. Where that works (Battle Brothers), the game capped the count so the screen never fills. Where it fails (Kenshi, Bannerlord, Wartales, Caravaneer 2), the count outgrew the screen and the player was left clicking through it.
2. **The best-loved one grows better, not bigger.** Battle Brothers puts twelve men in a fight on day 10 and twelve on day 300. What changes is who they are. Its players set the late game at the day "more than half of the team" are men "you do not want to replace".
3. **The others make each beat longer as you grow.** Wartales fights go from a few minutes to "10-20 mins per fight"; Caravaneer 2 ends at "40 vs 40"; in Vagrus the caravan sim comes to serve "PURELY TO SLOW YOU DOWN". This is the clearest warning for Kapman: a bigger house must never make one leg of one voyage take longer to play.
4. **Deputies fail in the same way everywhere.** Bannerlord's clan parties and caravans are sent out, lose, and are re-made with one click. Players stop caring ("I give them ZERO troops") or keep them on a lead ("only really going to add something provided you use them in an army under your own control").
5. **The money sink arrives too late or as a chore.** Vagrus's outpost costs 6,000 silver "in a game where 1000 silver will buy you everything else you'll ever need", and one player reached it on day 1625 with the stories used up. Warband's enterprises are "a lot of boring work".
6. **Players want an end they choose.** Battle Brothers offers retirement after the first crisis; Warband players stop when "there is nothing challenging ahead" and say so with a shrug: "You do not insist to play chess until the checkmate, do you?"

## 1. Mount & Blade: Warband

### How one becomes many

- **The rule.** The party's size is a sum you can read on the party screen: a base of 30, plus 5 for each point of the Leadership skill, plus 1 for each point of Charisma, plus 1 for each 25 renown (from memory; the wiki refused the fetch) (?). Renown comes from winning fights against odds and from tournaments. So the cap rises a man or two at a time, and every rise is something you did.
- **The brake.** Wages each week and food each day. Morale falls with every man added and rises with the variety of food and with victories. A big party of raw men is slower on the map and can starve or desert.
- **Where men come from.** Villages give a few volunteers at a time, more if the village likes you. Men are trained up through a tree by fighting. Named companions (sixteen in the game (?)) are met in taverns; some pairs quarrel, so in practice a party holds about eight.
- **The gates above the party.** With enough renown a king offers you an oath and a village. A vassal gathers rents, garrisons castles and is called to campaign. A man who holds a castle with no king over him has founded a kingdom, and now gives fiefs to lords of his own. The lords are the "many": each is a person with a temper who may refuse, sulk or defect.
- **Money that works by itself.** One enterprise in each town (a mill, a dye works, a brewery), bought once from the guild master. A player in the passive-money thread gives the scale: a good trade run "will net you about 15k each run. 15k is enough to buy an enterprise in a city."
- **The screens.** Three, the same all game: the map, the party list (two columns of names and counts), and a weekly budget page of plain lines (rents, enterprises, wages, the sum).

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** You are alone and slow. You lead five farm boys against looters, carry a letter, drive cattle, enter a tournament, buy salt cheap and sell it dear. Every man lost is felt. Every coin has a use.
- **Middle.** Forty to eighty men. You serve a king, ride with the marshal's host, win a village and then a castle. You know lords by name and by grudge.
- **End.** You are a king. You chase enemy lords across the map, sit through sieges, hand out each new castle to whichever lord is least angry, call a feast to mend tempers, and ride back to lift a siege at the other end of the realm.

### The end screen, to draw

A parchment-coloured map seen from above and a little behind, with small 3D riders standing for whole armies. Your own rider in the middle with a number over him. Around him a ring of other riders with names and numbers: your lords, following. Towns and castles as small models with a banner each; most banners are now your colour. Text messages scroll up the left edge in several colours: who took what, who was captured, who has left you. When you stop, a text menu opens over a still picture of a camp or a wall. The weekly budget is a white page with a column of figures.

### What players praise and hate

- "once you have the best armor, and your own faction with at least 5+ castles / towns...it starts to get too easy and repetitive" (Partyboy, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/48700/discussions/0/1495615865218802202/))
- "yeah its boring thats why i always create a new character everytime my character gets too good" (Hayter, same thread)
- "Once I know there is nothing challenging ahead, I just pick up another mod...You do not insist to play chess until the checkmate, do you?" (Tuidjy, same thread)
- "It's a common problem in games unfortunately...the sense of accomplishment tails off when all that's left is conquering castles" (Rob, same thread)
- "Making your own rules, sticking to your roleplaying plan, adding more restrictions or goals to standard gameplay—That's where you can really push limits" (Morkonan, same thread)
- "Vanilla is nice and all but being king (Or Lord on that) feels pretty underwhelming and almost boring." (Spooky Witch, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/48700/discussions/0/1620599015894711457/))
- On setting up enterprises: "ya have to do lots of trading and then set up all the businesses in the towns" (Bobby Digital, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/48700/discussions/0/2564160288799958142/)); the thread's opener calls it "a lot of boring work" (as the reader gave it; check the wording)
- And the praise, from a reviewer who knew it had no end: "just about the most enjoyable game I've ever played", though "there is not a specific quest line and endgame" (Bob Byrne, Black Gate, quoted on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_%26_Blade:_Warband))

### The first hour: kept or lost

Kept in the hands, lost in the heart. You still ride and fight in person at the end, on the same map with the same keys. What is gone is risk and want: you have the best armour, the money no longer buys anything, and the one verb left is the siege. Players do not finish; they begin again, because the climb was the game.

## 2. Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord

### How one becomes many

- **One number gates everything: the clan tier.** Renown raises the clan from tier 0 to tier 6. Players in the Steam threads give the steps as 50, 150, 350, 900 and 2,350 renown for tiers 1 to 5 (tier 6 at about 6,150, from memory (?)).
- **What a tier gives.** More named companions (players give 7 at tier 4 and 9 at tier 6), more parties led by clan members (about one more every two tiers; a player reports "5 parties active" at tier 6), more workshops (one more than the tier, by the figures players give), and a bigger party of your own. Service as a mercenary opens at tier 1, an oath as a vassal at tier 2, a kingdom of your own at tier 4 (from memory (?)).
- **Caravans.** A caravan is bought in a town for about 15,000 denars (?), needs a companion to lead it, and takes one of your party places. It then trades by itself and its profit is a line on the clan page.
- **Clan parties.** Pick a companion or a family member on the clan page, press a button, and he rides off with whatever men you gave him. He recruits, fights and is paid from your purse up to a wage limit you set. One player notes they "do not cost any influence to rise and maintain".
- **Armies.** A ruler or a lord spends influence to call parties to him. The army moves as one blob.
- **Heirs.** People age and die; a child or spouse becomes the player.
- **The screens.** The map; the party list; the **clan page** with tabs (Members, Parties, Fiefs, Income) and a row for each caravan and party; the **kingdom page** with tabs (Clans, Fiefs, Policies, Armies, Diplomacy).

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** As Warband: alone, then ten men, looters, a tournament, a trade run. The first caravan is a real moment: 15,000 saved and a companion sent away.
- **Middle.** Clan tier 3 to 4. A mercenary, then a vassal with a castle. Two workshops, a caravan, a brother leading a second party. The clan page is worth opening.
- **End.** A ruler at war with two or three realms at once. You gather an army, besiege, storm, garrison, move on. Captured lords slip out of your prisons and are back in the field. Your clan parties are caught, ransomed and re-made. Votes come up on who gets each town.

### The end screen, to draw

A painted 3D map with snow in the north and sand in the south. In the middle one large banner-bearer with a number such as 1,200 over him: the army. A column of small portraits down the left edge, one for each party in the army. At the lower right a tall thin log that never stops: prisoners escaped, caravans attacked, wars declared. Round disc buttons along the bottom open the pages. The clan page is a dark panel: names down the left, and on the right a party's troop count, its wage limit as a slider, and one line of income.

### What players praise and hate

- "Once the player reaches Clan Rank 5 or 6, the game really stagnates because the recipe for capturing territory is extremely tedious, slow and uninteresting." (Caelib, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/261550/discussions/0/3452590985292020463))
- "parties are nearly worthless because they don't really make any meaningful gains on their own and they just become a nuisance to have them constantly getting captured" (Caelib, same thread)
- "My daily combat feed is filled with constant spam of nobles escaping my prisons" (Caelib, same thread)
- "Once I get a town or two I lose interest. The rest of the campaign is an awful slog." (teasippin, same thread)
- "The actual problem is the scale of the game. The number of towns is something like doubled from Warband, and conquering Calradia...was already tedious, even with mods." (Rhapsody, same thread)
- "When I start a clan party, I give them ZERO troops. I slow the car down long enough for them to tuck and roll and tell them to get the F out." (Flyingscot, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/261550/discussions/0/592885886403794735/))
- "If a clan party did get defeated, It wouldn't bother me much either. Once the party leader escaped captivity I just tell them to form a new party." (Ruffio, same thread)
- "Additional clan parties are only really going to add something provided you use them in an army under your own control." (kristianjakob, same thread)
- "I worry what they get up to if I don't manage them directly via an army." (the thread's opener, same thread)
- "Caravan is garbage, everytime you on war your caravan get instant destroyed." (PotatoScav, same thread)
- "my caravans with 30 people are being smacked by looters the moment they go out, and now theyre far from profitable" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/261550/discussions/0/4233889512144487733/))
- "I just get bombarded by battles that never stop because the Lords always escape captivity." (DaniTheHero, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/261550/discussions/0/2144217924390594786/))
- A critic: "The actual kingdom-management strategy is weak" (Jon Bolding, IGN, quoted on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_%26_Blade_II:_Bannerlord))

### The first hour: kept or lost

Lost more than in Warband, and the reason is useful. Bannerlord added exactly the things Kapman means to add (trading parties you send out, businesses, heirs) and they are the least loved parts of it. They fail in three ways. They are units and not people: a defeated party is re-made with a click, so nobody mourns it. They have no judgement the player can see or shape: you set a wage limit and nothing else. And they report to a feed that scrolls past, so their story is noise. The sent-out party is not a bad idea. What Bannerlord shows is a sent-out party with no orders, no person and no tale.

## 3. Kenshi

### How one becomes many

- **The rule.** There is no gate but a ceiling. The squad page shows "/30": players confirm "Base game is 30" and "30 characters (which includes animals)". Squads are tabs that hold up to a set number each (one player gives 10 a squad; I remember 20 (?)). The best-known mod lifts the total to 256.
- **Where people come from.** Bars: a stranger at a table who joins for a fee (a few thousand cats, more for the named ones (?)), or for nothing if you say the right thing. Slaves you free. Animals bought from nomads. No renown, no rank.
- **Handing work over.** Any order becomes a standing job if you hold Shift when you give it: mine this, haul to that, cook, mend the wall. A tip from the forum: "If you press the shift key while doing a job ALL highlighted units will add that to their jobs list". Each person has a list of jobs done in order, and a few switches for behaviour (hold, passive, taunt and so on).
- **The base.** Built anywhere, piece by piece, from a build menu; research at a bench opens better pieces. A base draws raids and tax men. A player warns: "A base attracts monsters/animals & raids from whatever local factions are in the region."
- **The screens.** One, always: the 3D world, with a panel along the bottom.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** One person with a rusty bar and 1,000 cats (?). You mine copper outside a ruined town, run from starving men, are beaten, lie in the dust, crawl to a bed, and get up a little tougher. An hour can go on one walk between two towns.
- **Middle.** Six to ten people. A pack beast. One squad that travels and fights, and a few who stay and work. You pick a place for a base and lose it once.
- **End.** Thirty people in three or four squads. The base farms, cooks, smelts and forges with nobody touched for hours of play. You take the war squad out to kill the leaders of the nations that beat you at the start, and come home to mend.

### The end screen, to draw

A wide rust-coloured desert seen from a free camera, high. A walled compound in the middle distance: wind generators turning, square green fields, a stone tower, small figures walking set paths between a well, a field and a store. At the bottom left, a block of small square portraits in rows, each with a thin health bar, grouped under squad tabs (Home, Farmers, Fists). To the right of them, the chosen person's job list as lines of text, and a strip of toggle buttons. At the lower right, the limbs of the chosen person as a stick figure with numbers. Pause and three speeds at the bottom edge.

### What players praise and hate

- "If you can get them assigned jobs and working like a clock it is very satifying." (willderyes, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/1479856439031441711/))
- "50+ Who doesn't want to have an army of dear compaions, each unique from the other?" (dq_177, same thread)
- "I dont like playing with a large group of people. All of my playthroughs, the maximum number of people i reach is about 6-8 people." (JFifo10, same thread)
- "Larger squads are a pain. Multiple squads are masochism." (CHOO CHOO, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/2183537632756037166/))
- "It's so annoying having to click through your 20 odd characters, trying to find the one bloke who has no job" (Karak Sonen, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/1640917196995721095/))
- "once you have 3 ore drills, 2 stone mines, a dozen different refining benches, about 7 crafting benches and a dozen fields, you do wish more and more for a simple list" (Karak Sonen, same thread)
- "I really like this squad size, because running a base requires a lot of micromanagement" (VayneVerso, on a squad of four, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/3111393214044972095/)); and later: "I almost wish I'd kept the group smaller, but I can't bear to part with any of them now."
- "In the late game for now it boils down to how much you enjoy watching the game playout infront of you." (a post from before release, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/610574394225874514/))
- The reason to go on: "destroy every faction that ♥♥♥♥ on me when i was weak" (Fabulous, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/2268069450212527618/)), and "mid to late game is getting the chance to actually explore the map w/ minimal repercussion." (cdplayer906, same thread)
- A critic: the interface "can get cumbersome as your group's numbers grow" (PC Gamer, quoted on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenshi_(video_game)))

### The first hour: kept or lost

Kept, if the player wants it. Kenshi never takes the single person away. Any one of the thirty can be picked and walked across the world alone, and a good share of players never hire anyone ("Primarily a solo player here", says a moderator). Two things carry the first hour forward. The world remembers who beat you, so the late game is a list of old debts. And people are kept because of what they went through with you ("I can't bear to part with any of them now"). What is lost is a way to see thirty people's work at once: the game has no list, so the base is run by clicking on faces.

## 4. Battle Brothers

### How one becomes many

- **The rule.** You begin with three men (the standard origin). Twelve can stand in a battle. The roll holds up to twenty, the rest in reserve (from memory; some origins change it: the militia fields sixteen, the lone wolf starts alone and stays small) (?).
- **The brake.** Wages every day, per man, rising with his level. Food, tools, medicine and ammunition as four numbers on the top bar. A dead man is gone, and all his experience with him.
- **Where men come from.** Each town lists a few men for hire, each with a past (a fisherman, a deserter, a hedge knight) that hints at his worth. You pay to try him out, or hire blind.
- **Renown.** The company's name opens better-paid contracts and, past a threshold, work for the noble houses (?).
- **Ambitions.** Every so often the game offers three or four aims to pick one from: a banner, a cart for more stores, a roll of twelve, a named enemy. Meeting one cheers the men and gives a small lasting gain. The roll and the cart grow through these (?).
- **Crises.** From about day 80 to 120 a great trouble comes (a war of the nobles, the greenskins, the dead; later a holy war). After the first one ends, the game offers to let you retire the company with an epilogue.
- **Difficulty.** A forum moderator on what the game counts: "Contract difficulty will scale based on your 12 highest level bros...If you have more than 12 bros then the lowest level bros after the top 12 do not get counted." (Another player in the thread disputes this.)
- **The screens.** The map; the town as a row of buildings; the roster page; the hex battle.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Three men with pitchforks. A contract to clear a cellar of thieves for a handful of crowns. A fight of ten minutes in which a man may die to one bad roll. Then you count the coins against tomorrow's wages.
- **Middle.** Twelve men, each with a job in the line: shields in front, pikes behind, two archers. The first noble contract. The first crisis.
- **End.** The same fight, the same twelve, against far worse things. Between fights you tune gear and look for men good enough to replace the weakest of twelve. Then the legendary places, which players treat as the true end.

### The end screen, to draw

The roster page. On the left, one man at half length as a painted bust: scarred, a named helmet, a famed sword; beside him his numbers as short bars and a small tree of perks. On the right, the company laid out as busts on a grid: two rows of nine places for the battle line (twelve filled), and a row below for the reserve. Under that, the stash as a grid of gear. The top bar across the whole screen: crowns, food, tools, medicine, ammunition, the day. It is the same page as on day 1, with better faces on it.

### What players praise and hate

- Late game is "when you have more than half of the team made up of top-tier bro that you do not want to replace" (Losk Osmanthus, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/4408543929279501104/))
- "late game is the first crisis, that's why they invite you to start over... the game allows you to choose what late game is for you." (Jean-Maurice Nya, same thread)
- "Beyond that I would consider 'post-game' as you've technically 'won'" (turtle225, same thread)
- "I really appreciate the addition of the Ambition mechanic"; it "gives me a sense of Objective." "Ambitions tell you, 'Hey, try doing this!', and it all still ties in with your company" (WhoPurrsWins, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/135512625244997758/))
- "ambitions are really cool early game", but "After 400+ days i've only this ambition to get a really high renown." "there's still this point where you kinda get bored and want to retire your company." (Papa Pasda, same thread)
- "I don't rush to max it. Can usually get by fine with 12 to 15." (turtle225, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/4346607305579799204/)); "I hardly ever max the company -- a standard core of 12 bros plus 3-4 specialists." (Santo, same thread)
- On the crises: "Instead of getting a new 'crisis' every 80 turns or so, I would prefer smaller events that occur organically within the game." (HarryB, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/2/135511455865872429/)); "would prefer if the events could be started by player action rather than time" (Regulus, same thread); "it is a mechanic that very much impacts your own pacing." (FieserMoep, same thread); "world is still half dead and player is the only actor." (Sarissofoi, same thread). This thread is from before the game's release.
- "Battle Brothers has a good chunk of storytelling/make do with what you get mixed in." (Draba, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/1642038749320660213/))
- Critics: it "demands your time, but grants rewards with a thrilling and earthy experience" (RPGFan); "It's a world I want to dive into again and again, even if I know this incarnation will be just as taxing." (Joel DeWitte, Nintendo World Report; both quoted on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_Brothers))

### The first hour: kept or lost

Kept, better than any of the others, and by the simplest means: the unit of play never grows. A fight is twelve against some, ten to twenty minutes, on day 10 and on day 300. Growth goes into who the twelve are and what they face. The cost is that the map between fights stays thin ("half dead"), and the crisis comes by the calendar, not from what the player did. The game also says when it is over and lets the player close the book.

## 5. Wartales

### How one becomes many

- **The rule.** You begin with four. There is no cap. A number at the top left shows the group's size; a forum poster notes "Ponies make it go up, but prisoners don't."
- **Where people come from.** Taverns, for a fee. Beaten enemies taken in chains and talked round. Wild animals caught: bears, wolves, boars. Ponies to carry weight.
- **The brake.** At every rest each companion is paid and fed, and tiredness forces the rest. Happiness falls if they are not. Players' figures: 11 companions at level 6 cost 268 krowns a rest; 22 at level 9 cost 600.
- **The second brake.** In the adaptive mode the enemy's numbers follow yours. "adaptive scales down enemy gang size to match yours, +/- 1 lvl and usually +1 enemy" (Warden).
- **Paths.** Four tracks (roughly: war, trade, crime, lore (?)) fill as you do those things and pay out small lasting gains, such as lower wages.
- **The camp.** The management screen is a place: a fire in a clearing with things round it (a cooking pot, a tent, a workbench, a lectern, a hitching post, a post for prisoners), and each companion stood at one.
- **The screens.** The map; the town as a small diorama; the camp; the battle.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** Four people, hungry, walking a green county. A fight with a boar is a matter of care. You steal a loaf, do a job for the tavern keeper, and rest when the tiredness bar says so.
- **Middle.** Eight to ten. Each has a trade (cook, smith, angler, scholar). The camp has six things in it. You pick a side in a county's quarrel.
- **End.** Twelve to twenty and a bear. Every fight is 15 against 20 and you have fought it before. You run back across the map for materials. Wages are covered by a large reserve.

### The end screen, to draw

The camp at dusk. A fire in the middle of a forest clearing, painted like a tabletop model with a blurred edge. Round it a ring of objects: a black pot on a tripod, a canvas tent, a bench with tools, a book on a stand, a rail with three ponies, a post with a chained man. A dozen or more small figures: two eat by the fire, one hammers, one reads, a bear sleeps. A horizontal strip of round portraits along the bottom, each with a small trade badge; at twenty it has grown past the screen's width and must be scrolled. At the top, three numbers: krowns, food, the wage due.

### What players praise and hate

- "battles are becoming tediously long (not hard) to the point i just wanna stop playing" (GorishiDan, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1527950/discussions/0/3830916907600153046/))
- "I always fought enemy that have around 25+ and sometime they have reinforcement so it jump up into 35+...fights took around 10-20 mins per fight and still give you same amount of contract gold" (Zeion, same thread)
- "4-6 companions makes fights less epic but much faster. I would hate having 20+ minute fights every single time" (UltrawideGameGuy, same thread)

### The first hour: kept or lost

Half kept. The camp keeps it: the same fire, with more people round it, is the best picture of growth in any of these games, and it is why players grow the band at all. The fights lose it: with no cap, and with the enemy counted against you, every added companion is paid for in minutes at the table. The players' own answer is to refuse to grow. A game where the sound advice is "keep the numbers as low as possible" has put its reward and its cost on the same lever.

## 6. Vagrus: The Riven Realms

### How one becomes many

- **The unit.** You are the vagrus, the master of a comitatus: a caravan. It is counted in kinds, not in persons: workers, fighters, scouts, slaves, beasts of burden and mounts (the store page: "your crew of workers, fighters, scouts, and slaves"). You buy heads in towns.
- **The named few.** A handful of companions with their own stories fight the small fights on a grid, and each can hold a deputy's post in the caravan. The store page names them: "scoutmaster, guard captain, treasure hunter, or beast handler".
- **The brake.** Every head eats every day, and "you burn money on food which eats your cargo space too" (antja). Each day gives a fixed number of movement points. Morale, obedience and vigour are three more numbers that fall.
- **The gates.** Standing with the trading houses and other factions opens their tasks, their tax freedoms and their goods. Insight is spent on the leader's own perks.
- **Two kinds of fight.** Companions fight by turns on a grid. Crew against crew is a quick reckoning of strength; a player speaks of "a comitatus that maintains a strength between 390 to 480".
- **The outpost.** Late, one place of your own. Players give the price as 6,000 silver for the land alone; each stage wants building stuff carried there and many days of waiting.
- **The screens.** The map of nodes and roads; the caravan page; the camp; the event page of text and a picture.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** A dozen heads and three beasts (?), three days of food. You read the price lists of two towns, find almost nothing that pays, take a delivery task from a trading house, and count movement points against the next oasis. Many first runs die on the road. "You need a few practice runs to get the feel of it." (Nordil)
- **Middle.** Sixty to a hundred heads (?). Two or three deputies. A regular round of trade tasks between cities, with companion stories and faction tasks along it.
- **End.** A large comitatus on the same roads. Faction tasks pay the upkeep. The stories are mostly done. The outpost is being built in stages hundreds of days apart.

### The end screen, to draw

A dark hand-painted map: ochre waste, a black mountain range, a few walled cities as small drawn icons. Roads as dotted lines from node to node; your caravan a small marker on one. Across the top a strip of small numbers with icons: money in three coins, supplies, days of food, movement points left today, morale, obedience, and the head count by kind. At the left edge, five or six round companion portraits. When an event comes, a tall page of text opens over the right half with a sepia picture at the top and three or four choices as lines below it. This is the screen of the first hour with larger numbers in the strip.

### The first hour: kept or lost

Kept in form, soured in feeling. The table never changes: the map, the strip of numbers, the page of text. That is close to Kapman's own table, and the praise for the first dozens of hours ("a living world") is praise for that table. But the thing that made the start tense, feeding heads over distance, does not change its nature as you grow. At the end it is the same sum with bigger numbers and no danger, so it reads as friction. And the one building the game offers comes after the stories have run out, with nothing to do while it rises.

## 7. Caravaneer 2

### How one becomes many

- **The rule.** No cap and no rank. A caravan is whatever you can pay for, feed and water: people, pack animals, carts, later motor vehicles.
- **The brake.** Food, water and forage are used by the hour while you move, each by weight and by head. Run out in the desert and people and animals die. A reviewer's memory of it: "I once owned a mule i named Marius...i ran out of food and was about to run out of water".
- **The choices as you grow.** A long-time player lays them out. Donkeys are the "Early Game Default". Camels are "The slowest, but also extremely stable" and can keep herds in carts for "a very solid passive income" on the move. Horses are fast enough to "catch up to and outmaneuver the vast majority of enemies". Vehicles are "THE fastest way to move around the map" but "GUZZLE resources", though "they don't use resources when you are not moving."
- **The economy.** Towns make and use goods; other caravans carry them and can be seen and met. A reviewer: "Prices change based on supply and demand, and every other faction's caravans are simulated in real time". A forum thread asks "how to start new towns", so the late game seems to let you found or build up a place (?); a poster recalls that a third game was to be "more focused on the settlements development".
- **The screens.** The desert map; the town's menu and market table; the caravan list; the battle grid.

### Minute to minute

- **Start.** One man on foot with a donkey. You work out by hand how much water two days takes, buy that and a little cloth, and walk to the next dot. You arrive with nothing left to drink and a small profit.
- **Middle.** Ten people, twenty animals, four carts. A regular round. Guards hired by their shooting. A story that asks you to choose a side.
- **End.** Forty guns, a string of camels with sheep in the carts, or a column of trucks. Money is not short. Fights are large and slow.

### The end screen, to draw

A plain top-down desert: flat sand colour, a few brown ridges, towns as labelled dots, your caravan a small icon leaving a line, other caravans as other small icons that move while you move. A grey panel down one side in the style of a 2000s browser game: the date and hour, the caravan's speed, three stock lines (food, water, forage) each with "days left", and buttons. The caravan page is a table: one row for each person, animal and cart, with weight carried and what it eats. A reviewer on how it sits on a modern screen: "ui quality is really bad at 1080p or higher".

### What players praise and hate

- "BEST game i have ever played, not joking or exaggerating" (danila646, 317 hours, [Steam reviews](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1500820/reviews/?browsefilter=toprated))
- "offers an expansive open world with a surprisingly robust simulated economy" (ominoarya28, same page)
- "been playing since the Caravaneer 1 on flash like 15 years ago and never stopped" (AlYasa, 724 hours, same page)
- "grindy just like kenshi"; "economy is weird sometimes(towns run out of money but you can come back the next day and they are fine again)" (Puccio, 121 hours, same page)
- "battles are turn based and it gets pretty annoying when you are fighting 40 vs 40" (Puccio, same page)
- "I think an auto battle system for looters and a better battle system overall." (SlayerSley, on what a third game needs, [Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1500820/discussions/0/3032599335588769703/)); "never thought a simple flash game would be one of my favorites" (the same poster)
- Steam's own count on the day of this research: 94% of 538 reviews positive ([store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1500820/Caravaneer_2/)).

### The first hour: kept or lost

Kept in the sum, lost in the fight. The game's heart is one honest calculation (weight, water, days) and it is still the calculation at the end, which is why people play it for 700 hours. Growth changes the answer in kind, not only in size: camels with herds, horses that hunt, trucks that eat only when they roll. That is its best idea. But every added gun is one more figure to move by hand in a fight, and the players ask first of all for a way to skip the small ones.

## 9. Five lessons for Kapman, and a proposal for each

### Lesson 1. Cap the house at what the table can show, and grow better before bigger.

Battle Brothers is the only one of the seven whose late game its players describe with affection, and it is the only one that never lets the unit of play grow. Kenshi's cap of 30 is past what its screen can show, and the complaint follows at "20 odd characters". Wartales has no cap and its players cap themselves at 8 to 12.

**Proposal: the day stick is the cap.** The house's ships are a second row of notches on the day stick (already in the first answer), and that row has six places, plus your own boat: seven keels at the very most, on one line, never scrolled. The places open by stage: none in stages 1 and 2, one in stage 3 (the partnership), three in stage 4 (the house), six in stage 5 (the name). The first answer proposed eight; this is one fewer, and tied to something the player can see. And before the first place opens, the player has already grown twice without it: a bigger boat (the Gislinge to a sea-going trader), and a crew of four who have got better. Make "a better boat" and "a better man at the steering oar" the choices of the first ten hours, and a second keel the choice of the next ten.

### Lesson 2. The house may grow; the leg may not.

Wartales ("10-20 mins per fight"), Caravaneer 2 ("40 vs 40"), Vagrus ("PURELY TO SLOW YOU DOWN") and Bannerlord (the siege) all let the size of what you own stretch the length of the thing you do most. Their players' cure is to stay small or to stop.

**Proposal: a budget for the house's voice, written into the pacer.** A leg of your own voyage stays five to fifteen minutes at every stage, as the concept says. To keep it so:

- Your other ships never raise a card while you are at sea. Their word reaches you only in a port (or from a ship met at sea that has a letter for you: one card).
- In a port, at most **three** house letters are opened, the oldest first. Any more wait in a bundle in the chest.
- Each ship that comes home gives **one** card: the tally (what she made, in pennies) and the tale (one line of why). Never a list to tick through.
- The winter council reads the whole bundle back, which is where a year of seven ships belongs.

A test to put in the build: time a leg in a stage 1 save and in a stage 4 save. If the second is more than a quarter longer, the house is talking too much.

### Lesson 3. A skipper is a person with orders and a tale. Never a unit you re-make.

Bannerlord built Kapman's planned feature and its players do not love it: parties "constantly getting captured", re-formed with a click, given "ZERO troops", useful only "under your own control"; caravans "instant destroyed" in a war. Nobody in those threads names a single clan party's leader. Warband's lords are people with tempers, and Kenshi's players "can't bear to part with" theirs. The difference is the person and the story, not the pay.

**Proposal: five ends for every sent voyage, and no re-make button.** A ship under orders comes to one of five ends, each drawn from the same weather, prices and raiders the player sails in, and each told on her one card with its cause:

1. **Home and well**: the tally, and what the skipper learned (a price word that is now yours).
2. **Home and thin**: the price had moved, or he sold at the first fair price as ordered. The card says which.
3. **Late**: windbound, iced in, or wintering abroad. A hollow mark, a red notch, and a letter when one can come.
4. **Hurt**: cargo over the side, a mast gone, a man lost. The skipper's own account, and a choice of how you answer him.
5. **Gone**: no word. After a season, a card with three choices: send to ask, pay for a search, or have a Mass said and write her off. What you chose is remembered by his kin.

A lost skipper is not replaced from a menu. A new one must be found the way the first was: a kinsman grown up, a crewman of yours raised, a partner met in a port. And a skipper's orders (the three slots and one rule of the first answer) are the lever Bannerlord lacks: when a run goes thin, the card shows which of **your** rules made it so.

### Lesson 4. Bring the first thing to build early, in stages, and never make the player wait with nothing to do.

Vagrus put its one building at the end, priced it at six times what anything else costs, and made its stages take hundreds of days after the stories were spent. Its own players say what would have worked: "started that early-mid game", "another layer of questing/gathering", "the game should push you a little more". Warband's enterprises are bought at a counter and never seen again. Wartales's wages show the other half: a cost per head at every rest is "a core mechanic" that "sets limitations to early expansion", and it works as long as it is a slope and not a wall.

Kapman's ladder of works (Design/works_and_upgrades.md) already has the right shape: a rented corner of a storehouse for 6 d, a plot for 480 d, a storehouse for 360 d, a jetty for 600 d, and builders who work while the house sails on. Its prices sit on a slope (a good voyage clears 100 to 300 d), where Vagrus's outpost is a wall. What the seven games add is about **when** and **how it is put in front of the player**. Vagrus's players never met the outpost until the game was nearly done, and the game did not "push".

**Proposal: the reeve's offer, one rung a winter, each with an errand.**

- **The first winter reckoning always carries the first rung.** Whatever the year made, the toll-keeper offers the corner of a storehouse at 6 d. Nobody can miss the ladder, and the first rung costs less than a leg.
- **The winter the 480 d debt is paid, the plot is offered**, at the same 480 d, by the same man who took the debt. The player has just learned what 480 d takes. It is the first act's ending and the second act's beginning in one card.
- **One rung a winter at each port.** The winter reckoning's "one thing to build" (concept 4.4) is kept as a hard rule, so the ladder at home takes years and is never bought in an afternoon. Vagrus's wait was dead time because there was nothing else to do; here the season's voyages are the wait.
- **Each rung names an errand for the coming season**, using the works file's own rule that goods the house brings cut the cost: oak posts down the river for the fence, boards for the storehouse, a wright to be found for the jetty, a man to be trusted for the factor's house. The errand is a reason to sail somewhere, never a condition that locks the rung.
- **Each rung does something on the day it is done**, shown on the next card that uses it ("Your own jetty. No lighterage. A day saved.").

| Winter (at the soonest) | Rung | The errand it offers | What it gives at once |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A corner of a storehouse, 6 d | None | Six units wait for the dear season |
| The debt paid | The plot, 480 d | Oak posts for the fence | House works open at this port |
| The next | A storehouse, 360 d | Boards or roofing, fitted in the hold | Thirty units under your own roof |
| The next | A jetty, 600 d | Find a wright | A day saved each call; a second keel may lie there |
| The next | A factor's house, 400 d | Find the man | Letters from this port; standing orders |

The costs are the works file's; the order and the errands are this proposal's.

### Lesson 5. Offer aims, let the world's troubles land by the house's size, and let the player close the book.

Battle Brothers' ambitions are praised because they point without ordering ("Hey, try doing this!"), and missed late ("After 400+ days i've only this ambition"). Its crises are disliked because they come by the calendar in a world where "player is the only actor". Its retirement is quietly the best thing about its end: "the game allows you to choose what late game is for you". Warband has none of the three, and its players stop mid-game with a shrug about chess.

**Proposal: the vow, the chronicle's blows, and the last voyage.**

- **The vow.** At each winter council the house may take one vow from three offered, drawn from what is true of it that year: cross the wide sea a third time at your own cost; give a bell to the church at Traiectum; see a daughter wed at Ribe; be first of the house at Birka; bring a man home from the Danes. A vow kept is a deed and a line in the saga; a vow dropped costs a little standing and nothing else. Offer vows for every stage, so that the fifth stage has its own (a son fostered at a court, a place of burial, a book copied) and never only "more".
- **The chronicle's blows.** Kapman has what Battle Brothers lacks: real troubles with real dates, in a world with 138 other ships in it. Do not scale them to the player by count, as Wartales does. Let them fall where they fell (Dorestad is raided from 834) and let the house's own choices decide the harm: what lay in the storehouse that year, which ships were in the river, whether a letter of warning came in time and was believed.
- **The last voyage.** At any winter council after the first debt is paid, the player may choose "lay down the oar". The life is closed and ranked in the chronicle, its log is read back as a saga, and the heir takes the chart. The game also offers this, once and plainly, when a life's great vow is kept, as Battle Brothers does after its first crisis. A player who has made the house he wanted should be told he may stop, and that he may go on.

## 10. What the first answer got right, and what this changes

- **Right: letters, not telepathy.** No game here tried it. The nearest, Bannerlord, shows every party live on the map and its players still "worry what they get up to". Not seeing them is not the risk. Not hearing a story from them is.
- **Right: you always have a boat.** Kenshi and Warband both keep the single figure in the player's hands to the end, and both are still played after thousands of hours by people who say so.
- **Right: the three-crossings rule.** No game here has it. All seven let you hand over what you never did, and in all of them the handed-over part is the weakest.
- **Changed: the cap.** Seven keels on one line of the day stick, not eight in a list (lesson 1).
- **Added: a budget for the house's voice** (lesson 2), **five ends for a sent voyage** (lesson 3), **the reeve's offer and one rung a winter** (lesson 4), **the vow and the last voyage** (lesson 5).
- **A warning carried over from Kenshi.** The House tab needs one page that answers "who has nothing to do?" before anything else. Put ships with no orders at the top of the winter council, in red.
