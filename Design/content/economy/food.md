# Grain, fish, livestock, hides, honey, wax and ale, 793 to 1066

**What this is.** The trade in the plain goods: the things everyone ate, wore on their feet, burned on the altar and
drank. None of them made a merchant rich by the boatload. All of them moved, every year, and the year turned on them.
For the game they are the low, steady end of the hold, and they carry the calendar: the harvest, the slaughter, Lent,
Candlemas.

**The short of it, for the design.**

1. **Grain is cheap and heavy.** A full Gislinge boat of wheat (660 kg) is worth about 66 pennies at the king's price
   of 794. It pays only when somebody is hungry: in the hungry months before harvest, in a famine year, or on a coast
   that grows no bread (the Frisian terps, western Norway). In a famine the king forbids taking it out.
2. **Fish is the Church's gift to the trade.** Forty days of Lent, Advent, every Friday: no meat. Herring before 1000
   is a local and seasonal thing, counted by the thousand, not yet the great barrel trade. Sea fish become town food
   about 1000 (the "fish event horizon"). Dried cod from Arctic Norway was at Hedeby well before 1000.
3. **Livestock walks.** It is driven, not shipped, except over short water. It is the good the law watches most
   closely, because a cow is the thing most often stolen. Buy it before witnesses or hang for it.
4. **Hides and tallow come all at once**, at Martinmas, when the beasts that cannot be wintered are killed.
5. **Honey is the only sweetness and wax is the Church's light.** Both come out of the forests of the east and the
   Saxon heaths. Wax is the one good in this file that is worth its weight: light, dense, keeps for ever, wanted by
   every altar, dearest before Candlemas. It is the small boat's best cargo of the seven.
6. **Ale is not a cargo.** It is brewed where it is drunk and sours in a fortnight. What moves is malt, and from the
   9th century hops and, in the Low Countries, gruit. Ale matters to the game as stores, as rent, as the place where
   news is heard, and as a thing the crew will not sail without.

Coin throughout: `d` is the penny (denier). Frankish solidus = 12 d, pound = 240 d (reckoning units, not coins).
West Saxon shilling = 5 d, Mercian = 4 d, mancus = 30 d, English pound = 240 d.

## 1. Grain

### 1.1 What was grown, where, by whom

The makers are peasants paying part of the crop as rent and tithe. The sellers are their lords: abbeys, bishops, royal
stewards. The Capitulare de villis (c. 800) orders every royal estate to account each Christmas for what it has and
to sell the surplus. A merchant buys grain from a steward or a cellarer far more often than from a farmer.

Mills grind it: the horizontal mill at Tamworth (timbers felled c. 855), the tide mills at Nendrum on Strangford
Lough (619-621 and 787) and at Ebbsfleet in Kent (c. 692), and the water mills that the polyptychs count by the
dozen on the abbey estates. Elsewhere the hand quern does it, which is why the Mayen quernstone is a trade good
at all.

### 1.2 Where it was wanted

- **The Frisian terp coast.** Salt marsh: cattle and sheep, little corn, no trees. The Frisians bought their bread
  with cloth. Ermoldus Nigellus (c. 826-828) has the Rhine tell the Vosges that it is by the Rhine the Alsatians sell
  their corn and wine to the Frisians and the sea peoples, and get dyed cloaks back; without the trade Alsace would
  lie drowned in its own wine and glutted with its own grain. This is the one clear text for a Frisian grain trade.
  (Ermoldus, Carmen in honorem Pippini regis I; Lebecq 1983.)
- **Western and northern Norway.** Ohthere, the richest man of Halogaland, "had not more than twenty horned cattle,
  and twenty sheep, and twenty swine, and the little that he ploughed he ploughed with horses" (Old English Orosius,
  c. 890, checked today). His wealth was tribute, not corn. Malt and meal went north with every ship that came south
  for furs and fish.
- **The towns.** Dorestad, Lundenwic, Hamwic, Ribe, Hedeby, Birka and later York, Dublin and London ate what the
  country round them sent in. Birka on its island grew nothing to speak of.
- **Armies and wintering fleets.** In 896 King Alfred camped by the Lea so that the people could reap their corn
  "that the Danes might not deprive them of the crop" (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Ingram 1823, checked today). An army
  that winters eats a shire.
- **Anywhere, in a famine year.** See 1.6.

### 1.3 Routes and ships

- **Down the Rhine** from Alsace and the Middle Rhine to Dorestad and on to the terp coast: river boats, hauled
  upstream by the crew on the tow path (the Frisian merchant hauling his boat past St Goar: Wandalbert of Prum,
  Miracula S. Goaris, 839).
- **Seine, Oise and Scheldt** to the abbeys' own granaries: mostly the abbeys' own boats and carts on toll-free
  charters, not merchants.
- **Thames and the Kent coast** to London. The Graveney boat, built about 895 and left in a Kent creek about 950, a
  clinker coaster of some 14 m and perhaps 6 to 7 tons, is the right ship for this trade; she had hops and Rhenish
  lava querns aboard (Fenwick 1978) (dates from memory: the page was down today).
- **To Norway** from Jutland and England, in the summer, as part of a general cargo.

Grain is a river and coasting trade. Nobody crossed the North Sea for the grain alone unless the price had doubled.

### 1.5 Units, packing, and the small boat

- **The modius** is the Frankish corn measure. Charlemagne made a new, larger "public modius" in 793/4 (Frankfurt
  c. 4). Its size is argued: about 40 to 68 litres in the modern estimates (?). The game's sack of 60 kg as "a modius
  and a half" puts the modius at 40 kg of wheat, about 52 litres, which is in the range. A check from the same law:
  12 wheat loaves of 2 pounds for a penny is about 9.8 kg of bread a penny, so the 4 d modius gives the baker about
  39 kg of bread. It fits.
- **English measures:** the sester (sextarius), the amber (about 4 bushels (?)), the mitta, the wey for heavy goods.
  The chronicle's "sester of wheat" at 55 and 60 pence cannot be a pint measure; it is probably a horse-load, as
  Henry of Huntingdon understood it a century later (?).
- **Saxony:** the scapilus (scheffel, a bushel) in the Capitulare Saxonicum.
- **Packing:** sacks of hemp or coarse wool; for a sea passage, casks or skins keep it dry. Wet grain heats, sprouts
  and rots within days. Grain in the bottom of an open boat must lie on dunnage (brushwood, hurdles) under hides.

The lesson for the game: against a debt of 480 d, ordinary grain is ballast that pays its way. It is the cargo you
take because the hold is empty on the way home, or because you have heard a word nobody else has.

### 1.7 Tolls, bans and laws

- **One measure.** "Equal measures and just weights" everywhere (Admonitio generalis 789, c. 74); the new public
  modius (Frankfurt 794). In England, Edgar: "one measure and one weight, such as is kept in London and in
  Winchester" (III Edgar 8, c. 960s) (?).
- **Price caps** in 794 and 806, above. They bound sellers, the king's stewards first.
- **No food out of the realm in a famine.** Thionville, December 805 (c. 4): in the present hunger each man is to
  help his own, sell his corn no dearer than the set price, and no food is to be sold outside the empire (?).
- **No buying up the harvest.** Nijmegen 806, c. 17, above. Forestalling is "filthy gain" (turpe lucrum), and a
  priest can refuse you the sacrament for it.
- **Tolls.** The king's tenth at the Frankish toll places falls on grain as on anything else, but abbeys carrying
  their own corn went free by charter. Food bought for a traveller's own need paid no toll (a general rule of the
  capitularies) (?). At London about 1000 there is no corn toll named: only the ship pays (IV Aethelred 2).
- **Tithe.** A tenth of every crop to the church, enforced by the king in Francia from 779 (Herstal c. 7) and in
  England from Aethelstan and Edgar. Tithe barns are a place to buy.

### 2.2 Why it was wanted: the fast

No meat on fast days. By the 9th century that means the forty days of Lent, the four Ember weeks, the eves of the great
feasts, Advent in many places, and every Friday (and for the strict, Wednesday). For monks under the Rule of Benedict
the flesh of four-footed beasts is forbidden all year except to the sick. Fish is the food that is left.

The law stood behind it. Wihtred of Kent (695): whoever gives his household meat in a fast pays for it. Charlemagne's
first Saxon capitulary (c. 782, c. 4): a Saxon who eats meat in the holy forty days "out of contempt for the
Christian faith" shall die, unless a priest judges he did it from need. Every conversion (Saxony 780s, Denmark
960s, Norway and Iceland about 1000, Sweden through the 11th century) makes a new market for fish in February
and March.

### 2.3 Routes, ships and the great change about 1000

- **Before about 1000:** fish is local. Fish bones from the 8th and 9th century wics (Hamwic, Lundenwic, Ipswich,
  Fishergate at York, Dorestad) are mostly eel and river fish, with some herring near the coast.
- **About 1000, the "fish event horizon"** (Barrett, Locker and Roberts 2004): within a generation the bones in
  English towns turn to herring and cod. The towns have grown, the rivers are fished out or dammed by mills, and the
  fast is kept by everyone. Aelfric's fisherman, writing just then, says he cannot catch as many as he could sell.
  (The page was down today; this is the standard account (?).)
- **Dried cod from the Arctic** reached Haithabu in the Viking Age: the cod bones there carry the genetic mark of the
  north-east Arctic stock (Star et al. 2017) (?). So the stockfish road from Halogaland by Kaupang to Hedeby, the same
  road Ohthere sailed, was open before 1000. The mass trade to England and the Rhine comes after 1000 and grows
  through the 11th century (Barrett et al. 2008, isotopes). Egil's Saga has Thorolf send dried fish to England
  about 875, but the saga is 13th century and cannot be leaned on (checked today: that is the earliest mention
  by name).
- **Herring** went inland by river and pack horse as far as it would keep: from the Sound to the Danish halls, from
  East Anglia up the Ouse and the Thames, from the Flemish and Frisian coast up the Rhine and Meuse. Ibrahim ibn
  Yaqub (c. 965) says the people of Schleswig live chiefly on fish, "for it is plentiful there" (?).
- **Ships:** any coaster. The 14 m Skuldelev 3 (c. 1040, 4.5 tons) and the Graveney boat are the type. Stockfish
  came south in the big sea-going knarrs (Skuldelev 1, c. 1030, built in western Norway, about 24 tons).

### 2.5 Units, packing, keeping

- **Herring are counted,** not weighed: by the hundred and the thousand in Domesday, later by the "last" of ten or
  twelve thousand. The gutted, brine-packed barrel herring that keeps a year is a later skill (the 14th century, by
  tradition). In this period herring is dry-salted in heaps or baskets, or smoked, or eaten fresh, and keeps weeks to
  a few months, not a year (?). Barrels were certainly there (the silver-fir wine casks reused as well linings at
  Dorestad and Hedeby prove the cooper), but a "barrel of herring" as a unit is not attested before the 12th
  century (?).
- **Eels:** the stick of 25, ten sticks to the bind (250). Smoked, salted, or carried alive in a wet basket or a
  well-boat.
- **Stockfish:** split or round, dried hard as wood, tied in bundles; a dried cod is 0.5 to 1 kg and has lost four
  fifths of its weight. It keeps for years if dry and is ruined by one wetting.
- **Salt** is the limit. To dry-salt fish takes about a quarter to a third of its weight in salt (?). A herring coast
  without salt has nothing to sell. Bay salt, Zeeland and Frisian peat salt, Droitwich and Luneburg salt all sell
  best in the herring months.

**A Gislinge boat of 660 kg carries:** about 5,000 to 6,000 cured herring (?); or 25 to 30 bundles of stockfish
(the hold is full before the weight is reached); or some 2,000 smoked eels (80 sticks); or six or seven casks of salt
salmon.

### 2.6 Prices and rents

There is no market price for fish in a text before 1066 that I know of. What there is:

- **Rents in herring, 1066 and 1086** (Domesday Book): Dunwich owed the king 60,000 herrings; Beccles 30,000 in 1066
  and 60,000 in 1086 to the abbey of Bury; Sandwich 40,000 a year to the monks of Christ Church; Southease in
  Sussex 38,500; Kessingland 22,000. (Numbers from memory (?); today's page did not give them.)
- **Rents in eels:** Domesday fisheries and mills pay by the thousand; the Fenland abbeys counted eels in tens of
  thousands. Ine's ten hides owe 100 eels and 5 salmon a year.
- **A working guess for the game:** later medieval herring ran at roughly a penny for 20 to 40 fish; at the beach in
  a good autumn, a hundred for a penny (guess). The table's 12 d for 100 kg (about 800 fish) is inside that.

### 2.7 Tolls and laws

- **London, c. 1000** (IV Aethelred 2): "a merchant who came to the bridge with a boat containing fish paid one
  halfpenny as toll, and for a larger ship one penny." The men of Rouen with wine or blubber fish pay six shillings
  for a large ship and a twentieth of the fish (?).
- **Fisheries are property.** A weir belongs to a lord or an abbey and is given by charter. Weirs block rivers;
  quarrels between boatmen and weir owners run through the charters (the Thames, the Severn, the Rhine).
- **Wreck and stranding.** A stranded whale is the king's or the lord's, shared out by custom.
- **Koblenz** on the Rhine and Moselle: the toll list confirmed in 1104, said to be much older, takes its toll in
  kind from named towns, herring and salmon and eels among the payments from the men of the lower Rhine (?).

|---|---|

### 3.2 How it moved

**On the hoof.** Cattle are driven to market in autumn, when they are fat from the summer grass and before the hay
has to feed them. The earliest layers of Ribe, about 705 to 725, are thick with dung, read as a seasonal cattle
market at the head of the Jutland droving road (Feveile 2006) (?). Ireland's roads are named for cattle.

**By water only for short crossings:** over the Rhine mouths, between the Danish isles, across the Channel narrows.
The proof that it could be done at scale is military. In 893 the great army crossed from Boulogne to Kent in 250
ships and "transported themselves over at one time with their horses withal" (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Ingram 1823,
checked today). The Bayeux Tapestry shows horses standing in open ships in 1066. The Oseberg burial of 834 put
fifteen horses, two oxen and dogs with the ship (?).

**In a Gislinge boat** (7.7 m, 660 kg, four men): two small cows at most, hobbled, on a short fair-weather
crossing, and nothing else. More sensibly: eight or ten sheep, or a dozen geese in hampers, or pigs if you must.
Beasts need water every day and fodder (about a tenth of their weight in hay over four days); they foul the boat and
shift when she rolls. A frightened ox can sink her. The game should make livestock a river and ferry cargo with a
day limit, not a sea cargo.

**What does travel is the dead beast:** salt pork and bacon, salt beef, hides, horn, tallow, cheese, butter.

### 3.5 Laws

Livestock is the most law-bound good of the age, because it is the most stolen.

- **Witnesses.** Kent, c. 680: a Kentish man who buys in Lundenwic has two or three honest men or the king's
  town-reeve as witness (Hlothhere and Eadric 16). Edward the Elder (c. 900-924): "that no man buy out of port, but
  have the port-reeve's witness". Aethelstan: "that no man buy any property out of port over twenty pence" and "that
  every marketing be within port" (both checked today, Fordham). Edgar (IV Edgar 3-11, c. 962-3): sworn witnesses in
  every borough and hundred; a man who buys beasts away from home must tell his village what he bought and before
  whom when he comes back, or lose them (?).
- **Warranty.** If a beast is claimed as stolen, the holder names the man he bought from, and he the man before, up
  to three times (team). A merchant with no warrantor and no witness pays as a thief.
- **Hide and head.** Nobody slaughters a beast without two witnesses, and he keeps the hide and the head three
  nights, so a stolen cow can be known (III Aethelred 9, the Wantage code, c. 997) (?).
- **Night trading.** In Francia nobody may trade at night in gold or silver vessels, slaves, gems, horses or beasts,
  "except food and fodder for those on a journey" (Capitulary for the missi, c. 803) (?).
- **Horses out of the realm.** England: "that no man send a horse over sea, unless he wish to give it as a gift"
  (II Aethelstan 18, c. 930) (?). Francia: death for selling a horse to the Northmen (Edict of Pitres 864, c. 25).
- **Sunday.** No Sunday market: Admonitio generalis 789 c. 81; "let no marketing be on Sundays; but if any one do so,
  let him forfeit the goods, and pay thirty shillings" (Aethelstan); among the Danes of England twelve ores (Edward
  and Guthrum 7) (both checked today, Fordham). Aethelstan later withdrew his own ban (IV Aethelstan 2) (?).
- **London toll, c. 1000** (IV Aethelred 2): a hamper of hens pays one hen; a hamper of eggs pays five eggs; the
  women who deal in butter and cheese pay a penny a fortnight before Christmas and another a week before. The
  Emperor's men may buy three live pigs for their ship, and no more (?).

### 3.6 Murrain

A cattle plague is to the livestock trade what a famine is to grain, and it makes a famine the year after, because
the plough stops.

- **809-810:** "so great a pestilence of oxen" on the emperor's march against the Danes that hardly one was left to
  the army, and through all the provinces (Royal Frankish Annals, 810; in the chronicle already). People said
  the Duke of Benevento had sent men to scatter a powder on fields and springs, and men were killed for it (Agobard
  of Lyon).
- **892-896:** England, three years of "the disease of cattle, and most of all of men" (Chronicle 897, checked today).
- **986:** "the great murrain of cattle first came into England" (Chronicle) (?). Ireland and Wales record it too.
- **1042:** "More cattle died this year than any man ever remembered, either from various diseases, or from the
  severity of the weather." **1048:** "very great loss by disease of men and of cattle over all England."
  (Chronicle, Ingram 1823, both checked today.) **1054:** another (?).

After a murrain: oxen, hides next year, butter, cheese and parchment are all dear; in the year itself hides are a
glut and nobody will buy a live beast from an afflicted shire.

### 3.7 Frauds and tests

| Fraud | Test |
|---|---|
| Stolen beasts | Buy in the port before the reeve; know the ear-mark and the brand; get the seller's warrantor's name |
| Age lied about | The teeth; the rings on a cow's horn (one for each calf) |
| A sick beast from a plague shire | Dull coat, running eyes and nose, hot muzzle. Ask where the drove came from and ask a second man |
| A cow sold as in milk or in calf | Milk her yourself; wait a day |
| A horse lame, broken-winded or a biter | Ride it; trot it on hard ground; watch it eat |
| Short weight in salt meat; rusty bacon | Thrust a bone skewer to the bone and smell it |

## 4. Hides, leather and tallow

### 4.1 Where and by whom

Every slaughter makes a hide, so hides come from cattle country and from towns that eat beef: Frisia, Jutland,
Saxony, Ireland, and the wics themselves. Tanning needs oak bark, water and a year in the pit, so it sits on the
edge of a town, down wind. Goat and sheep skins are tawed with alum or dressed with oil for fine work; calf and
sheep skin are limed, scraped and stretched for parchment in the abbeys.

- **The finds:** 16-22 Coppergate at York, 9th to 11th century, with thousands of leather offcuts, shoes, scabbards
  and the tools of the trade; Fishamble Street and Wood Quay in Dublin; Hedeby's shoes and boots in the hundreds;
  Dorestad's harbour leather. The street name Coppergate is the cup-makers' (?), but the pits are tanners' and
  cobblers'.
- **What was made:** Aelfric's shoemaker (c. 1000) says: "I buy hides and skins and prepare them by my craft, and
  make of them shoes of various kinds, slippers, leggings, bottles, reins and trappings, flasks and leather vessels,
  spur straps and halters, bags and purses; and none of you would care to pass the winter without my craft."
  (Colloquy; my own plain rendering of the Latin.)
- **Ship's gear:** Ohthere's Sami paid in "two ship's ropes each 60 ells long made from whale or seal skin"
  (checked today). Hide rope is the best running rigging there is. Sail-cloth and sea-clothes were dressed with
  tallow and oil.
- **Books:** a great Bible takes the skins of a herd. The Codex Amiatinus (Wearmouth-Jarrow, before 716) took over
  five hundred calves (?). After a murrain, no books.

### 4.2 Where wanted, routes

- Raw and salted hides go from the cattle coasts to the tanning towns: Frisia and Jutland to the Rhine and to England;
  Ireland to the Loire and the Severn. An Irish ship with shoes and clothing for sale at Noirmoutier is in the Life
  of St Philibert (7th century event, 9th century text) (?). The big Irish hide trade to Rouen and Poitou for wine is
  written of only in the 12th century; before that it is likely but unproved (?).
- Goat skins, seal skins and walrus hide come down from Norway with the furs.
- Finished leather mostly sells where it is made. Fine cordwain from Cordoba is a rare luxury in the north (?).
- At London about 1000 the Emperor's men are allowed to buy wool that has been unloaded and "melted fat" (tallow),
  which shows tallow as an export from England to the Rhine (IV Aethelred 2.9) (?).

### 4.3 Season, units, the boat, price

- **Season:** hides and tallow are a glut from Martinmas to Yule and scarce by midsummer. Tanned leather has no
  season, but it is a year behind the slaughter.
- **Units:** counted singly and by the "dicker" of ten (a Roman soldiers' count, decuria, that lasts all through the
  Middle Ages; in Domesday the iron of Gloucester is counted in dickers (?)). A green (raw) hide of a small ox weighs
  15 to 25 kg, salted somewhat less, dried hard about 5 to 8 kg (?). Tallow in cakes or casks by the wey.
- **The boat:** 660 kg is about 30 salted hides or about 100 dried ones; the dried ones fill the boat first. Wet
  salted hides stink, drip brine on everything under them and spoil in warm weather within a few weeks.
- **Price:** no direct evidence. An ox at 24 to 30 d and the later rule of thumb that the hide is about a tenth to
  an eighth of the beast gives 2 to 4 d a raw ox hide (guess). The table's 15 d for five (3 d each) agrees. A pair
  of plain shoes should be 1 to 2 d (guess).

### 4.4 Laws, frauds, tests

- The hide-and-head rule (3.5): a hide with no witnesses to the killing is evidence of theft. A merchant buying raw
  hides in England after about 1000 should buy them in the port.
- **Frauds:** hides soaked to weigh more; warble-fly holes and flaying cuts hidden by folding; horse hide sold as ox;
  leather taken from the pit half-tanned (it shows a pale raw streak in the middle of the cut edge).
- **Tests:** unfold every hide and hold it to the light; cut the edge; bend it sharply (good leather does not
  crack); smell it.

## 5. Honey and wax

### 5.1 Where and by whom

Two kinds of beekeeping, side by side:

- **Forest beekeeping** in the east: the Wends, the Prussians, the Poles, the Rus and the Finnic peoples cut hollows
  in standing pines and oaks and owned the trees by marks. Ibn Rusta (c. 903-913) writes of the Slavs' wooden vessels
  for bees and honey; ibn Yaqub (c. 965) calls Mieszko's land rich in grain, meat and honey (?). Wulfstan (c. 890) on
  the Ests by the Vistula mouth: there is very much honey; the king and the richest men drink mare's milk and the
  poor and the slaves drink mead; no ale is brewed among them, for there is mead enough (Old English Orosius; the
  detail was not in today's page (?)).
- **Hive beekeeping** in the west: straw skeps and log hives on the heaths and in the orchards. A fragment of a skep
  with honeybees came out of 10th century Coppergate (Kenward and Hall 1995) (?). The English estate has its
  **beo-ceorl**, the bee-keeper, who "pays five sesters of honey as rent" where the custom is so (Rectitudines
  singularum personarum, c. 1000). Ireland has a whole law tract on bees, the Bechbretha (7th century). Saxony's
  honey is priced in the capitulary of 797 (1.6).

### 5.2 Why wanted

- **Honey** is the only sweetener (sugar is a drug from Egypt). It preserves fruit, dresses wounds, and makes mead.
  It is paid as rent everywhere: Ine's ten hides owe "ten vats of honey" before anything else on the list; Domesday
  towns and Welsh border manors still pay in sesters of honey in 1066.
- **Wax** is the Church's. Altar lights must be wax, not tallow. Every church burns it daily; Candlemas (2 February)
  blesses the year's candles; the paschal candle at Easter is a pillar of it. The English laws make it a tax:
  **light-scot**, a halfpenny's worth of wax from every hide of land three times a year (Easter eve, All Saints,
  Candlemas), in the codes of Aethelred (V, VI, VIII) and Cnut (I Cnut 12) (?). Wax also seals letters, coats
  writing tablets, casts bronze (lost wax), and waxes thread and bowstrings.
- **The pagan north** wants wax too, for casting, and for great men's graves: the candle on the chamber roof of the
  Mammen grave in Jutland (winter 970/71) and the one in the Jelling north mound (?).
- **Conversion makes demand.** Each new church province is a new buyer, for ever: Saxony from the 780s; Hamburg and
  Bremen's missions from the 830s; Denmark from the 960s; Norway and Iceland about 1000; Sweden slowly after.

### 5.3 Routes

- **From the Slavs to the Franks** over the Elbe and the Saale and down the Danube. Raffelstetten (903-906): the
  Slavs who come from the Rugi or the Bohemians to trade on the Danube pay toll on wax (two "massiolae" from a pack
  load, one from a man's load, each worth one scoti), and on slaves and horses (?).
- **By the Baltic:** from Truso and the Wendish ports to Hedeby and so to the Rhine and England; from Ladoga and
  Novgorod west with the furs.
- **From the Rus south:** "furs, wax, honey and slaves" are what Sviatoslav says come from Rus to the Danube (Primary
  Chronicle under 969), and what the Rus bring to Constantinople under the treaties of 911 and 944.
- **Inside the west:** from the Saxon heaths and the Welsh border to the minsters.

### 5.4 Season

- Honey is taken once, between Lammas and Michaelmas (in a skep the bees are killed to take it; forest hives are
  robbed and left). New honey is cheapest in September.
- Wax is rendered from the same combs at the same time; it reaches the western markets in autumn and by the last
  ships before the Baltic closes.
- Wax is dearest from Christmas to Candlemas and again before Easter. Honey is dearest in spring and before Yule
  (mead for the feast).

### 5.5 Units, the boat, prices

- **Honey:** by the sester in England (Domesday's sester of honey is a big one, perhaps 32 ounces by one reckoning and
  several gallons by another: argued (?)); by the siclus or sigla in Saxony; in tubs, vats and stoppered crocks. It
  is heavy: 1.4 kg a litre.
- **Wax:** melted and cast in round cakes or loaves, sold by the pound and the wey. It keeps for ever, does not mind
  wet, and stows flat.
- **The boat:** 660 kg is about 430 litres of honey in tubs (allowing for the wood), or 66 ten-kilo cakes of wax.
- **Prices:**
  - Saxony, 797: a solidus (12 d) for one and a half to two sigla of honey (?). The size of the siglum is unknown.
  - Warwick, 1086, for a custom older than 1066: the sester of honey reckoned at 15 d (Domesday, Warwickshire) (?).
  - England, c. 1000: a halfpenny's worth of wax a hide, three times a year. That is a demand figure, not a price:
    a village of ten hides owes 15 d of wax a year.
  - Wax by the pound: no figure before 1066 that I know. In the 12th and 13th centuries a pound of wax costs 4 to 7 d,
    eight to twelve times the price of tallow (?). For the game, 2 d a pound in a making country and 4 to 6 d at a
    western minster before Candlemas is a fair guess. The table's 40 d for a 10 kg cake (under 2 d a pound) is on
    the low side for the west.

**For the game:** a boat of wax at the table's own price is 2,640 d, forty times a boat of wheat. Wax is the cargo a
small boat should dream of and seldom find, because it comes from the far end of the Baltic and the abbeys have
their own men buying it.

### 5.6 Laws, tolls, frauds, tests

- Raffelstetten's toll in kind on wax (above). The Saint-Denis fair (9 October to Martinmas) names honey with wine
  and madder among what the Saxons and Frisians come for, and the abbey holds all the tolls (the diplomas of 753
  and after) (?).
- Wild bees in a tree belong to the tree's owner or the finder by local custom; the Bechbretha and the later
  Germanic laws spend pages on swarms.
- **Frauds in honey:** thinned with water (it then ferments); thickened with meal; old dark honey under new.
  **Tests:** a drop on the thumbnail should stand, not run; a spoonful in water should sink whole to the bottom;
  it should not froth or smell of yeast.
- **Frauds in wax:** tallow, resin or pitch melted in; a stone, sand or pease cast into the middle of the cake.
  **Tests:** break the cake across (the later Baltic trade made every cake be broken or stamped); it should snap
  clean, not smear; chew a crumb (it should not stick to the teeth or taste of fat); burn a taper (tallow smokes
  and stinks). (Practice written down later (?).)

## 6. Ale

### 6.1 What it was

Ale (ealu, ol, cervisa) is malted grain, water and yeast, flavoured with herbs or nothing, drunk within days.
Everybody drinks it: Aelfric's schoolboy drinks "ale if I have it, water if I have no ale", and wine is for his
elders and betters. It is food as much as drink.

- **Who brews:** every household, the women mostly; every hall; every abbey. The Plan of St Gall (c. 820) draws
  three brewhouses: for the monks, the guests and the pilgrims.
- **Kinds:** Ine's food rent (70.1, c. 690) asks from every ten hides "twelve ambers of Welsh ale, thirty of clear
  ale" beside the honey, loaves, cows or wethers, geese, hens, cheeses, butter, salmon, fodder and eels. Welsh ale
  (bragawd) is strong, sweetened with honey and spiced; "clear" ale is the fine drawn off; "mild" and "old" are in
  later English lists. Mead is honey and water; the Ests drank nothing else.
- **Herbs:** sweet gale (bog myrtle), yarrow, ground ivy, wild rosemary. In the Low Countries and the lower Rhine
  the mixture is **gruit**, and the right to sell it is a lord's: it first appears in the late 10th century
  (checked today), in imperial grants to the bishops of Liege (974) and Utrecht (999) (the two dates from memory (?)).
- **Hops:** Pippin's gift of hop gardens to Saint-Denis in 768 (checked today); Abbot Adalhard's statutes for
  **Corbie** on the Somme, 822, which deal with the gathering of hops for the brew (?); hop gardens at Freising in
  the 850s (?); hop and sweet gale among the plant remains of Haithabu (Behre) (?); and the Graveney boat, with
  hops in her bilge about 950. So hopped ale is real from the 9th century on the Frankish abbey estates and in the
  Danish towns, but most ale has none until long after 1066.

### 6.2 Trade

Ale is not carried far. It will not keep (unhopped ale sours in one to three weeks, quicker in summer and when
shaken in a boat), and it is cheap everywhere. What is traded:

- **Malt:** light, dry, worth half as much again as barley, keeps a year if dry. To Norway and the isles, to towns.
- **Hops** from the 9th century, and **gale and gruit** in the Low Countries: light bulky sacks.
- **Honey** for mead and bragawd.
- **Ale itself** only as ship's stores and over a river or a day's sail to a fair or a feast.

In the north the brewing dates are fixed: Yule, the spring and autumn feasts, weddings, funeral ales, guild
drinkings. The Gulathing law (written 12th century, older in substance) orders every farmer to brew for the feast
days (?). Malt is dear in the weeks before each.

### 6.3 Units, the boat, price

- The amber, the sester, the tun; casks of oak staves with withy hoops; yew-stave buckets and horn cups to serve.
- 660 kg is five casks of about 100 litres with their wood, or 14 sacks of malt.
- Price: none recorded. Reckon from the grain: a modius of barley (2 d) makes roughly 100 to 150 litres of
  ordinary ale (?), so a 100 litre cask is worth 3 to 6 d with the labour and the cask is worth more than the
  ale. The table's 10 d a cask is high, unless the cask is in the price.

### 6.4 Laws and the ale-house

- **The peace of the ale-house.** In the Danish shires of England about 997: breach of the peace in an ale-house is
  paid for with six half-marks if a man is killed, twelve ores if not (III Aethelred 1.2) (?). The ale-house (ealahus)
  is a real word and a real place by 1000.
- **Priests** are told to keep out of them and not to be "ale-poets" (Canons of Edgar 58-59) (?).
- **Edgar's pegs:** William of Malmesbury says Edgar had pegs set in the drinking cups so no man need drink more
  than his measure (12th century tale (?)).
- **Guild ales:** the merchants of Tiel in 1020 "swear and drink together" (Alpert of Metz; in the chronicle).
  The English guilds of Exeter, Cambridge and Abbotsbury (10th-11th century) fine members in sesters of honey and
  malt.
- **Gruit right:** from the late 10th century a brewer in a bishop's town buys his gruit from the bishop's man.
- No ale-taster by that name before the 12th century: do not put one in the game.
- **Frauds:** watering; selling sour ale mixed into new; short measure. **Test:** taste it, from the bottom of the
  cask. For malt: bite it.

### 10.3 Things the goods table cannot yet say

1. **Demand that starts with a conversion.** Fish in Lent and wax for the altar are wanted in Saxony from the 780s,
   Denmark from the 960s, Norway and Iceland from about 1000. `wanted` has no year. Either a `wanted_from` map by
   region, or a rule keyed on the port's `faith` in `ports_793.json`: a Christian port wants fish, wax, wine and oil
   more, and wants fish most in the forty days before Easter.
2. **Easter moves.** `dear` is a list of months; Lent runs from early February to late April depending on the year.
   The clock should compute Easter and make "Lent" a season of its own.
3. **Livestock needs its own rule:** water and fodder aboard each day, a day limit by ship class, a risk card in a
   seaway, and a cap for the Gislinge boat (two cattle, or ten sheep).
4. **Units that are counts,** not weights: the thousand of herring, the stick of eels, the dicker of hides, the long
   hundred (120) in the north. A `count` field beside `kg` would let the words be right.
5. **Famine and murrain as states of a region,** not only one-off shocks: in a famine food is dear, export is
   banned and a price cap binds the honest seller; after a murrain oxen, hides, butter, cheese and parchment move
   together for two years.
6. **Glut goods.** Hides, tallow and salt meat at Martinmas are not merely "cheap": there is more than the market
   can take. `stock` should swell by season, not only the price fall.
