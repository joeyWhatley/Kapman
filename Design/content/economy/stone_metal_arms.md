# Stone, metal and arms: querns, whetstones, soapstone, iron, lead, tin, swords and mail, 793 to 1066

Written 6 Oct 2026 for the content brief (BRIEF.md, BRIEF_2.md). A research page: nothing here is game data yet.

**How to read the marks.** A fact with no mark was checked today on a page I could open, or already stands verified in
the project's own files (`Design/economy_and_trade.md`, `Design/content/ships.md`). **(m)** means I give it from memory
of the named source and could not open that source today; treat it as probably right, to be checked before it ships.
**(?)** means uncertain, argued among scholars, or my own reckoning. Most of the specialist literature (Parkhouse, Kars,
Pohl, Resi, Baug, Williams) is not on open pages, so much of this page carries (m). Several fetches failed outright
today (the Springer page for Baug et al. 2019, Wikipedia's Graveney boat and Eidsborg pages); nothing from those is
claimed as checked. All prices are in silver pennies (d).

## 1. The eight goods at a glance

| Good | Made | Wanted | Why it is wanted | Small boat (660 kg) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hand querns of Mayen lava | The Eifel, behind Andernach on the Rhine | Frisia, England, Denmark, the Baltic towns | Every household grinds its own flour daily; the lava stays rough as it wears and sheds little grit | about 13 to 20 pairs (?) |
| Whetstones | Norway: Trondelag (dark), Telemark (pale) | Everyone who owns a blade | No scythe, sickle, knife or sword works without one | about 60 bundles of 20 |
| Soapstone pots | Norway, west Sweden, Shetland after the Norse settle | Norse households everywhere; Denmark, Hedeby; Iceland | A pot that sits in the fire, heats evenly, does not crack | 80 to 150 pots by weight; fewer by room |
| Bar iron | Forest country: the Veluwe, the Rhine hills, Svealand, inland Norway, the Forest of Dean | Treeless and oreless coasts: Frisia, Jutland, the isles, Iceland | Tools, nails, rivets, ploughshares | about 60 bundles of ten bars |
| Lead | The English Peak; Melle in Poitou; the Harz after 968 | Church builders in Francia and England; salt boilers | Roofs, gutters, salt pans, weights | 26 pigs of 25 kg |
| Tin | Cornwall and Dartmoor, and nowhere else in the west | Bell founders, bronze casters, tinners of iron | Bells, bronze, bright coatings | about 130 small ingots; nobody has that much |
| Swords | The Rhineland | The whole north and east | The best blades in the known world | limited by silver, not weight |
| Mail | The Frankish realm | Every lord's following | A fortune worn on the back | limited by silver and by the law |

The first five are the opposite of the last three. Stone, iron and lead are heavy, cheap by weight, lawful and safe:
the boat is full long before the purse is empty. Swords and mail are light, dear and forbidden: the purse is empty
long before the boat is full, and the load can cost the merchant everything he carries.

## 2. Querns (and millstones)

**Where and by whom.** The stone is a grey-black bubbly basalt lava from the flows of the Bellerberg volcano at Mayen
in the Eifel, quarried without a break since before the Romans. In Roman times about 600 men worked the Mayen
quarry field and the stones were shipped from the Rhine harbour of Andernach as far as Britain. Mayen millstones are
recorded from the Norse trading town of Haithabu (the game's Sliesthorp). In our period the quarrymen were small
crews of free or dependent stonecutters working open pits; who held the lordship over the pits before the 11th
century is not known (?). The stones went about 25 km by cart or sledge to Andernach (?) and then down the Rhine.

**Roughouts, not finished stones.** The quarry roughed the stone to a thick disc and the buyer's town finished it:
at Dorestad specialist stonecutters dressed the roughouts on the spot, and thousands of fragments were found there
(the project's `dorestad_c800_notes.md`, after Kars 1980 and Van Es and Verwers). Unfinished stones are also known
from Hedeby, Ribe and London (m: Parkhouse 1997; Schoen 1995 for Hedeby). This matters to the game: a roughout is
cheaper, heavier, and worth more work at the far end.

**Where it was wanted.** Nearly every excavated settlement from the Rhine mouth to Jutland has Mayen lava: the
Frisian terps, Dorestad, Medemblik, Ribe, Hedeby, and in England the wics (Hamwic, Ipswich, London, York) and many
farms behind them (m: Parkhouse 1997). It thins out sharply north of Denmark: Kaupang and Birka have little, because
Norway and Sweden ground on local stone (?). England had its own quern stones (Millstone Grit, Greensand,
Hertfordshire puddingstone) and still bought lava, which says how much better it was thought to be.

**The Graveney boat.** A clinker cargo boat of about 13.6 m, built about 895 and abandoned in a Kentish creek in the
10th century (ships.md). Her hold had fragments of unfinished lava querns and hop remains (m: Fenwick 1978). She is
the proof that a coaster of the Thames mouth carried Rhenish querns.

**The black stones of 796.** Charlemagne's letter to Offa (in Alcuin's letters, 796) answers a request for "black
stones" (petrae nigrae): Offa is to send a man to say what kind he wants, and the king will help with the carriage;
in turn he complains that English cloaks have grown short. The project reads these as Mayen querns. The other
reading, now the stronger among scholars, is dark marble or porphyry columns for a church (m: Peacock 1997,
"Charlemagne's black stones", Antiquity 71). Keep the story; keep the (?).

**Routes and ships.** Andernach, Cologne, the lower Rhine, Dorestad (to about 850); from there by Frisian ships up the
coast inside the islands to the Ems, Weser and Elbe, to Ribe, and over the Jutland neck to Hedeby; and across to the
Thames, Ipswich and the Humber. After Dorestad the same stones pass Deventer, Tiel and Utrecht (?). On the river they
ride in barges; at sea they are the ballast of a wine and pottery cargo, laid flat on the floor timbers. No quern
wreck of the period is securely published on any page I could open; a Rhine barge with a quern cargo is reported near
Xanten (?).

**Size and weight (my reckoning) (?).** A hand quern is two discs of 40 to 50 cm. A roughout 45 cm across and 8 cm
thick, at a density near 2.2 for bubbly lava, is about 28 kg; dressed, a stone is 12 to 20 kg. A pair is therefore
25 to 40 kg dressed, 45 to 55 kg rough. A small boat's 660 kg is 13 pairs rough or 20 dressed, all of it stowed on
the bottom. A water-mill stone of the time was not the giant of later mills: 70 to 90 cm across and 100 to 200 kg
(?). One or two would fill the boat's weight and need a crane or a beach and six men to shift.

**Season.** Stone keeps, so there is no harvest. What turns is the river and the buyer: the Rhine carries heavy
loads best from March to early summer; late-summer low water strands stone boats; ice can close the lower river
after Christmas. Buyers want a quern most between harvest and winter, when the new grain is to be ground (?, my
inference). Cheap at Dorestad in April to June, dear in England and Denmark in September to November.

**Price.** No penny price survives from the Rhine or the North Sea in these centuries. The only figure near the
period is Welsh: the law books give "a quern, four pence" among household goods (m: the laws of Hywel Dda, 10th
century in name, 13th-century manuscripts; Aneurin Owen's translation of 1841 is in the public domain). The game's
12 d a pair, sold far from the quarry, is a guess of the right order.

**Frauds and tests.** Local stone sold as "the black stone": true Mayen lava is dark grey, full of small holes, and
glitters with tiny black crystals; sandstone is neither. A cracked stone: stand it on edge and strike it; a sound
stone rings and a flawed one answers dull. A roughout too thin to dress. The test is the eye and the knuckle.

**How it changed.**
- 793 to about 850: the Dorestad years. Roughouts down the Rhine, finished at Dorestad, carried out by Frisians.
- About 850 to 950: Dorestad is gone (last mentioned 863). The stones still come; other river towns handle them,
  and Hedeby finishes its own.
- From about 950: the garnet mica schist quarries of Hyllestad in Sogn, western Norway, begin to send out
  quernstones (distribution starts around 950 and grows in the Middle Ages). They reach all Norway, Sweden,
  Denmark, northern Germany and the Atlantic islands; about 400 quarries are known, with two loading harbours whose
  sea floor is strewn with querns and ballast, and several wrecked quern cargoes lie along the Norwegian coast. In
  Scandinavia the Rhenish stone now has a rival that comes by Norse ships.
- To 1066: England and the Low Countries stay with Mayen lava; Denmark uses both.

## 3. Whetstones

**Where and by whom.** Two Norwegian stones served the whole North Sea (m: Baug, Skre, Heldal and Jansen 2019, "The
Beginning of the Viking Age in the West", Journal of Maritime Archaeology 14; Resi 1990 for Hedeby):
- a dark, blue-grey, very fine stone from Mostadmarka in Trondelag, well north of the Dovre mountains. It is at
  Ribe from the first half of the 8th century (m), which is the reason that paper gives for dating regular sailing
  between the far north and Denmark before the first raids;
- a pale grey, slightly glittering schist from Eidsborg in upper Telemark, at Kaupang from about 800 (m) and the
  common hone of Hedeby, the Danelaw towns and later all northern Europe.

The quarrymen were upland farmers working in the slack of the year. For later centuries it is recorded that the
Eidsborg stone was sledged down on winter snow to Lake Bandak and boated down the lakes to the fjord head at Skien;
for our period that is a fair inference, not a record (?).

**The Klastad ship.** A clinker cargo ship, oak with pine top strakes, keel 16.25 m, 20 to 22 m long and 4.5 m
broad, tree-ring dated to 998, wrecked near Kaupang in Vestfold with a cargo of whetstones from Eidsborg. She is
shown at the Slottsfjell museum in Tonsberg. She proves the bulk trade by sea: a whole shipload of nothing else.

**Where it was wanted and why.** Everywhere, by everyone, always. A mower whets his scythe every few minutes; a
woman carries a small hone on her belt; a warrior is buried with one. Whetstones wear away and are lost, so the same
house buys again in a few years, unlike a quern. Lands without good fine stone (Frisia, Jutland, the Danish isles,
East Anglia) took Norwegian stone. In England Norwegian hones become common in York, Lincoln and London only with
the Danish settlement, from the late 9th century (m: Ellis 1969; the Coppergate finds); before that the English
whetted on local sandstones and Kentish rag.

**Units and packing.** Shipped as rough-split bars, a hand to a forearm long, to be cut to length by the seller;
bundles tied with bast. A finished belt hone is 50 to 300 g; a bar blank 0.5 to 1.5 kg (?). The game's bundle of 20
at 10 kg is fair. 660 kg is about 65 bundles, some 1,300 hones: more than a small market buys in a season.

**Season.** Down from the hills in late winter, at the coast in spring, in the southern markets by early summer.
Dearest just before haymaking and harvest, from Saint John's day to the Assumption (late June to mid August), when
every farm needs its edges (?, inference). Cheap in autumn when the Norse ships are selling up to go home.

**Price.** None survives. A cheap good: the profit is in the number.

**Frauds and tests.** A bar with a quartz vein or a cross-crack that will break in use; coarse local stone sold as
northern stone. The test: wet it and draw a knife along it; a good hone bites evenly and leaves a grey slurry.
Colour tells the two kinds apart, and smiths were said to want the dark stone for the last edge (?).

**A thing to use.** In the Norse stories Odin throws a whetstone among nine mowing thralls and they kill each other
scrambling for it, and Thor carries a piece of the giant Hrungnir's hone in his skull, which is why a hone must
never be thrown across a floor (Snorri, Skaldskaparmal, 13th century, older tales). A Northman who hands you a
whetstone hands it; he does not toss it.

## 4. Soapstone

**Where and by whom.** Soft talc rock that can be carved with a knife. Norway had quarries from Agder round to
Trondelag and in the east country by the Oslo fjord; there were more in what is now western Sweden. During the
Viking Age soapstone was the main cooking-vessel material in Norway: the Norse hewed pots straight out of the rock
face, shaped them and sold them at home and abroad, and Norway made almost no pottery of its own. The carvers were
farmers on whose land the rock lay. In Shetland (Catpund at Cunningsburgh, and on Unst) the Norse settlers opened
quarries of their own after they came, from the mid 9th century (m); Shetland vessels were used for fish and dairy
fats.

**Where it was wanted.** First by the Norse themselves, wherever they went: the Atlantic islands, Iceland from 874
(which has none), Dublin, York. Then by their neighbours: Jutland and Hedeby took it from the 9th century, and the
Hedeby digs produced soapstone sherds by the thousand (m: Resi 1979). South and west of the Danes it barely sold:
the Rhineland and England had good cheap pottery. At Dorestad soapstone appears only after 850 and thinly (the
project's Dorestad notes).

**Other uses.** Lamps, spindle whorls, loom and net weights, and above all moulds for casting silver ingots and
bronze: every broken pot was cut up for these. A smith buys sherds.

**Units and packing.** Finished bowls, round-bottomed, 20 to 45 cm across, walls a finger thick, 2 to 8 kg, often
with an iron bail. They nest badly and crack if they knock; packed in hay or moss between the ribs. By weight a
small boat holds over a hundred; by room and by sense, sixty (?).

**Season.** Quarried between the spring and autumn farm work; sold from the boats in summer. No dear season, but a
port is bare of them from October until the first ships of May.

**Price.** None survives. More than a clay pot, far less than an iron or bronze cauldron.

**Frauds and tests.** A hairline crack hidden with fat and soot; a pot mended with iron clamps sold as whole (a
mended pot is honest goods at half the price). Fill it with water and watch the outside; tap it and listen.

**How it changed.** 793: a Norwegian thing, scarcely seen south of the Skagerrak. 9th and 10th centuries: common at
Hedeby and in Jutland, carried with the whetstones; the Norse of the west take it to the isles and Ireland. 11th
century: Danish demand falls as better local and Slav-style pottery spreads (?), while Norway and the islands keep
to stone for centuries more.

## 5. Iron

**Where and by whom.** All iron of the period is bloomery iron, smelted from bog ore or weathered ore with charcoal
in small clay furnaces, a few kilos at a time, by farmers and estate smiths. It was made wherever ore and forest
met:
- the Veluwe, east of Dorestad, on ironstone nodules: one of the largest iron districts of north-west Europe
  between the 7th and 10th centuries, ending when the forest gave out (m: Joosten 2004);
- the Rhine hill country (Siegerland, the Eifel, the Lahn) and Saxony;
- Svealand's forest belt and Jamtland; inland Norway (upper Telemark, Hallingdal, Valdres, Trondelag): the
  mountain valleys smelted far more than they used and sent it to the coast (m);
- in England the Forest of Dean, the Weald and Northamptonshire. Domesday Book records that before 1066 the city
  of Gloucester paid the king 36 dickers of iron and 100 iron rods fit for making nails for the king's ships (m:
  Domesday, Gloucestershire); a dicker is ten bars. Several manors paid rent in blooms of iron (m).

**Where it was wanted.** The coasts with no ore and no wood: the Frisian terp country, the Danish isles, Gotland's
smiths (who worked far more than the island smelted), Iceland, and every shipyard. A clinker boat is riveted
plank to plank; a boat the size of the player's needs several hundred rivets and roves, a longship several
thousand (?).

**Form and packing.** Iron travelled as bars of a known shape, so the shape vouched for the metal: in Scandinavia
long thin bars and blade-shaped blanks, hoarded in bundles of a dozen or more; a bar drawn out thin at one end
shows it can be forged without cracking (m). Bars are 0.1 to 1 kg. The English reckoned them by the dicker of ten.
The game's bundle of ten bars at 10 kg sits well with this. 660 kg is 66 bundles.

**The Mastermyr chest.** A travelling smith's oak chest, 90 by 26 by 24 cm, lost in a Gotland bog about the year
1000 and found in 1936: more than 200 tools and pieces, with hammers, tongs, saws, augers, files, three padlocks
and a steelyard, and beside it three bronze cauldrons, three bells and an iron fire-grid. It is the largest find of
tools of the age in Europe and the best picture of what "iron tools" means as a cargo.

**Season.** Ore is dug and roasted and charcoal burnt after the harvest; the furnaces run in autumn and early
winter; the bars come out of the forest by sledge over snow and ice and are sold at the late-winter markets (the
Svear's Disting at Uppsala in February or March) (m for the pattern, ? for the dates). Iron is cheapest at the
northern ports in spring and reaches the southern ones by midsummer. Dear in late summer before the new smelt, and
dear wherever a fleet is building.

**Price.** No penny price for bar iron survives. Worked iron has a few (m, Welsh laws as above): a broad axe 4 d, a
fuel axe 2 d, a coulter 4 d. Iron was cheap enough for every farm to own some and dear enough that nobody threw a
nail away.

**Frauds and tests.** Brittle iron: bog ore rich in phosphorus gives metal that works hot and snaps cold. Slaggy
bars with a sound skin. The test is to bend a bar cold across the knee and to look at a broken end: grey and fine is
good, bright and coarse is brittle. A buyer may ask for one bar of the bundle to be broken.

**How it changed.** The Veluwe fades in the 10th century. Norwegian and Swedish output grows through the whole
period. From 968 the Harz adds metal of other kinds (below). In England the king's fleets of 1008 and after pull
iron to the yards. The blast furnace and Swedish "osmund" iron are later (osmund first in writing in 1203): not in
this game.

## 6. Lead

**Where and by whom.**
- The Peak of Derbyshire. Many lead mines there in Anglo-Saxon times were owned by Repton abbey, and three are
  named in Wirksworth's Domesday entry. A charter of 835, the oldest of the Peak, has the abbess granting land at
  Wirksworth to Duke Humbert; its condition (m: Sawyer 1624) is that he pay lead worth 300 shillings each year to
  Christ Church, Canterbury. The Danes wintered at Repton in 873-4 and the abbey ended; the mines went on under new
  lords. In 1086 lead mining and smelting was an established industry, with the mines at Wirksworth and Bakewell in
  Domesday Book; three royal manors of the Peak had paid, before 1066, five cartloads of lead of fifty slabs each
  among their dues (m: Domesday, Derbyshire).
- Melle in Poitou, the Carolingian silver mine: lead is what is left when the silver is taken out (m).
- The Rammelsberg in the Harz: first recorded about 968, when Widukind of Corvey says Otto the Great had silver
  veins opened; the mine gave copper first, then lead and silver. Goslar is first named in 979.
- Mendip and Flintshire are likely but poorly attested in these centuries (?).

**Where it was wanted and why.** Church roofs, gutters and pipes: Bede has the bishop of Lindisfarne cover his
church in sheets of lead. In 852 abbot Lupus of Ferrieres wrote to king Aethelwulf of Wessex and to his secretary
Felix asking for lead to roof his church, to be brought across to Etaples, by Quentovic, where his men would meet
it (m: Lupus, Letters; Levillain's edition nos. 84-85). That is the one named cross-Channel lead cargo of the age,
and it is abbey to king, not market trade. Lead also made the boiling pans of the English salt works at Droitwich
and in Cheshire (m), trade weights, net sinkers, the models for casting brooches at Ribe and Hedeby, and the
sounding lead (the project's seasons file).

**Units and packing.** Cast slabs or pigs. If the Domesday cartload was near the later fother of about a tonne,
fifty slabs to the load makes a slab about 20 kg (?), a man's lift. The game's pig of 25 kg is fair. 660 kg is 26
pigs, which fill one row along the keel and leave the boat looking empty. A boat loaded with lead is stiff and
quick in her roll and hard on her fastenings, and if she fills she sinks at once: no wreckage, no salvage.

**Season.** Later practice was to smelt on windy hill brows in the gales of autumn and winter and cart the lead
out when the roads dried (?, later medieval evidence). Assume lead at the Humber and Trent landings from late
spring. Demand has no season, but a church building is a single great order: dear for one year in one place.

**Price.** 835 gives a value (300 shillings a year) and no weight. No price by weight survives before the 12th
century. The game's 15 d a pig is a guess.

**Frauds and tests.** A stone, sand or dross cast into the middle of a pig; short weight. Lead is sold on the scale
and a doubtful pig is cut through with an axe: sound lead is bright and even to the heart.

**How it changed.** 793 to 870s: church lead, moving by gift and order between abbeys and kings. 874 on: the Peak
is under Danish lords, and its lead reaches York and the Trent ports. From 968: Harz lead comes down the Weser and
Elbe (?). By 1066 lead is an ordinary English royal due.

## 7. Tin

**Where and by whom.** Cornwall and Dartmoor, from stream gravels, washed out by farmers with running water. Tin
slag at Week Ford on Dartmoor is dated 570 to 890, and valley sediments on the Erme show tin working peaking between
the 4th and 7th centuries. After that the evidence is thin for three hundred years: Domesday Book has no record of
tin mining at all (possibly because the right was the Crown's), and the great Dartmoor boom comes in the 12th
century. The Trewhiddle hoard, buried about 868, was found in an old tin streamwork near St Austell (m). The tin of
the German Ore Mountains is not worked before the 12th century (m). So in this game's years tin is scarce, Cornish,
and small.

**Who ruled it.** The Cornish (the West Welsh) under their own kings until Ecgberht's victory at Hingston Down in
838; under the West Saxon kings after, with the Tamar as the border from Athelstan's time (m).

**Where it was wanted and why.** Bell founders: a bell is about four parts copper to one of tin, and every church
from Ireland to Saxony wanted one. Bronze casters on the Meuse and Rhine. Tinners of iron: spurs, strap-ends and
harness were coated bright. The tin foil on Tating jugs. Cheap pewter brooches in 10th and 11th century London (m).
Note Aelfric's Colloquy, written about 1000: the English merchant says he brings home from over the sea purple and
silk, gems and gold, wine and oil, ivory, brass, copper and tin, sulphur and glass. An English schoolmaster thought
of tin as something a merchant brings in, which shows how little of Cornwall's reached the English towns.

**Units and packing.** Small bun-shaped or bar ingots of a few kilos, in a sack or a hide. The game's 5 kg bar is
fair. A small boat could carry the whole yearly trade of a port.

**Season.** Streaming needs water: winter and spring. Metal comes out to the ports (Exeter, the Hamwic road) from
Whitsun on (?, inference from later practice).

**Price.** None survives before 1156.

**Frauds and tests.** Tin let down with lead. Bend a bar by the ear: pure tin crackles (the "cry" of tin) and leaded
tin is silent; lead also marks a pale cloth grey and tin does not.

**How it changed.** Almost invisible to 950; a little more with the 10th-century church reform and its bells; the
real trade begins after this game ends.

## 8. Swords

**Where and by whom.** The Frankish Rhineland, in workshops whose masters signed their work. 167 blades inlaid
+VLFBERH+T are known, of the 9th to 11th centuries; the name's form is Frankish and points to the Rhineland. A hilt
found in the Weser has lead traced to the Taunus hills, and an abbey workshop (Fulda or Lorsch) has been proposed
(?). INGELRII blades follow from the 10th century (m). The blade was the export; many were hilted in the north in
the local fashion.

**The metal.** The older blades have a pattern-welded core with hard edges welded on. Pattern welding went out of
use in the 9th century as better steel came in. Some Ulfberht blades are of a very clean high-carbon steel that may
have come from Central Asia; this is argued. The spellings vary, and a class of misspelt blades of ordinary metal
is read as imitation: the first forged trademark in northern Europe (m: Williams 2009).

**Where it was wanted.** Of the 167: Norway 44, Finland 14 to 31, Sweden 17, Germany 13, Russia 11, Estonia 9,
Latvia, Poland and Ukraine 7 each, Britain 4, Denmark 3, the Netherlands 3, Iceland 2, Ireland 2. (Norway's count
reflects heathen burial with weapons as much as trade: Christians were buried without.) In the east: Ibn
Khurradadhbih (c. 870) has the Rus bring beaver, black fox and swords south; Ibn Fadlan (922) saw the Rus on the
Volga each with a sword "of the Frankish type"; after the Rus raid on Bardha'a in 943 the Muslims opened their
graves for the swords (m: Miskawayh). In 869 Saracens ransomed an archbishop of Arles for silver, cloaks, slaves
and 150 swords (m: Annals of Saint-Bertin).

**The law.** See section 10. In short: mail could not leave the realm from 779; arms and mail could not be sold to
Slavs and Avars from 805; churchmen could not sell them to outsiders from 811; selling to the Northmen was death in
the western kingdom from 864. A sword sold to an Englishman or a Frisian in 793 broke no law that survives.

**Price.**
- Ripuarian law: a sword with scabbard 7 solidi, without 3 (in the project's price table). At the game's 12 d to
  the solidus, 84 d and 36 d. The scabbard and fittings are more than half the value.
- Welsh law (m): a rough-ground sword 12 d, a dark-bladed one 16 d, a white-bladed one 24 d. The plain man's sword.
- The will of ealdorman Aelfgar, about 950 (m): the sword king Edmund gave him, worth 120 mancuses of gold (3,600
  d), with four pounds of silver on the sheath. The lord's sword.
- Laxdaela saga (13th century, of the 10th): a sword worth half a mark of gold, sixteen milk cows.
So a sword runs from a shilling to fifteen pounds. One good can not carry that; see section 13.

**Units and packing.** A blade is 1 to 1.5 kg and under a metre. Bare blades travel greased and rolled in cloth,
a dozen to a bundle; the bundle looks like any bale. That is the whole of the smuggler's art.

**Season.** No making season. Dear in spring, when hosts muster (the Frankish assembly in May) and ships fit out;
cheap after a defeat, when the field has been picked over.

**Frauds and tests.** A false name on common iron. Notker of St Gall, writing in the 880s, tells how king Louis
tested the swords the Northmen's envoys brought: he bent each from point to hilt; one snapped, the other sprang back
straight (Notker, Deeds of Charles, book II; Grant's translation of 1907 is in the public domain). Bend it, let it
go, sight along it; flick it and listen for a clear ring; look for the welded pattern in the fuller under breath.

**How it changed.** 793: pattern-welded blades, unsigned. From about 850: signed blades of plain steel. 10th
century: the east and north are full of them, true and false. 11th century: longer, tapering blades, INGELRII and
pious inscriptions; England and Scandinavia are Christian kingdoms that arm by royal order.

## 9. Mail

**What it is.** A shirt of some 20,000 to 30,000 iron rings, each riveted or welded shut, 8 to 12 kg: weeks of a
specialist's work on top of the wire. Frankish "brunia", English "byrne", Norse "brynja". Made on the estates of
kings, bishops and abbots for their own mounted men, not for a market.

**Who had it.** In the Ripuarian list a byrnie is 12 solidi, a helmet 6, greaves 6: the mail shirt alone is worth
six oxen or two stallions. Charlemagne's order of 805 expects a byrnie of any man holding twelve farms (m). Otto
II's summons of 981 lists about 2,000 mailed horsemen owed by the German bishops, abbots and counts (m: Indiculus
loricatorum). In England the Chronicle for 1008 says the king ordered ships built over all England, and a helmet
and a mail shirt from every eight hides; Cnut's law fixes an earl's death-due at four helmets, four mail shirts and
four swords among other things, a king's thegn's at one helmet, one mail shirt and two swords (m: II Cnut 71).
Among the Northmen it was rarer still: one near-complete shirt of the age is known from all Scandinavia, in the
Gjermundbu grave in Norway, about 970 (m).

**The law.** The one good named in every ban from 779. See section 10.

**Price.** 12 solidi (144 d at the game's rate) in the old law; nothing later, because it was not sold.

**Frauds and tests.** Rings merely bent shut, not riveted; rust under grease; a shirt patched from three. Pull two
rings apart with the fingers; a riveted ring will not open.

**How it changed.** 793: a Frankish lord's thing. 9th century: taken as loot and ransom more than bought. 10th and
11th centuries: every king's household wears it, and kings order it made at home. Demand moves from the heathen
north (smuggled) to Christian kings (ordered, lawful).

## 10. Tolls, bans and laws

| Year | Law | What it says | Standing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 779 | Herstal, c. 20 | No mail shirt to be sold outside the realm | in `trade_rules.json`; chapter text (m) |
| 803 (?) | Instructions to the king's envoys | No mail to be given or sold to merchants | (?) |
| 805 | Thionville (Diedenhofen), c. 7 | Merchants going to the Slavs and Avars may go only as far as nine named posts, each under a named officer: Bardowick, "Schezla", Magdeburg, Erfurt, Hallstadt, Forchheim, Premberg, Regensburg, Lorch. They may not take arms or mail to sell. Whoever is caught loses his whole load: half to the palace, half shared between the officer and the finder (the split is m) | the list and the ban checked today |
| 811 | Boulogne, c. 10 | No bishop, abbot or abbess may give or sell a mail shirt or sword to an outsider, only to their own men | checked in outline today |
| 864 | Pitres, c. 25 | Whoever gives or sells mail, arms or horses to the Northmen, for ransom or any price, dies as a traitor | Charles the Bald's kingdom only |

Three things the game should take from this. First, the bans are about **mail and about named enemies**, not about
all iron: a Frisian could lawfully carry axes, knives and bar iron anywhere. Second, the ban of 805 is enforced at
**named posts by named men** and its penalty is the **whole cargo**, which is exactly a card. Third, Pitres binds
the western kingdom (Neustria, Aquitaine, the Seine and Loire); in 864 the Rhineland and Frisia were under other
kings.

**Tolls.** The Frankish tenth at Dorestad and Quentovic falls on stone and metal like anything else (the project's
table). Nothing in the London toll list of about 1000 names stone or metal; ships pay by size (a small boat a
halfpenny). Nothing survives on tolls at Mayen or Andernach. In the north the king's man took his due in kind; for
a whetstone cargo that is a bundle or two.

**Lordship.** Ore, stone and lead belonged to whoever held the land: an abbey (Repton), a king (the Harz, the
English Peak after 874), a farmer (Norwegian soapstone). There were no mining laws, no miners' courts and no
stannaries yet.

## 14. What `goods_793.json` and `trade_rules.json` lack or have wrong

### Wrong, or probably wrong

1. **`lead`, note: "Charlemagne asked Offa for lead."** I know no source for this. The letter of 796 is about black
   stones and cloaks. The attested request for English lead is Lupus of Ferrieres to king Aethelwulf, 852 (m).
   Change the note; `src` should not rest on it.
2. **`quernstones`, kg 90 a pair.** Too heavy for hand querns: 25 to 40 kg dressed, 45 to 55 rough (my reckoning,
   ?). At 90 kg the player carries 7 pairs; at 45, 14. This changes whether querns are worth a voyage.
3. **`millstones`, kg 500.** A mill stone of the period is nearer 100 to 200 kg (?). 500 kg is a later stone, and
   one of them is three quarters of the boat.
4. **The `arms` law is wider than the laws.** `trade_rules.json` gives the Frankish zone "no export" of arms from
   779. Herstal bans only mail. Arms as such are banned to Slavs and Avars (805) and from churchmen to outsiders
   (811). As written, selling a spearhead to an Englishman in 793 is a crime; it was not. Swords, spearheads, axes
   and helmets should not carry `law: arms` before 805, and then only toward the named peoples.
5. **Pitres 864 reaches too far.** It is applied to the whole `frankish` zone, which includes the Rhine delta,
   Frisia, the Rhineland and Saxony. In 864 those were not Charles the Bald's. It should bind `neustria`, `loire`,
   `aquitaine`, `flanders` (?). The same for the `horses` law.
6. **Every arms law has `to: 0`.** They run to 1066 unchanged. After 911 the Northmen of Rouen are the king's own
   men; after 965 or so the Danes have a Christian king. The bans should lapse or change.
7. **`soapstone`, made in `shetland` from year 0.** Norse quarrying in Shetland starts with Norse settlement, about
   850 (m). The good has one `from` for all regions, so this can not be said in the present format.
8. **`soapstone`, wanted in `frisia` from year 0.** At Dorestad it appears only after 850 (the project's own notes).
9. **`copper`, made in `saxony` from year 0**, while its own note says the Rammelsberg opens in 968.
10. **`sword_blades`, from 800.** The signed blades are 9th to 11th century; the earliest are usually put near 850
    (?). 800 is early.
11. **`swords`, made in `rhine_delta` 1.** I know no evidence for sword making in the delta. Dorestad sold them on.
12. **`whetstones`, made in `norway_west` 1.** The two attested sources are Trondelag (no region of its own;
    nearest `norway_north`) and Telemark (reached through `vestfold`). Western Norway is not one of them.
13. **`swords` value 84 d.** This is 7 Ripuarian solidi at 12 d. The project's own table warns the text is in gold
    solidi of 40 d. Either way one figure can not stand for a 12 d rough blade and a 3,600 d lord's sword.
14. **`iron_tools`, unit "crate".** Not a word or a thing of the time: a chest, or a bundle.
15. **Tolls.** `english` toll 0.05 by value: the only English tariff (London, c. 1000) charges by the ship, a
    halfpenny for a small boat. `weight` zone toll 0: kings at Hedeby and Birka took dues (the note itself says "to
    come").

### Lacking

16. **No season on any of these goods.** `season`, `cheap` and `dear` are empty for all sixteen stone, metal and
    arms rows. Section 11 gives a first filling: querns cheap 4-6, dear 9-11; whetstones dear 6-8, cheap 9-10; bar
    iron cheap 3-5, dear 8-9; swords dear 3-5; tin cheap 5-7.
17. **No rival quern.** Hyllestad stones from about 950, made in `norway_west`, wanted across Scandinavia. Needs a
    second good, or a way to date a `made` region.
18. **No rough and dressed querns.** The Dorestad trade was roughouts finished at the port. One good hides the one
    piece of work a player could pay for (a stonecutter as a town work or an act).
19. **No dark and pale whetstone.** One good covers both. At the least the note should name Mostadmarka, and
    `from` should let the pale stone begin about 800.
20. **Regions missing from `wanted`.** Querns: `rhine_delta`, `zeeland`, `flanders`, `northumbria`. Whetstones:
    `danish_isles`, `gotland`, `northumbria` (after 866). Soapstone: `northumbria` and `ireland` after the Norse
    come, `danish_isles`. Bar iron: `gotland`, `iceland`, `danish_isles`, `shetland`. Lead: the English regions
    themselves (salt pans, churches), `flanders`. Tin: `meuse`, `england_south`, `ireland` (bells) (?). Swords,
    byrnies, helmets: the English regions, strongly from 1008.
21. **Regions missing from `made`.** Bar iron: `mercia_west` (Forest of Dean), `england_south` (the Weald),
    `rhineland`. Lead: `aquitaine` (Melle), `saxony` from 968; and the Peak has no region (it is in neither
    `mercia_west` nor `northumbria` as described).
22. **Tin can not be bought.** `cornwall` has no port in `ports_793.json`. Give `england_south` a made 0.5 (it
    comes overland to Hamwic and Exeter), or add a port.
23. **No sources on most rows.** `bar_iron`, `iron_tools`, `rivets`, `tin`, `millstones`, `axes` have empty `src`;
    `quernstones` rests on "Kirk and others", which is not a reference. Add: Parkhouse 1997; Kars 1980; Baug and
    others 2019; Resi 1979 and 1990; Joosten 2004; Williams 2009; Domesday Book (Gloucester, Derbyshire); the
    Wirksworth charter of 835; Lupus's letters; Aelfric's Colloquy; the Chronicle for 1008; II Cnut 71.
24. **No risk on the heavy goods.** `risk` is empty for querns, millstones, lead and iron. A dense cargo makes a
    small boat stiff and sinks her at once if she fills; soapstone breaks. The arms rows have risk; these have none.
25. **No tests and no frauds.** The town acts `sample` and `haggle` exist, but no good says what can be false about
    it. A field such as `fraud` and `test` per good would carry sections 2 to 9: the false Ulfberht, leaded tin, the
    stone in the lead pig, brittle iron, the cracked pot.
26. **Laws lacking in `trade_rules.json`.** Boulogne 811; the nine posts and the penalty of 805 (the whole load);
    the English arming order of 1008 as a demand shock; lordship over mines.
27. **A glut never ends for a thing that lasts.** `glut_fade` is one number for all goods. A market that has been
    sold forty querns does not want another for years; one sold forty bundles of whetstones wants more next
    summer. A per-good fade (or a "lasts" value) would make stone play differently from grain.
28. **Mail as a market good at all.** Mail was made to order for lords and moved as gift, loot and ransom. `made`
    3 in the Rhineland with ordinary stock makes it a shop good. It would be truer as a commission or a card.
29. **Small things missing that these trades imply:** bells and bell metal; lead weights and the sounding lead;
    soapstone sherds for moulds; scrap bronze; scabbards and sword fittings (more than half a sword's value in the
    Ripuarian list).
