# The trade in cloth, wool, linen and dyes, 793 to 1066

Written 6 Oct 2026 for the content brief (BRIEF.md, BRIEF_2.md). A research file for the writers and for whoever
tunes `goods_793.json` and `trade_rules.json`. Nothing here is game data yet.

**How far to trust it.** Web searching was shut for this session (the budget was already spent), and several pages
refused the fetch. Six pages were read today and are marked **[read]** in the source list. Everything else is from
memory of the standard books and editions, and each such fact carries its reference so it can be checked. A `(?)`
marks a figure or a wording I am not sure of. No text is quoted word for word: the old sources are retold in plain
words, so no translation rights are touched.

Money: `d` is a Frankish denier of the reform of 793/4 (about 1.7 g). `p` is an English penny (about 1.2 to 1.4 g
in Offa's and Alfred's time, heavier under Edgar). A solidus or shilling of account in Francia is 12 d; a pound is
240 d. A West Saxon shilling is 5 p, a Mercian one 4 p.

---

## 1. The short of it

- **Cloth was the one made thing every household bought or paid dues in.** Grain feeds, salt keeps, cloth clothes
  and also rigs the ship: a sail is cloth.
- **In 793 the name that sells is Frisian.** "Frisian cloaks" (pallia fresonica) are the standard good cloth of the
  Frankish world: sold up the Rhine for wine, paid as dues to Fulda, given by the emperor at Easter, sent as a gift
  to the Caliph. Whether Frisians wove all of it or only carried it (from England and the Flemish marshes too) is
  still argued. For the game both are true: the Frisian is the carrier, and the terp women weave.
- **England sells cloaks in 796 and wool by 1000.** The one trade letter of the age (Charlemagne to Offa, 796) is
  about the length of English cloaks. By Edgar (died 975) the price of wool is fixed by law. By 1066 England is
  sheep country and Flanders has begun to weave for sale.
- **The north wears its own wadmal and buys the fine stuff.** Norway and, after 874, Iceland make coarse twill,
  shaggy cloaks and sails. Their rich buy fine worsted, linen and strips of silk.
- **Silk comes two ways and never by the piece.** Down the Rhine from Italy and up the Baltic from the Volga. In the
  north it is cut into strips two fingers wide and sewn on the best clothes.
- **Dyes are a small, dear, light cargo**, the best fit of all for a boat of 660 kg: woad balls, madder root, and
  from the north a moss (clubmoss) that fixes dye where there is no alum.
- **The big change comes at the end of the game:** about 1000 to 1066 the treadle loom, the fulling trough and the
  town weaver (a man, paid) begin to replace the woman at the standing loom. Cloth becomes long, heavy and uniform,
  and Flanders becomes its name. A house that begins with Frisian cloaks ends with Flemish cloth and English wool.

## 2. Where it was made, and by whom

Who did the work matters for the game's people. Until about 1000 **every weaver the player meets is a woman**, and
nearly all cloth is made at home or on an estate, in winter. The merchant buys from a steward, an abbey cellarer, a
farmer's wife or another carrier. He does not buy from a "weaver's shop". A town weaver who is a man, a fuller, a
dyer with his own vats: these belong to the last two generations of the game, and to England and Flanders first.

### 2.2 The sheep and the wool

- The sheep are small and short-tailed, many of them brown, grey or black. A fleece is small: about half a kilo to a
  kilo of wool (?) [Ryder 1983]. White wool is worth more because it takes dye.
- Wool is plucked or shorn once a year, about midsummer. Ine's law (c. 690, kept in Alfred's law book, c. 890) says a
  sheep goes with its fleece until midsummer, or the fleece is paid for with two pence [Ine 69].
- Isotope work on the terp textiles shows both local and brought-in wool in Frisian cloth [FCT, read]. So wool
  already moved in the 8th and 9th centuries, at least along the coast.
- England in 1086 (Domesday, just after the game) has great flocks on the Essex and Norfolk marshes and the
  downs; Ely Abbey alone has about 13,400 sheep (?) [Sawyer 2013]. Sawyer's case is that English silver from about
  970 was earned by wool.

### 2.3 Linen, flax and hemp

- Flax wants good soil and much water. It is pulled (not cut) in high summer, the seed is rippled off, the stalks
  are rotted in pits or ponds for one to three weeks, dried, broken, scraped and combed over the autumn, spun in
  winter. Linen is bleached on the grass in spring and summer.
- **Where:** the Frankish estates (the *camsiles* of the polyptychs); the terps (a retting pit at Ezinge, flax seed
  found in plenty [FCT, read]); Saxony and the Wends; and Denmark, where the site of Naes on south Zealand
  (8th to 9th century) had dozens of retting wells and pits: flax worked for sale, not for one farm
  [Hansen and Hoier 2000] (?).
- **Who wanted it:** linen under-clothes spread north in the Viking Age. The pleated linen shifts of the Birka
  graves and the glass and whalebone smoothing tools found from Dorestad to Birka show it [Geijer 1938; Hagg 1984].
- Slav lands used small linen cloths as money. Ibrahim ibn Yaqub, at Prague about 965, saw thin little cloths that
  served for nothing else, ten to a penny (?) [IbnYaqub]. The Rani of Rugen still did so in the 12th century
  (Helmold, after the game).
- Hemp is for rope, nets and coarse sacking, from the Baltic and the east. Hemp sailcloth is later.

### 2.4 Dyes and what fixes them

| Dye | Colour | Where from | Notes and evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Woad** (*waisdo, wad*) | Blue | Grown on good land in Francia, England, the Rhineland; seeds in the Oseberg ship (834) | Leaves cut two to four times from midsummer to autumn, crushed, balled, dried. The balls travel; the vat is made with them later. Named in Capitulare de villis c. 43 |
| **Madder** (*warentia, garantia, maedere*) | Red | Francia above all; perhaps England by the 10th century | Roots of the third year, dug in autumn, dried and ground. The Saint-Denis fair charters name merchants from over the sea buying wine, honey and madder (?) [StDenis]. The common red at Coppergate, York [Walton 1989] |
| **Weld**, dyer's greenweed | Yellow; green over woad | Wild and grown, everywhere | At York. Hardly a trade good |
| **Lichen purple** (orchil, later *korkje*) | Purple, not fast in light | Rocks of Norway, the Isles, Ireland | Found on textiles at York, Dublin and in Scandinavia [Walton 1989; Taylor 1983] (?). A real northern export in small bags |
| **Whelk purple** | True purple-red | Irish and British shores | Bede (731), Ecclesiastical History I.1: Britain has whelks whose scarlet never fades with sun or rain. A dye works on Inishkea, Mayo, 7th century (?). By 800 a rarity for book-painters more than dyers |
| **Kermes** (*vermiculum, coccum*) | Scarlet | Oak scrub of Spain, Provence, the east | In the north found on silk only, not on local wool [Walton 1989] (?). The "vermilion" of Capitulare de villis c. 43 may mean it |
| **Walnut hulls, bark, iron mud** | Brown, black | Local | Walnut hulls in the Hedeby and Oseberg finds (?) |
| **Clubmoss** (*Diphasiastrum*) | No colour: it holds alumina and fixes madder and weld | The heaths of Scandinavia and north Germany. **Does not grow round York** | Found in the Coppergate dye waste, brought over the sea with the Scandinavian settlers [Hall et al. 1984; Walton 1989]. The north's stand-in for alum |
| **Alum** | The true fixer | Egypt, the Aegean, Italy | **No good evidence that it reached the northern dyers in this period.** It comes with the Italian trade in the 12th and 13th centuries |

Most cloth was not dyed at all: it was the sheep's colour, sorted into white, grey and brown. The four colours of
the Frisian cloaks in Notker (white, grey, red, blue) are two sheep colours and two dyes. Colour is what a buyer
pays for.

### 2.5 Silk

- Made only in Byzantium, the Caliphate and further east (Sogdian weavers; Spain from the 9th century).
- **Southern road:** Italy (Venice, Pavia, Rome) over the Alps to the Rhine, as church hangings, vestments and
  wrappings for relics. Nearly every old church on the Rhine and Meuse (Aachen, Maastricht, Cologne, Sens) keeps
  such silks.
- **Eastern road:** the Volga and the Dnieper to Ladoga, Gotland, Birka and Hedeby, along with the dirhams, from
  about 800 to about 970. Oseberg (burial autumn 834): more than a hundred small pieces, cut in strips and sewn on
  [Oseberg, read; Vedeler 2014]. Birka: silk in dozens of graves [Geijer 1938]. York and Lincoln: silk caps of the
  10th century, two of them cut from one piece [Walton 1989]. Dublin: silk caps and scarves [Heckett 2003].
- Aelfric's Colloquy (c. 1000) has an English merchant list his goods from over the sea: purple cloth and silk
  first, then gems, gold, strange garments, spices, wine, oil, ivory, brass, tin, sulphur, glass [Colloquy].

**What moves a price up, for the game:**

- A lord clothing his men before a feast (Easter above all, then Christmas and Pentecost).
- A fleet fitting out (sailcloth and coarse wool for tents and sea clothes) in late winter.
- A new abbey or a new church (linen, a silk, wax).
- A flood on the marsh coast (838): the sheep drown and wool and cloth are gone for a year or two.
- A murrain of sheep. The Chronicle records great cattle deaths more than once (986, 1041, 1054) (?).
- Winter coming: coarse cloth is dear from Michaelmas to Christmas.

Cloth is a **return cargo and a filler** as well as a main one: it is light for its worth, it packs round casks
and stones, and it does not spoil if kept dry. Wool is the opposite: it fills the boat long before it loads her.

## 5. The year

### 5.1 Wool and cloth

| When | What happens | In the market |
|---|---|---|
| Candlemas to Easter | The winter's weaving is finished and cut from the loom. Lords buy for the Easter gifts. Fleets fit out | **New cloth comes to market, and the best is bought at once.** Sailcloth dear |
| Easter to Pentecost | The season opens; the Frisians go up the Rhine and over to England | Cloth plenty in the making lands, wanted everywhere else |
| Midsummer (Saint John) | Shearing. Ine's law takes midsummer as the day the fleece is the owner's | **New wool, at its cheapest** for six or eight weeks |
| Lammas to Michaelmas | Harvest takes every hand. Wool is washed, sorted, combed; spinning goes on all year, at every idle hour | Little cloth made. Last year's stock sells down |
| Saint Denis (9 October) to Martinmas | The fair at Saint-Denis. Rents and dues are paid at Michaelmas and Martinmas, many of them in cloth | **Cloth changes hands in bulk** (abbeys take in their pieces and sell what they do not need). Coarse cloth dear before winter |
| Martinmas to Candlemas | The looms are up. Sea closed | Wool dear by late winter (the stock is spun); nothing moves but by river |

### 5.2 Linen and flax

Sown in spring (about a hundred days in the ground), pulled from late July, rotted in water in August and
September, broken and combed in autumn, spun in winter, woven in late winter, bleached on the grass from April to
July. **New white linen comes to market from midsummer to autumn**; raw scraped flax in bundles from Martinmas.
Linen is dearest in spring, before the bleaching.

### 5.3 Dyes

- Woad: first cutting about midsummer, the last before the frosts. Balls dry for weeks. **New woad from Lammas to
  All Saints**; dear in early summer when last year's is used up.
- Madder: dug after the leaves die back, October and November, dried and pounded. **New madder from Martinmas**, and
  at the Saint-Denis fair. It keeps for years if dry.
- Lichen and clubmoss: gathered in summer in the north, brought south on the autumn ships.
- Dyeing itself wants warm weather and running water: late spring to early autumn.

### 5.4 Wadmal and sails

Iceland's ships sail for Norway about midsummer and must be home, or laid up in Norway, by the autumn gales:
wadmal reaches Norway in high summer and is cheapest there then. Sails are woven over more than one winter and
wanted before the spring launch.

## 6. Units, packing, and what a boat of 660 kg carries

### 6.1 How it was counted

- **The cloak-length** (*pallium, sagum, sagellum*): the unit of the Frisian and English trade. It is a finished
  rectangle that is worn as it comes from the loom: about one and a half ells wide and three long, say 1.5 by 2.5 to
  3 m (?), which is 1.5 to 2.5 kg in a fine twill. A "double" cloak (*sagellum duplum*, capitulary of 808) is twice
  the stuff or two layers, and twice the price. **There is no bolt of cloth before the treadle loom.** A standing
  loom gives a piece as long as the weaver cares to wind on, but it was made and sold as lengths for a garment.
- **The piece owed as dues** (*camsilis* of linen, *sarcilis* of wool): a set size, about 8 to 10 ells long and 2
  wide in the Prum polyptych (?). Four to five metres by one.
- **The ell.** Every land has its own, from elbow to fingertip (about 45 to 50 cm) and longer "thumb ells". Cloth
  is measured along the edge with the seller's stick, and the buyer brings his own.
- **Wadmal** goes by the ell, two ells wide. In Iceland six ells make one "ounce" of account (*eyrir*), and the
  worth of a cow, 120 ells, is the "hundred" (the reckoning is written down in the 12th century; it is put at about
  1000 for its beginning) (?) [Gragas].
- **Wool** goes by the fleece (Ine) and by the **wey** (*waege*: "a weighing", Edgar). The later English wey is 182
  pounds (about 82 kg), half a sack; what Edgar's wey weighed is not known (?). On the Frankish side wool is weighed
  by the pound of the reform (about 408 g). Wool is trodden into big sacks of coarse cloth or carried as rolled
  fleeces tied in bundles.
- **Dyes:** woad as dried balls the size of a fist, in baskets or casks; madder as dried root in sacks or ground in
  small casks; kermes, lichen and gold thread by the pound or the ounce.
- **Silk:** by the whole piece only in a church treasury. In trade by the ell, the strip and the ounce.

### 6.2 Packing

- Cloth is folded, stacked, pressed and corded into a **bale** in a wrapper of coarse cloth with a hide or a
  greased cloth over it. A bale one man can lift is 25 to 30 kg: twelve to twenty cloaks.
- Wool in **sacks** trodden hard; a boat's sack is small, 25 to 30 kg, so a man can throw it up a bank.
- Everything rides on dunnage above the bilge. Sea water ruins dyed cloth and rots wool in the sack. This is the
  good's one great risk and should be the game's too.
- Casks are for the dear and small: ground madder, woad balls, silk, gold thread.

### 6.3 The Gislinge boat (7.7 m, four men, 660 kg, perhaps 2 m3 of dry space amidships (?))

| Cargo | What stops her | A full load | Its worth where it is made (see section 7; rough) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Good cloaks, baled | Weight, just | 20 bales: about 300 cloaks | Far more than a pedlar owns: at even 12 d a cloak, 3,600 d (15 pounds). **The player's real limit on cloth is silver, not the boat** |
| Coarse cloth or wadmal | Weight | 600 kg: about 2,000 ells of wadmal, or 120 dues-pieces | 600 to 1,000 d |
| Wool in sacks | **Room.** Trodden wool is about 150 kg a cubic metre (?) | 10 sacks: 300 kg, some 350 to 450 fleeces | 300 to 600 d |
| Linen | Weight | 600 kg: 250 pieces | Dear; again silver stops him first |
| Woad balls, madder root | Weight | 600 kg | 300 to 900 d (?) |
| A sail for a large ship | Neither | One sail of 90 m2 is 60 to 90 kg dry (?), and a rare, dear, single sale | See 7 |

For the game this gives three clear shapes: **wool is a cargo for a small boat only on a short haul** (it fills her
at half load, and is cheap); **cloth is the cargo to grow into** (a bale or two beside the casks at first, then all
the silver the house has); **dyes are the pedlar's secret** (dense, they keep, few know where to sell them).

By the game's own reckoning (`kg` times `bulk` against 660): 55 units of `frisian_cloth`, 41 of `wool_cloth`,
33 of `wadmal`, 82 of `linen`, 7 sacks of `raw_wool`, 44 baskets of `woad`, 22 sacks of `madder`.

**What follows for the game (my reckoning, to tune):**

- Raw wool: **1 to 2 d a kilo** where it is grown, a little more at midsummer's end, up to double by late winter
  and across the sea.
- Plain undyed wool cloth: about **2 d an ell**, 8 to 15 d for a cloak-length. Cloth is worth three to five times
  its wool, because the spinning is most of the cost (about ten hours of spinning to one of weaving (?)
  [Andersson 2003]).
- A good dyed Frisian or English cloak: **20 to 40 d** where it is made, 40 to 80 d at a court or in the north. The
  very best, 120 d (the 808 ceiling).
- Wadmal: a third to a half of a penny an ell in Iceland and Norway.
- Linen: half as dear again as plain wool cloth of the same size; white bleached linen double.
- Dyes: no figures at all. Guess from the labour: woad balls 1 to 2 d a kilo, madder root 1 to 2 d, ground madder
  3 d; lichen and clubmoss a few pence a bag; kermes by the ounce.
- Silk: no northern price. It is sold by the strip. A strip for a tunic hem should cost what a good cloak costs.
- A ship's sail: woven of something like 60 to 90 kg of sorted wool and two or three years of one woman's work (?).
  It should cost as much as a small boat: 150 to 300 d.

## 10. How the trade changed, age by age

### 793 to 834: the Frisian age

Dorestad at its height. Frisian cloaks are the common coin of gifts in the Frankish world. The cloth goes up the
Rhine against wine and to Saint-Denis in October. English cloaks cross the Channel, and their length is a matter
for kings (796). Royal estates and abbeys make their own plain cloth in women's workhouses and buy colour. The
north is a small market for fine cloth at Ribe, and after 808 at Hedeby. Silk reaches the north only as scraps.
The Frankish law fusses about cloak prices (808).

### 834 to 900: raids, and the cloth moves house

Dorestad is plundered from 834 and is finished by 863. The flood of 838 drowns the marsh flocks. Fulda's and
Werden's books still count Frisian cloaks, so the weaving goes on, but the selling shifts to Tiel, Deventer and
Utrecht and to the Frisian quarters of the Rhine towns. Danes hold Frisian counties (Rorik, 850 to about 873). The
eastern silver road is at full flow: silk strips and dirhams at Birka; Oseberg's hundred pieces in 834. Northmen
settle York (866 on) and Dublin (841 on) and bring their wives, their looms, their taste in dress and their
dye-moss. Iceland is taken from 874. Share of sheep on the terps passes its peak.

### 900 to 975: the northern towns and the English burhs

York under its Northman kings is a cloth-finishing town: madder, woad, weld, moss, teasels (?) and silk workers at
Coppergate. English kings pull trade into the ports under witnesses (Edward, Aethelstan) and set one measure and
a wool price (Edgar). Iceland's first full generation sends wadmal and pile cloaks to Norway (Harald Greycloak, 960s).
The name "Frisian cloth" fades from the texts after Notker (880s); sheep fall to half the terp stock. In Flanders
the counts hold the marshes and the abbeys' flocks grow. About 965 to 975 the Volga silver fails, Birka ends, and
eastern silk now comes by Kiev, by Gotland and over the Alps.

### 975 to 1066: wool, towns and the new loom

- **The treadle loom** with a level warp reaches the north about 1000 (parts from 11th-century Hedeby and Gdansk;
  the rabbi Rashi of Troyes, 1040 to 1105, writes of men weaving with their feet where women use a rod) (?)
  [Cardon 1999]. It makes long cloth fast, and it is men's paid work in towns. The old loom lives on for wadmal
  and sails for centuries.
- **Fulling** by trampling in troughs becomes a trade, and with it the thick felted cloth the later Middle Ages
  call simply "cloth". The water-driven fulling mill is first heard of in Normandy about 1086 (?), just outside
  the game.
- **Flanders** has towns (Ghent, Bruges, Saint-Omer, Arras) and by about 1070 a name for cloth. Its own marsh wool
  begins to run short; English wool begins to cross. The great fairs of Flanders are of the end of the century.
- **England** sells wool (Edgar's floor price; the Emperor's men at London c. 1000) and is, by Domesday, a land of
  sheep. English needlework in gold and silk is famous abroad: the stole and maniple made at Winchester between
  909 and 916 and laid in Saint Cuthbert's coffin in 934 show the standard [Battiscombe 1956].
- **The Rhine towns** (Cologne, Tiel) carry cloth to London and pay their toll in it.
- **Iceland** makes wadmal its money.
- **Denmark and England under one king** (1016 to 1042): one market for Danish linen, English wool and Norwegian
  wadmal.
- 1066: the Normans take the land of sheep. The Bayeux hanging, made within a few years, is wool thread on linen
  in the same woad, madder and weld (?) [Bayeux].

### 13.1 goods_793.json: what is wrong

1. **Wool is far too cheap.** `raw_wool`: a 30 kg sack at 8 d is about a quarter of a penny a kilo. Ine's fleece
   (2 p for well under a kilo) and Edgar's wey (120 p) both give **1 to 2 d a kilo**. A 30 kg sack should be about
   35 to 45 d.
2. **`fleeces` is wrong in weight and price.** "Bundle of 10, 25 kg, 8 d": ten fleeces of the period weigh 6 to 10
   kg, and by Ine are worth 20 p (about 14 d). It also doubles `raw_wool`. Either drop it, or turn it into
   **woolfells** (sheepskins with the wool on), which are a different and real good.
3. **Cloth is too cheap beside its wool, once wool is right.** `wool_cloth` at 18 d for 8 kg is 2.25 d a kilo, the
   price of the raw wool in it. Spinning and weaving make cloth worth three to five times its wool: about 35 to 45 d
   for 8 kg.
4. **`alum` wanted in Frisia and Flanders (strength 2) has no evidence for this period.** Northern dyers fixed their
   dyes with clubmoss, as the York finds show. Alum belongs to the Italian trade after 1100. Lower it to a rarity,
   or give it a start year near the end.
5. **`madder` made in Zeeland** is a thing of the 12th to 14th centuries. For 793 to 1066 the source is Francia
   (`neustria` should be 2, not 1; add `rhineland` or `meuse` at 1 from the royal estates). Wanted is too narrow:
   add `northumbria` (York, from about 870), `england_south`, `schleswig`.
6. **`raw_wool` wanted in Flanders at strength 3 from the first year** is 250 years early. In 793 the Flemish
   marshes have their own sheep. It should be weak or nothing until about 1000 and strong only from about 1050.
7. **`frisian_cloth` made in Flanders (2) and England (1) for all 273 years.** English cloaks are a separate, named
   good in 796. Flemish cloth as a trade name is of about 1050 on. And the name "Frisian cloth" drops out of the
   texts after the 880s. One row cannot carry all three.
8. **`frisian_cloth` wanted in the Caliphate (2)** rests on one gift told as a story. It should not make a market.
9. **`frisian_cloth` wanted in Svealand and Ladoga at 3** rests on Geijer's reading of the Birka twills, which is
   disputed (Norwegian and Syrian origins are argued). Keep it, but 2 is safer, and say so in the note.
10. **Iceland makes `wadmal`, `fleeces` and `sailcloth` "from 0".** Iceland is empty before 874 and has no surplus
    before about 930. The table has only one `from` per good, so a region cannot start late.
11. **The unit "bolt" is out of period** for every row that uses it. Before the treadle loom cloth is sold by the
    cloak-length, the dues-piece and the ell. `frisian_cloth` "bolt, 6 kg" would be truer as "three cloaks" or as
    one cloak of 2 kg at a third of the price; `wadmal` as "20 ells"; `linen` as a "piece".
12. **`kermes` wanted in Neustria, the Rhineland and southern England as a dye** is not shown by the finds: in the
    north kermes turns up only on silk that was dyed far away. Keep it very rare.
13. **`sailcloth` at 40 d for 15 kg** undersells the most labour-heavy cloth there was. And `wanted` is only 1 in
    three regions; every shipbuilding coast wants it, most of all before a fleet sails.
14. **`linen` is not made in Frisia, the Danish isles or the Rhineland** in the table, though the terp finds, Naes
    and the abbey dues say it was.

  - **English cloaks** (*saga*), 793 on: the subject of the 796 letter.
  - **Flemish cloth**, from about 1030.
  - **Pile cloaks** (*vararfeldir*), Iceland and Norway, from about 950.
  - **Clubmoss** (dye-moss), Norway and Jutland to York: light, cheap at source, wanted by few, and a secret worth
    having. The best "knowledge is the treasure" good in this whole trade.
  - **Lichen purple**, from Norway, the Isles and Ireland.
  - **Weld** (yellow), if a third dye is wanted; it hardly travelled.
  - **Teasels** and **wool combs and shears** (Capitulare de villis c. 43); the iron ones could sit under
    `iron_tools`.
  - **Woolfells** (see 2 above).
  - **Gold thread and gold-worked bands**, and **English embroidery** from about 900.
  - **Spindle whorls and loom weights of soapstone** from Norway could sit under `soapstone`.
  - **Glass linen smoothers** could sit under glass.
- **A way for a region to start or stop making a good in a given year** (Iceland 874, Flanders about 1000, the terps
  fading after 900, York's dyers from about 870). Without it the cloth trade cannot change age by age, and section
  10 cannot be played.
- **A note on `wool_cloth`, `linen`, `flax`, `hemp`, `madder`, `woad`**: their `note` fields are empty or one line.

### 13.3 trade_rules.json: what is wrong or missing

1. **No law touches cloth.** The file has arms, horses, captives and sacred things. Missing, all from section 8:
   - the **measure rule** on Frisian cloaks (Francia, to 814): short cloaks refused or half price at court;
   - the **808 ceiling** on cloak prices (Francia);
   - the **witness rule** (England, from about 900; over 20 p from the 930s): this one touches every good, not only
     wool;
   - **Edgar's floor price for wool** and his one measure (England, from about 962);
   - the **Byzantine ban on purple and the best silks** and the **Rus cap of 944** (50 gold coins, sealed): the
     `byzantine` zone has a toll but no law;
   - **Sunday** (Francia from 789): no work and no market;
   - the **Alfred and Guthrum pledge** for trade across the border (878 to about 920).
2. **The English toll is a flat 5 %.** The one English tariff we have (London, c. 1000) charges **by the ship**
   (a halfpenny, a penny, fourpence) and takes **toll in kind** from the Emperor's men (cloth, pepper, gloves,
   vinegar). For a boat like the player's that is a halfpenny, not a twentieth of the cargo.
3. **Cloth as money is missing from `coins`.** Iceland sits in the `weight` zone, which is right until about 1000;
   after that its money is the ell of wadmal (six to the ounce). The Wends used linen cloths (965), and Frisia
   reckoned in cloth later (?). At least the Icelandic ell belongs here once Iceland has ports.
4. **Freedom from toll for abbey goods** is not a rule anywhere. Much cloth moved as an abbey's own goods under a
   charter, toll-free. It is the natural reward for carrying on commission.
5. **`market.cheap` and `market.dear`** (0.85 and 1.2) are there and work, but with no months on the cloth rows they
   never fire for cloth. For wool the swing should be wider than for cloth: wool cannot be stored cheaply by those
   who grow it, cloth can.
6. **No rule for a good that fills the boat before it loads her.** `bulk` 3 for wool gives 220 kg in a 660 kg boat;
   my reckoning is about 300 kg. Close enough; but nothing tells the player why, and the word on the item should.

### 13.4 Things other files should know (needs)

- An effect or a market rule for **"measure the cloth"** (the act `sample` exists; a result "short measure" does not).
- Chronicle entries, if they are not there: 808 (the cloak price cap: the chronicle has it, with figures that
  differ from mine), about 830 (Fulda's 855 cloaks), about 962 (Edgar's wool price and measure), 968 (Liudprand's
  silks), about 1000 (London tolls: there).
