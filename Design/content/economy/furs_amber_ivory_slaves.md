# Furs, amber, walrus ivory and slaves, 793 to 1066

Written 6 October 2026 for the content team. One file, four trades, because they are one trade: the north's
raw riches going south and west for silver, cloth, wine and swords, in the same ships, through the same few
harbours, taxed by the same kings.

**How to read the marks.** A fact with no mark is one I am sure of from the source named. `(?)` means I could
not check it this session and it is from memory, or the scholars disagree, or the source is later than 1066 and
is being read backwards. Web searching was shut off for this session and Wikipedia gave little, so more facts
than I would like carry the mark. Section 13 lists every one worth checking before it ships.

**Money used here.** `d` is the Frankish denier of about 1.7 g of silver, as in `trade_rules.json`. A pound is
240 d (about 408 g). A Norse mark of silver is about 204 to 216 g, so about 120 to 127 d. A dirham of 2.9 g is
1.7 d. An English pound of pennies around the year 1000 is about 310 to 360 g of silver, so about 185 to 210 d.
A Byzantine gold nomisma (4.4 g of gold) is about 31 d if gold stands to silver at 12 to 1 `(?)`.

**No verbatim quotes.** I could not open a public-domain translation of Ohthere, Rimbert or Adam this session,
so everything below is paraphrase. Where the game wants the words themselves, take them from the editions named
in section 12 (all translator-died-before-1956).

## 2. Furs

### 2.1 Where they came from, and who took them

- **Halogaland and the Sami.** Ohthere, a chieftain from the far north of Norway, told King Alfred about 890
  that his wealth was mostly the tribute the Sami (he says *Finnas*) paid him. The richest paid fifteen marten
  skins, five reindeer skins, one bear skin, ten measures of feathers, a coat of bear or otter skin, and two
  ship-ropes sixty ells long, one of walrus hide and one of seal. He owned 600 tame reindeer, six of them decoys,
  but only twenty cattle, twenty sheep and twenty pigs. (Old English Orosius, c. 890.) This is the best single
  source for the whole northern trade and it is exactly in period.
- **The forests behind Ladoga.** Aldeigjuborg (Staraya Ladoga) stands from the 750s (tree-ring dates, 753 `(?)`)
  as the gate. The Primary Chronicle says the Northmen from over the sea took tribute of the Chud, the Slovenes,
  the Merya and the Krivichi, and the Khazars took a squirrel skin from each hearth further south (entry for 859;
  the chronicle is of about 1113, so the detail is late). Tribute, not trapping for sale, was how most furs
  left the forest.
- **The Volga Bulgars and beyond.** Ibn Fadlan (922) saw the Rus at Bulghar selling sable and slave girls.
  Ibn Rusta (c. 903-913) says the Rus have no fields and live by selling sable, squirrel and other furs for coin
  they tie into their belts, and that among the Bulgars marten skins pass as money, one skin for two and a half
  dirhams `(?)`. The Bulgars got the best furs from the Wisu and the Yura further north by a dumb barter in the
  dark of winter (Ibn Fadlan mentions the Wisu; the full account of the silent trade is later, al-Biruni and
  after `(?)`).
- **The Prussians.** Adam of Bremen (c. 1075, book IV, 18) says the Sembi have plenty of strange furs and give
  costly marten skins for the woollen cloaks the Saxons call *faldones*. He says of the Swedes that they hold
  marten skins as cheap as dung, to the shame of Christians who crave them (IV, 21 `(?)` for the chapter).
- **Ireland and Britain.** Pine marten, otter and fox were native. The marten skins that came by ship to
  Chester before 1066 (Domesday) are most likely Irish.
- **Francia itself.** Beaver and otter were hunted on the royal estates; the Capitulare de villis (c. 800) lists
  beaver-hunters (*beverarii*) among an estate's men `(?)` for the chapter number.

### 2.2 The kinds, as the sources rank them

1. **Sable**: the richest, from the far north east, sold through Bulghar to the Caliphate. Ibn Fadlan, Ibn Rusta.
2. **Black fox**: Ibn Khurradadhbih (c. 870 `(?)`, some date the passage to the 840s) names beaver skins and
   black fox skins, with swords, as what the Rus carry south. Al-Masudi (c. 943) says the black fox of the Burtas
   is the fur kings wear `(?)`.
3. **Marten**: the staple of the western trade (Ohthere, Adam, the capitulary of 808, Domesday Chester) and
   money on the Volga and later in Rus (the *kuna*).
4. **Beaver**: named first by Ibn Khurradadhbih; with it goes castoreum, the beaver's musk, a medicine.
5. **Ermine**, the white winter stoat.
6. **Otter**: Ohthere's tribute coat; the capitulary of 808.
7. **Squirrel**, the grey winter skin: the hearth-tax of the forest. It becomes the great bulk fur of the west
   only in the 11th and 12th centuries; before that it is the cheap one.
8. **Bear, wolf, reindeer, seal**: hides more than furs.

Al-Muqaddasi (c. 985) gives the list of what came from Bulghar through Khwarazm: sable, squirrel, ermine,
marten, fox, beaver, spotted hare, goat skins, wax, arrows, birch bark, tall caps, fish glue, fish teeth,
castoreum, amber, dressed hides, honey, hazelnuts, falcons, swords, armour, maple wood, Slav slaves, sheep and
cattle. That one sentence has all four of this file's trades in it (the fish teeth are walrus ivory).

### 2.3 Where they were wanted, and why

- **Warmth and rank in the west.** Einhard (c. 23) says Charlemagne wore a jerkin of otter or marten skins in
  winter. Notker (II, 17) tells how the king in a sheepskin took his courtiers hunting in the rain in the silks
  and fine furs they had just bought from the Venetians at Pavia, and made them wear the ruined things next day.
  The joke is in period and already in the brief (Grant 1907 is safe to quote).
- **The Caliphate** paid most. Furs were worn at the Abbasid court and in cold Khwarazm and Khurasan; the silver
  that paid for them is the dirham flood in the Baltic (section 2.7).
- **Byzantium**: the Rus treaties of 911 and 944 are about slaves, wax and furs going down the Dnieper.

### 2.4 Routes and ships

- **The north way.** Halogaland down the Norwegian coast to Skiringssalr (Ohthere: a month, camping each night,
  with a fair wind), then five days to Hedeby, Denmark to port for three days and the Danish isles for two. From
  Hedeby 18 km overland to the Treene and so by the Eider to the North Sea, the Frisian coast and Dorestat.
- **The east way.** The forests to Ladoga in winter by sledge; Ladoga to Birka and Gotland at ice-break; Birka to
  Hedeby. Rimbert (Vita Anskarii, c. 20, 27) shows Birka and Dorestat in direct touch about 850: a rich woman of
  Birka sends her daughter to Dorestat with her alms because there are churches and poor there.
- **The Volga**, from about 800 to about 970: Ladoga, the portages, Bulghar, Itil, the Caspian, and camels from
  Jurjan to Baghdad (Ibn Khurradadhbih). **The Dnieper**, from about 900: Kiev to Constantinople in June
  (Constantine Porphyrogenitus, De administrando imperio, c. 9, written c. 950).
- **Ships.** Furs weigh nothing and are worth a great deal: they travel in whatever is going, on top of the
  heavy cargo, kept dry. A boat the size of the player's is the right boat for them. See `ships.md`.

### 2.5 The season

- **Trapped** from the first hard frost to the thaw (Martinmas to Lady Day). Only the winter skin is prime:
  thick, with a clean pale flesh side.
- **Gathered as tribute in winter.** The Rus princes went round their Slav tributaries from November to April
  (the *poliudie*), came back to Kiev at the thaw and sailed in June (De administrando imperio, c. 9).
- **At Birka and Ladoga at ice-break**, and at Birka also over the ice in late winter: the town had a winter
  market (ice-nails, skates and sledge parts in the finds `(?)`).
- **At Skiringssalr, Ripa and Hedeby in summer**; at Dorestat, Lundenwic and the Rhine from midsummer to
  Michaelmas.
- **Dear** from Martinmas to Christmas, when the courts meet and last year's skins are gone.

So the cheap and dear months move with distance from the forest. A single pair of month lists for all regions
(as `goods_793.json` has) is right for Birka and wrong for Dorestat.

### 2.6 Units, packing and a small boat's load

- **The timber of forty skins.** Domesday Book says Chester paid the king, before 1066, its rent and three
  timbers of marten skins `(?)`. The word is Norse and Low German as well (*timbr*, *Zimmer*), and the Rus
  counted sables in forties (*sorok*, attested later). It may come from the two boards the skins were pressed
  between `(?)`. Forty is the lining of one robe.
- **Bundles of ten** for the big skins (beaver, fox, otter), tied flesh side out.
- **Sealed sacks.** Novgorod has given up dozens of wooden cylinder locks of the late 10th and 11th centuries:
  a cord through the mouth of a sack of tribute furs, pegged inside a wooden cylinder cut with the collector's
  mark and a tally of what was in it. The sack could not be opened without breaking it. This is the period's own
  answer to fraud, and a good thing for a card.
- **Weights** (my estimates from dried skins, all `(?)`): squirrel 15-20 g, so a timber under 1 kg; ermine about
  10 g, a timber under half a kilo; marten and sable 60-80 g, a timber about 3 kg; fox about 350 g, ten about
  3.5 kg; otter about 500 g; beaver 700-900 g, ten about 8 kg; a bear skin 5-10 kg.
- **In the Gislinge boat.** Furs are light and bulky. At the table's bulk of 4, her 660 kg takes about 165 kg of
  furs: some 55 timbers of marten, 2,200 skins, worth near 6,600 d at the table's value. No merchant with a debt
  of 480 d can fill her. The truth of the fur trade for the player is that **silver is the limit, not the hold**,
  and a single dry sack under the foredeck can pay the debt. Wet is the enemy: an open boat, four men, spray. A
  soaked timber rots and sheds in days.

Read together: a marten skin is worth one to two pennies in the forest, four on the Volga, and a made-up robe of
forty is capped at 360 d in Francia. The table's marten at 120 d a timber (60 at source, 204 where keenly wanted)
sits inside that. Sable, black fox and ermine have no price in any western source.

### 2.8 Tolls, bans and laws

- **Nobody banned furs.** They are the one great northern good with no law against them.
- **A price cap**, 808 (above): the only Carolingian price law on a luxury.
- **The king buys first.** At Chester before 1066, anyone with marten skins was ordered by the king's reeve to
  sell to no one until the reeve had seen them and bought what he wanted; the fine was 40 shillings (Domesday
  Book, Cheshire `(?)` for the exact wording). The same right of first purchase is known for kings in the north.
- **The Sami trade as a lord's right.** Ohthere's tribute was his, not open to others. Later Norwegian kings
  made the Sami trade (*finnkaup*) and tax (*finnskattr*) a royal monopoly; Egils saga puts that under Harald
  Fairhair, but the saga is of the 13th century `(?)`.
- **The tenth.** The Khazar king took a tenth of what the Rus carried through Itil, and the Emperor a tenth on
  the Black Sea (Ibn Khurradadhbih). The Bulgar king took a tenth of every cargo (Ibn Fadlan). The Frankish
  king took his tenth at Dorestat and Quentovic. This agrees with `trade_rules.json`.

### 2.9 Frauds and tests

- **Summer skins sold as winter.** Turn the skin: a prime winter pelt is white or cream on the flesh side; a
  skin taken out of season is blue-grey or dark there, and the hair pulls out.
- **Blow into the fur.** The hairs part and show the underwool down to the leather. No underwool, no winter.
- **Dyed and smoked skins**: a brown marten darkened to pass for sable. Wet a finger and rub; look at the roots
  of the hair, which stay pale under dye.
- **Bad skins inside the bundle.** A timber is forty: open it and count, look at the middle ones. Hence the
  sealed sack.
- **Pieced skins, heads and paws** sewn in to make up the number.
- **Moth and damp.** A bundle that smells sweet or sour has been wet.

### 2.10 How it changed

- **793-833.** Furs come west by Birka, Skiringssalr (from c. 800), Ripa and Hedeby (from 808) to Dorestat.
  The first dirhams reach Ladoga (hoards from the 780s `(?)`). Few furs, high prices, Frisians in the middle.
- **834-899.** Dorestat fails (834-863); the western road shifts to Hedeby, Daventre, Tiel, Cologne. The Volga
  road grows: silver in the north is now Islamic. Ohthere sails c. 890.
- **900-954.** The height of the eastern trade: Samanid dirhams by the hundred thousand, Ibn Fadlan in 922.
  Gotland alone has given over 700 hoards. The best market for a fur is east, not west.
- **955-990.** The silver fails (the Samanid mines; the Khazars broken by Sviatoslav c. 965-969). Birka ends
  c. 975; Sigtuna (c. 980) and Novgorod take over. Furs turn west again, for German and then English pennies.
- **991-1066.** Novgorod's sealed tribute sacks; squirrel becomes the bulk fur; marten skins in a town's rent at
  Chester; furs on every great man in the west. Adam of Bremen complains of it within ten years of 1066.

### 2.11 The finds

- **Birka**: thousands of foot bones of squirrel, marten and fox in the Black Earth, which is what is left when
  skins come in with the paws on and are dressed in town (Wigh 2001 `(?)`); fur remains in the chamber graves.
- **Aland and the upper Volga**: clay models of beaver paws laid in graves of the 8th to 10th centuries, only in
  those two regions, joining the Malar valley to the fur road.
- **Novgorod**: the wooden cylinder locks (above), some with the word for a sack and a count cut on them.
- **Blunt arrowheads** of bone and antler, across the north: they kill a squirrel without holing the skin.
- **The hoards**: the Spillings hoard on Gotland (c. 870, 67 kg of silver) is what furs and slaves bought.
- **Sami hearth-row sites** in the north of Norway and Sweden, c. 800-1300, read as camps of the fur trade `(?)`.

## 3. Amber

### 3.1 Where and who

- **Samland** (the Sambian peninsula, the Prussians' coast between the Vistula and Curonian lagoons) has by far
  the greatest deposit in the world. In our period nothing was dug: amber was picked off the beach and netted in
  the surf after a storm had torn it out of the sea bed.
- **Truso**, at Janow Pomorski on Lake Druzno by the Vistula lagoon, was the amber harbour: founded late in the
  8th century, Scandinavians in the middle and Balts around, ended c. 1000 as the river silted and after a fire
  (excavated by Marek Jagodzinski from the 1980s). Wulfstan sailed from Hedeby to Truso in seven days and nights
  about 890 and told King Alfred; he speaks of the Ests' honey, mead, mare's milk and many kings, and says
  nothing of amber. The amber is in the ground: raw lumps and half-made beads all over the site.
- **The Curonians** (Grobina) and the coast of Pomerania (Wolin, and Gdansk from c. 980) had it too.
- **The North Sea** gives a little: the west coast of Jutland and the Frisian isles. Ripa's amber may be local.

### 3.2 Where it was wanted, and why

- **Beads, pendants, Thor's hammers, gaming pieces, spindle whorls.** It was the north's own gem, and worked in
  every market town: Ripa (from the 8th century), Hedeby (the largest finds), Skiringssalr, Birka, Aldeigjuborg
  (a workshop in the earliest layers, 750s `(?)`), Wolin, Duiblinn (Fishamble Street, 10th-11th century,
  thousands of pieces `(?)`), Eoforwic (Coppergate).
- **The east.** Al-Muqaddasi lists amber among the goods of Bulghar. Amber beads lie with dirhams along the
  Volga road. It went east with the furs.
- **Medicine and luck.** Worn against fever and the evil eye; burned as incense. Its smell when burned is how
  it is known from glass.
- The Franks and English used little of it in this period compared with the 6th and 7th centuries, when every
  rich woman's grave had amber beads. In 793 it is a northern and eastern taste.

### 3.3 Season, units, the load

- **Gathered** from the first autumn gales to spring, best the morning after an onshore storm, by people wading
  with nets. So the stock is made in winter and sold when the ships come: **cheapest at Truso in spring and early
  summer, dearest in autumn** when the winter's gathering has gone.
- **Sold raw by weight**, in sacks and baskets, sorted by size: lumps a bead-maker can turn, and small stuff
  for burning. A fist-sized lump is a rarity worth more than a sack of crumbs.
- **Weight**: it is very light, hardly denser than water. A 10 kg sack is a big sack.
- **In the Gislinge boat**: 660 kg would be 60-odd sacks, more than Truso would have on a given day. Amber is a
  cargo of a few sacks bought where it is cheap and carried to a town with a bead-maker. Worked beads are worth
  many times the raw stone by weight; the profit is the craftsman's.

### 3.4 Prices

**There is no price for amber in any source of the period that I know.** The table's 30 d for a 10 kg sack is a
guess and should be marked as one. The only anchors are that worked beads were common in ordinary graves in the
north (so not dear there) and rare south of the Rhine.

### 3.5 Laws and tolls

None in the period. The right to the beach was the local lord's by the 13th century (the Teutonic Order hanged
people for gathering); nothing shows that before 1066 `(?)`. Do not put a gathering ban in the game.

### 3.6 Frauds and tests

- **Yellow glass** sold as amber. Real amber is warm in the hand and light; glass is cold and heavy.
- **Salt water.** Amber floats in strong brine and sinks slowly in fresh water; glass and stone sink in both. A
  merchant with a bucket and a handful of salt can test a sack. (The physics is sure; that men of the time
  did it is my inference from how it was gathered: it floats in on the surf.)
- **Rub it on wool**: it draws chaff and thread to itself. Known since the Greeks (Pliny, Natural History 37).
- **A hot needle**: amber gives a pine-resin smell; glass gives none.
- **Small stuff under the big.** Tip the sack out.

### 3.7 How it changed

- **793-899.** Truso at its height; raw amber to Ripa, Hedeby, Birka and Ladoga; Wulfstan sails c. 890.
- **900-990.** Amber goes east with the furs; Wolin rises; Dublin's workshops begin.
- **990-1066.** Truso ends c. 1000; Gdansk (c. 980) and Wolin are the amber towns; the Prussians are now a
  mission field (Adalbert of Prague is killed among them in 997).

### 3.8 The finds

Workshop waste (raw lumps, split blanks, beads broken in the drilling) at Ripa, Hedeby, Skiringssalr, Birka,
Staraya Ladoga, Truso, Wolin, Gdansk, Dublin and York; amber gaming pieces and Thor's hammers from Hedeby; amber
beads in the Volga graves.

## 4. Walrus ivory

### 4.1 Where and who

- **Halogaland and the White Sea.** Ohthere said he first sailed north round the cape partly for the walrus,
  because they have very fine bone in their teeth, and he brought some of the teeth to the king. Their hide makes
  the best ship-ropes. He says he and five others killed sixty in two days; whether he means walrus or the great
  whales he speaks of next is argued.
- **Iceland**, from the settlement (c. 870). There was a native herd; it was hunted out within two or three
  centuries (Keighley and others 2019 `(?)`).
- **Greenland**, from c. 985. The hunt was in the Nordrsetur, around Disko Bay far north of the farms, in
  summer. Greenland becomes the main source only after our period.
- **The proof by DNA.** Star and others (2018, Proceedings of the Royal Society B) took DNA from the tusk
  sockets of walrus skulls found in the towns of Europe. Before about 1120 they are nearly all of the eastern
  kind (the Barents Sea and perhaps Iceland); after it nearly all are of a kind found only in west Greenland and
  Canada `(?)` for the exact count and date. For the game: **to 1066 the ivory comes from the north of Norway
  and the White Sea, with Iceland second and Greenland a rumour.**
- **The Bulgar road.** The fish teeth in al-Muqaddasi's list are walrus, from the White Sea by way of the
  Bjarmians and Bulghar. Al-Biruni (c. 1040) calls it *khutu* and says it is prized for knife hafts `(?)`.

### 4.2 Where it was wanted, and why

- **Elephant ivory was nearly gone** north of the Alps from the 7th century to the 10th. Carolingian carvers cut
  their book covers from old Roman panels shaved down. Walrus tusk was the ivory that could be had.
- **The Church**: crozier heads, tau crosses, pyxes, book covers, crucifix figures. English carving in walrus
  ivory flowers from the later 10th century (the Winchester style); the Rhineland and Cologne follow in the
  11th and 12th. Aelfric's Colloquy (c. 1000) has the merchant list ivory among what he brings over the sea.
- **The hall**: sword grips, gaming pieces, combs, belt ends.
- **In 793** it is a rare thing a king shows a guest. By 1000 it is a regular good with workshops waiting.

### 4.3 Routes, season, units, the load

- **Route**: the same north way as Ohthere's furs, to Skiringssalr and Hedeby, then to England and the Rhine.
  From the 10th century also by Dublin and by Trondheim (founded c. 997).
- **Season**: hunted in summer on the haul-out beaches; the tusks come south with the last ships before
  Michaelmas or with the first of the next year. From Greenland one ship a year at best.
- **How it was shipped**: the tusks were left in the front of the skull, hacked off as one block (the rostrum),
  so the buyer could see they were a true pair and whole; the carver broke them out at the other end. Rostra
  with the tusks gone are what is found in Dublin, Trondheim, Bergen, Schleswig, Sigtuna and Kiev.
- **Weight**: a tusk of the Atlantic walrus is commonly 30 to 50 cm and half a kilo to a kilo and a half `(?)`;
  a pair in its block of skull perhaps 3 to 5 kg.
- **In the Gislinge boat**: there are never enough tusks to load her. Five pairs is a rich man's purchase.
  The thing to carry with them is **walrus-hide rope**, which is real cargo and which every shipwright wants.

### 4.4 Prices

**No price in the period.** Ohthere's word is the only measure: fit for a king. The one hard figure is far
later: in 1327 Greenland's crusade tithe was paid in walrus tusk, some hundreds of tusks sold at Bergen `(?)`.
Leave the table's 90 d a pair marked as a guess. It is not out of line with a sword at about 84 d (Lex Ribuaria).

### 4.5 Laws and tolls

None known on ivory itself. A king's right to buy first is likely wherever it landed (as with the martens at
Chester). Greenland and Iceland law on the hunt and on wreck and stranding is 12th century and later.

### 4.6 Frauds and tests

- **Walrus or elephant?** Cut across, a walrus tusk has a smooth outer layer and a core that looks like curds
  or oatmeal. Elephant ivory has no such core and shows fine crossing arcs. A carver wants the outer layer thick.
- **Bone and antler** sold as ivory: bone shows small dark pores and lines; ivory is close and waxy.
- **Whale tooth and whale bone** are honest goods of their own (the Franks Casket is whale bone) but cheaper.
- **Cracked and weathered tusks** picked up from an old kill, sold as fresh. They ring dull and split along
  the length. This is why a buyer wanted them still in the skull.
- **The unicorn.** Narwhal tusk sold as unicorn horn is a trade of the 12th century and after `(?)`. I know of
  no evidence for it before 1066.

### 4.7 How it changed

- **793-889.** Known in the north (pieces at Ripa and Hedeby are few `(?)`); a curiosity in the west.
- **c. 890.** Ohthere at Alfred's court: the first written word of it.
- **900-990.** Icelandic ivory joins the Norwegian. English carvers begin.
- **990-1066.** The carving schools of England and the Rhine at work; Dublin and Trondheim handle rostra;
  Greenland's first tusks. The great Greenland trade (and the Lewis chessmen) are the next century's.

### 4.8 The finds

Tusk-less rostra from Dublin, Trondheim, Bergen, Oslo, Schleswig, Sigtuna, London and Kiev; English late Saxon
ivories (tau crosses, a pen case, small Crucifixion panels, mostly in the British Museum and the V&A); gaming
pieces and comb plates from Hedeby and Lund; Ohthere's own account.

## 5. Slaves

The table calls them captives, and that is the better word for the game. The people of the time said *theow*
(English), *thraell* (Norse), *mancipium* (Latin), and from the 10th century *sclavus*, which is simply "Slav".

### 5.1 Who was taken, and by whom

- **By the Northmen in the west.** The Annals of Ulster: in 821 Howth was plundered and a great number of women
  carried off; in 871 Olaf and Ivar came back to Dublin from Alba with 200 ships and a great prey of English,
  Britons and Picts. Findan of Leinster was taken about 845, sold from ship to ship and escaped in Orkney (his
  Life, written at Rheinau). The Frankish annals have captives taken on the Seine and Loire in most years from
  841, and great men ransomed: the abbot of Saint-Denis in 858 for 686 pounds of gold and 3,250 of silver.
- **By the Rus in the east.** Ibn Rusta: the Rus raid the Slavs by ship, take them and sell them at Khazaran and
  Bulghar. Ibn Fadlan saw them do it in 922. Constantine (c. 950) describes the slaves walked in chains for six
  miles round the Dnieper rapids.
- **By the Franks and Saxons among the Wends**, all through the period; by the 10th century so many that the
  people's name became the word.
- **By everyone at home.** Debt, crime and famine made slaves too. In Domesday Book (1086) about one person in
  ten in England is a slave, more in the west.

### 5.2 Where they were sold

- **Hedeby.** Rimbert's Life (c. 18) tells how he saw a line of chained Christians at Sliaswich, among them a nun
  who sang psalms to show what she was, and gave his horse and its harness for her. Ansgar bought Danish and
  Wendish boys to train as priests (Vita Anskarii, c. 15) and rebuked the Nordalbingians for selling on
  Christians who had escaped to them (c. 38).
- **Dublin**, from 841: the western pen. **Bristol** and **Chester** fed it from England in the 11th century.
- **Rouen**, c. 1000: Warner of Rouen's poem *Moriuht* has an Irishman taken by Northmen, sold in Northumbria
  and again in Saxony, and finding his wife for sale at Rouen. The prices in the poem are insults (pennies and
  half a loaf) and are not evidence `(?)` for the exact figures.
- **Brannoy** (the Brenn isles at the mouth of the Gota river): Laxdaela saga, c. 12, has Hoskuld buy an Irish
  woman there from a merchant called Gilli the Russian, about 950. The saga is 13th century.
- **Prague.** Ibrahim ibn Yaqub (965) says Rus and Slavs come there from Cracow, and Muslims, Jews and Turks from
  the lands of the Turks, and carry away slaves, tin and furs.
- **Verdun.** Liudprand of Cremona (Antapodosis VI, 6; he was in Constantinople in 949) says the merchants of
  Verdun make eunuchs and sell them to Spain at an immense profit.
- **Venice**, for the Slavs of the Adriatic and the Danube road.
- **Bulghar and Itil**, then Baghdad; **Constantinople** after 907.
- **London.** Bede (IV, 22) has a Northumbrian thegn sold to a Frisian in London in 679. It is before our time
  but it is the one text that puts a Frisian merchant in this trade, and the player is a Frisian.

### 5.3 Routes

1. **Irish Sea**: Dublin, Chester, Bristol; north to the Isles and Norway; from the 9th century on to Iceland.
2. **North Sea and Baltic**: Dublin and England to Hedeby; Hedeby to Birka and the east.
3. **The overland road south**: the Elbe and Saale marches to Regensburg and Prague, to Mainz, to Verdun, down
   the Saone and Rhone to Arles, and to Spain. Or down the Danube past Raffelstetten and over the Alps to Venice.
4. **The Volga and the Dnieper.**

A small boat's part is the short sea legs of 1 and 2, and ransom.

### 5.4 The season

- **Taken** in the raiding months, from the spring sowing to the harvest.
- **Sold** from Lammas to Martinmas, when the fleets come home and before the buyer must feed a mouth all winter.
  That is when they are cheapest.
- **Dearest** in spring: hands are wanted for ploughing and the pens are empty.
- **In the east**: taken on the winter round, sold at Bulghar when the river opens (Ibn Fadlan was there in
  May) and at Constantinople in summer.

### 5.5 Units and the load

- Sold **by the head**, priced by age, sex, health and skill. A woman who can weave or a man who can smith is
  worth more; so is anyone who can be ransomed.
- **In the Gislinge boat**: 7.7 m and four of a crew. Three captives is a load; five is a danger. Each eats and
  drinks as a crewman does, must be watched at night, and can be lost, fall sick or go over the side. At the
  table's 60 kg and bulk of 3 the hold takes three, which is right.
- **Ransom is the better card.** A captive carried home to kin or to a bishop is worth more than one sold, and
  is a good deed where selling is a sin.

### 5.7 Laws and bans

No one in the period banned slavery. What was banned, again and again, was **selling Christians to heathens or
out of the land**. The repetition shows it went on.

**In the north** a thrall was property at law. The Gulathing law (written 12th century, older in substance) has
the seller answer for a month for hidden faults in a thrall he sells, the falling sickness among them `(?)`.
Freeing a thrall was a good deed that earned a rune stone: at Horning in Jutland, about 1000, Toki the smith
raised a stone for the man who gave him gold and freedom.

**For the game**: selling a Christian in a heathen port is the deed the Church remembers. Selling a heathen
Wend at Verdun is lawful and taxed. Ransoming is praised everywhere. The table's single flag cannot tell these
apart (section 11).

### 5.8 Frauds and tests

- **A free person sold as a slave**, or a Christian as a heathen. The nun at Hedeby proved herself by singing
  the psalms. Ask a captive to say the Lord's Prayer.
- **A stolen slave.** English law from Aethelstan (c. 930) wants any purchase over 20 d made in a town before
  the reeve or sworn witnesses, so the buyer can name his seller if the true owner turns up. Buy without a
  witness and you lose the slave and the price.
- **Hidden sickness.** Look at teeth, eyes, hands and feet; make them walk and lift. The north gave a month to
  return one with the falling sickness.
- **A runner.** Marks of the iron on ankle or neck mean one who has tried before.
- **Age lied about** in both directions.

### 5.9 How it changed

- **793-833.** A quiet trade under law: Wends through the Frankish marches to Verdun and Spain; a few captives
  from the first raids in Ireland and Northumbria. A Frisian skipper could carry them inside the realm.
- **834-864.** The raids come home. Captives from Frisia, the Seine and Ireland are sold at Hedeby and in the
  winter camps. Dublin is founded (841). Ransom becomes a trade of its own.
- **865-899.** The great armies sell what they take; Rimbert at Hedeby; 871, the 200 ships to Dublin.
- **900-954.** The height of the eastern trade: Slavs for dirhams at Bulghar, Ibn Fadlan in 922, Liudprand's
  Verdun, Raffelstetten's toll. The treaties of 911 and 944 set prices at Constantinople.
- **955-990.** Prague (965). The dirhams stop; the Rus turn to Constantinople. In the west the raids slacken.
- **991-1041.** The second raiding age in England: Wulfstan's sermon (1014), English sold over the sea, Rouen a
  market. Scandinavia turns Christian and the heathen buyer disappears from the North Sea.
- **1042-1066.** Bristol to Dublin is the last regular sea trade in English people. William of Malmesbury says
  Earl Godwin's wife Gytha shipped slave girls to Denmark `(?)`. Bishop Wulfstan is preaching against it.

### 5.10 The finds

- **Iron shackles, neck rings and chains** from the harbour of Hedeby and from Birka, and from Slav strongholds
  in Mecklenburg (Neu Niekohr) `(?)`; a neck chain from an Irish crannog `(?)`.
- **The Horning rune stone** (DR 58): a freedman's thanks.
- **The dirham hoards**: silver that slaves and furs bought, from the 780s to about 1010.
- **Domesday Book**: the slaves counted manor by manor, and the tolls on a man at Lewes.

15. **`marten.made`** lacks prussia (Adam: the Sembi sell marten for wool cloaks), finns, rus_volga (it is the
    Bulgars' money) and ireland (the Chester skins). **`marten.wanted`** lacks mercia_west with the reeve's first
    purchase. `marten.note` is empty: it should carry Ohthere's fifteen skins and the 30-shilling robe.
16. **`otter.made`** lacks norway_north (Ohthere's otter coat). **`beaver.made`** lacks saxony, rhineland,
    prussia and wendland at more than 1; **`beaver.wanted`** lacks spain. Beaver has no `season`, `cheap` or
    `dear`, though it is a winter pelt like the rest.
17. **`sable.wanted`**, **`beaver.wanted`**, **`squirrel.wanted`** lack khazaria and rus_volga as the places
    that buy to sell on, which is how the Rus sold them.
18. **`amber.made`** lacks wendland (Wolin, the Pomeranian coast). **`amber.wanted`** lacks the places with
    workshops that had no beach of their own: svealand (Birka), vestfold (Skiringssalr), schleswig (Hedeby),
    ladoga, ireland (Dublin, from the 10th century), northumbria (York), and rus_volga (al-Muqaddasi). As it
    stands the main real buyers of raw amber do not want it.
19. **`amber_beads.made`** lacks ladoga, svealand, wendland, ireland.
20. **`amber.value` 30 for 10 kg** has no evidence either way (3.4), but beside `amber_beads` at 24 d for 0.3 kg
    the working multiplies the price twenty-seven times by weight, which is too much. Raw amber at 60 to 90 a
    sack, or beads at 12, would be more believable. Mark both as guesses.
21. **`walrus_ivory`**: no `season` (summer hunt; cheap [8, 9, 10] in the north), no word that it is shipped in
    the skull. `made` lacks the White Sea road (the "fish teeth" at Bulghar: rus_volga 1). `wanted` lacks
    caliphate, schleswig, jutland, ireland, northumbria. Sources lack Star 2018 and al-Muqaddasi.
22. **`captives`**: no `season`, `cheap` or `dear` (cheap [8, 9, 10], dear [3, 4, 5]: the seasons document already
    says cheapest in September). `made` lacks the raided lands by age: england, frisia, neustria (834-900 and
    again england 991-1016), and finns. `wanted` lacks schleswig (Hedeby, the best-attested market of all),
    meuse (Verdun), rhineland (Mainz, Koblenz), italy (Venice), and the English regions themselves (one in ten
    of Domesday's people). `keeps` is 0 but the note says they eat: the hold rules must charge stores for them.
23. **No way to tell a Christian captive from a heathen one**, which is the whole of the law (5.7). Either two
    goods (`captives_christian`, `captives_heathen`) or a tag on the lot. Without it the `law` field cannot be
    right in any zone.
24. **No ransom.** The best-attested prices (Rimbert, the treaties, Saint-Denis) are ransoms. The effect verb
    `commission` or `passenger` could carry it; the goods table cannot.
25. **Seasons do not move with distance.** Furs have `cheap` [3, 4, 5]. That is right at Birka and Ladoga and
    wrong at Dorestat, where the winter's skins arrive at midsummer. The same for amber and ivory. The table
    needs either a lag by region or a `cheap`/`dear` pair per made region.
26. **`reindeer_hides.wanted`** has only vestfold and jutland; Ohthere's tribute shows them as wealth in the
    north but nothing shows a market south of Hedeby. Fine as it is; noted so nobody raises it.
27. **Regions.** There is no Bohemia (Prague, the slave market of 965) and no White Sea. Prague matters only
    overland and can stay out. The White Sea is reached through `norway_north` and `rus_volga`, which is enough.

### 10.3 What the table cannot say at all

- **Change by age.** One `made` and one `wanted` per good for 273 years. The four trades here change more than
  any others: furs turn from east to west about 970, ivory demand rises tenfold after 950, the captive supply
  moves with the raids. `ages.md` says "what else the ages ask of the tables"; this is the largest case. A
  per-age override (`"ages": {"5": {"wanted": {...}}}`) would do it.
- **Supply that is a few pieces.** `stock_min` 1 and a stock reckoned from value let a market hold dozens of
  tusk pairs. A `rare` flag (stock of 0 to 3, renewed once a season) is wanted for ivory, sable, black fox.

## 11. `trade_rules.json` compared

1. **The `captives` law treats all captives alike and has no dates.** "No export" from year 0 in both the
   Frankish and English zones. In truth: (a) Francia from 779: no sale beyond the march, and sale only before
   bishop or count; heathen captives lawfully bought and sold inside the realm (charters c. 825; Meaux 845).
   (b) England: Ine's rule (c. 690) covers only one's own countrymen; the general ban on selling Christians out
   of the land is 1008, repeated c. 1020, and broken at Bristol until the 1060s. (c) Nothing at all in the
   weighed-silver lands until they turn Christian. The rule should read "no Christians to heathens or out of the
   land", with `from` 779 for the Franks and 1008 for the English, and needs the tag of 10.2 item 23.
2. **No tolls by the head.** Every toll is a flat share of value by zone. The period's slave tolls are per
   person: Raffelstetten (903-906), Lewes (4 d, before 1066), Koblenz. A `per_unit_d` toll on a good would hold
   them.
3. **The weighed-silver zone has `toll` 0.0.** The file says "to come". It should come: Godfred moved the
   merchants of Reric to Hedeby in 808 for the dues; Birka had a king's reeve; Ohthere's tribute is a tax in
   furs. A king's share at Hedeby and Birka from 808 is the minimum.
4. **No right of first purchase.** Chester's marten rule (before 1066) is the model: the reeve buys what he
   wants first, at his price, and the fine for selling round him is 40 s. A `rule: "preempt"` on marten in
   mercia_west, and more widely on ivory and falcons at a king's town, would be true and a good card.
5. **No price cap.** The capitulary of 808 caps the marten or otter robe at 30 s and the cloak at 20 s and 10 s
   in the Frankish zone. The 794 grain caps are in the goods notes; neither is in the rules as a law the market
   obeys.
6. **No witness rule.** English law from c. 930 wants purchases over 20 d made in a town before witnesses. It
   bears on every dear good here and most on captives and livestock. `ages.md` (age 4: "trade is lawful only in
   a town, before a reeve") already asks for it.
7. **The zones have no dates.** The dirham zone (ladoga, rus_volga, khazaria) runs for ever, but the dirhams
   stop between about 970 and 1015 and Itil is destroyed c. 965-969. After that Rus reckons in fur-money (the
   kuna, a marten skin) and western pennies. The weighed-silver zone likewise: Hedeby strikes coin from c. 825,
   Dublin from 997, Sigtuna from c. 995, and Cnut's Denmark and Harald's Norway have royal pennies.
8. **No fur-money.** Marten skins were money at Bulghar (Ibn Rusta) and in Rus (the kuna). A fifth "coin",
   `kuna`, for ladoga and rus_volga from about 970, would be the truest thing in the table and is squarely this
   file's subject.
9. **The tenths are right.** Khazar tenth, Byzantine tenth, Frankish tenth: all attested (section 6). The Bulgar
   king's tenth is attested too (Ibn Fadlan) and is covered by the dirham zone's 0.10.
10. **`market.cheap` 0.85 and `dear` 1.2** give a seasonal swing of about 40 % from bottom to top, inside the
    20-60 % of DECISIONS no. 16 but at the weak end. For furs the difference between a prime winter skin and a
    summer one is more like half the price. A per-good swing, or a quality grade on furs, would serve better
    than one number for all goods.
11. **`market.stock_value` and `appetite_value`** scale stock by value, so cheap bulky goods and tiny dear ones
    behave alike. For ivory, sable and amber lumps the real rule is "a few, when a ship has come". See 10.3.
12. **`start.coins` has 6 dirhams** at Dorestat in June 793. Dirhams are barely known in Francia then and the
    Frankish mints melted foreign coin; the first reach Ladoga about that decade `(?)`. It is a nice hint of the
    east but it is early. Outside my subject; noted because the fur trade is what brings them.
13. **The English penny at 1.2 g** (`unit_d` 0.706) is the old light penny. Offa's heavy penny of c. 792 is
    about 1.45 g, and it matters here because it sets what "a pound for a slave" means in d. Outside my subject.
