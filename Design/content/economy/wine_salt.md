# The trade in wine and salt, 793 to 1066

Written 6 Oct 2026 for the word on the item, the market panels and the cards. One file, two goods: the lord's drink
and the Mass's, and the stuff no slaughter, no cheese and no herring can do without.

**How to read the marks.** A key in square brackets is a source in section 17. `[D:port]` is this project's own
dossier for that port (`Design/content/dossiers/<port>.md`), which gives its own references; I have relied on those
and not re-read them. `(?)` after a fact means I wrote it from memory and could not check it this session: the web
search was shut for the whole session and most Wikipedia pages I fetched had nothing on the period. So this paper
rests on three things, in this order of trust: the project's dossiers; the few pages that did answer (the
Raffelstetten tariff, the Dutch page on peat salt, the French page on Guérande, the Capitulare de villis extract);
and what I know of the literature, marked `(?)`. Nothing here is quoted from a modern translation. Where words stand
in quotation marks they are from a dossier's own rendering or from a public-domain book named beside them.

**The short of it, for the game.**

1. Wine is the best cargo a small boat can carry out of the Rhine: dear for its weight, wanted by every church and
   every hall, and it flows one way, downstream and north. But a real trade cask is bigger than the whole boat can
   bear. The first boat carries wine in small casks, skins and tall Badorf jars.
2. Salt is the worst cargo for a small boat and the best for a big one: a full load of 660 kg is worth less than one
   small cask of wine. It moves in bulk, by river barge and by ships that came out with grain. For the player it is a
   filler, a short hop, or a thing to carry to a fishing shore in the herring weeks.
3. Almost no price for either survives. What there is: a measure of wine for a penny in a market by the Loire about
   838; four pennies of wine for a party of women at Dorestad about 860; a law of 806 against buying at the vintage
   to sell at double or triple; a wagon of Droitwich salt paying a shilling toll and a pack-load a penny about 890.
4. The table has the wine about right and the salt mostly wrong: the long-haul salt trade from the Loire to Norway
   and the Baltic that `goods_793.json` assumes is a thing of the 14th century, not of ours. See section 16.

## 1. Wine: where it was made and by whom

**The vine's northern edge** ran about from the Loire mouth by Paris and Laon to the Meuse at Huy and Liège (?) and
the Rhine at Bonn and Cologne. North of that line nothing worth shipping grew. Frisia "pours neither oil nor wine":
Alcuin's own joke about Utrecht, where he was fed honey, porridge and butter [D:dorestad, Alc]. England had a few
vines: Bede says so in his first chapter (?), and Domesday counts some forty-odd vineyards, most of them newly
planted by Normans (?) [DB]. Everything north and west of the line had to be shipped.

**The Rhine** was the north's vineyard:

- Alsace (the "Vosges" of the poets) and the Palatinate, Rheinhessen round Worms and Mainz, the Rheingau, the Middle
  Rhine down to Bonn, the Moselle from Trier down, the Ahr (?). The casks found at Dorestad are of silver fir and of
  oak from the Hessian and south German Rhine: "wine from south of Mainz" [D:dorestad; D:mogontia].
- Mainz had "vineyards all round" [D:mogontia, Jacob]. Its river front had long plots with jetties where Frisian
  traders lived; in 886 the fire took "the best part of the city, where the Frisians lived" [D:mogontia, AF 886].
- Cologne was the loading place for the lower river: Badorf ware and its tall relief-band jars, used to ship wine
  and oil, went out from there [D:colonia].

**The Seine and its rivers:** the Paris basin (the slopes round Paris, Argenteuil, Suresnes (?)), the Marne and
Champagne round Reims and Épernay (?), the Oise round Laon (?), the Yonne and Auxerre (?). Rouen itself grew none:
"the vine does not thrive at Rouen; the land bears wheat and rye" [D:rotomagus, IY]. The wine Rouen sold was the
Paris basin's, come down river [D:rotomagus].

**The Loire:** Orléans, Tours, Angers, Nantes (?). At Déas, the Noirmoutier monks' mainland house south of the Loire,
there was a wine-seller and an ale-house by the monastery market about 838 [D:herio, E I.71].

**Aquitaine:** Bordeaux's fame for wine is Roman. For 793 to 1066 the dossier found no text that names wine shipped
from Bordeaux at all; one charter gives "lands with vines" to Holy Cross before 977 [D:burdigala]. The great
Bordeaux wine fleets come after 1152 and are not in this game [D:burdigala].

**Who made it.** Not free farmers selling at a market. Wine was made on the estates of the king, the bishops and
above all the abbeys, by tenants who owed work in the lord's vineyard and a share of their own vintage:

- **The king's estates.** The Capitulare de villis (about 800) orders the stewards to look to the vineyards, to keep
  the wine in good vessels, to see that the presses are clean and that nobody treads the grapes with his feet, and to
  carry the king's wine in good casks bound with iron, not in skins (chapters 8, 48 and 68 (?) by number) [CV]. The
  steward's yearly account lists "the mulberry wine, cooked wine, mead, vinegar, beer, and wine, new and old" [CV,
  Robinson 1904, read this session].
- **The abbeys.** Saint-Germain-des-Prés (its estate book of about 825 lists thousands of tenants and their dues in
  wine [D:parisius, Irminon]); Saint-Denis, which "sold its grain and spare wine" at its own fair [D:parisius];
  Prüm, whose tenants at Retersdorf near Königswinter "pay in wine and owe a trip with a ship twice a year to St Goar
  or Duisburg" (estate book of 893) [D:diusburg, PU]; Lorsch, Fulda, Wissembourg and Saint-Gall with their Rhine and
  Alsace vineyards (?); the abbeys of the north (Corbie, Saint-Bertin, Saint-Vaast, Lobbes, Werden, Echternach),
  which owned vineyards far to the south for no other reason than to have wine (?) [Verhulst].
- **The carriers.** An abbey moved its own wine by its tenants' carrying service, by cart and by boat, and sold the
  rest. The men who took it beyond the estate were Frisians above all on the Rhine, the abbeys' own traders
  ("Saint-Germain's own traders" [D:parisius, T 81]), the "city's merchants with their own boats" at Paris
  [D:parisius, AB 861], Mainz men, and from the mid-10th century Jewish long-distance traders at Mainz [D:mogontia].

**The poet's witness.** Ermold the Black, a cleric in exile at Strasbourg about 826 to 828, wrote a poem for King
Pippin in which the Rhine and the Vosges (Alsace) argue. The Vosges boasts of its wine and timber. The Rhine answers
that without the river the wine would lie unsold in the cellars: it is the river that carries it to the Frisians and
the men of the sea, who pay for it with cloaks dyed in many colours (?) [Ermold]. This is the wine trade of Dorestad
in one picture: wine down, Frisian cloth up. (I could not fetch the poem; the gist is well known but the wording
above is mine and unchecked.)

## 2. Wine: where it was wanted and why

1. **The Mass.** No wine, no Mass: the Church allowed nothing else in the cup (?). Every minster, abbey and mission
   church north of the vine line needed it all year, a little at a time, and paid whatever it cost. This is steady,
   small, sure demand in every Christian port: Utrecht, Dokkum, York, Lindisfarne, Jarrow, Iona, and after the
   missions Hamburg, Ribe (church about 860), Hedeby (church about 850), Birka.
2. **The lord's table.** Wine marked rank. Ale and mead were everyone's [BRIEF rule 2]. A king's hall, a bishop's
   guest house, a jarl's feast wanted wine, and the cups and jugs to match: Rhenish glass beakers, Tating jugs with
   their tin-foil crosses [goods table]. The Norse had the word (vín) and the idea: of Odin in his hall it is said
   that he lives on wine alone (Grímnismál 19; Bellows 1923 has "But on wine alone does the weapon-decked god, Othin,
   forever live" (?), wording to check before it is shown).
3. **The sick and the dying.** Wine was medicine and the last sacrament. Rimbert tells of Frideburg, a rich Christian
   woman at Birka in the years when the town had no priest: she bought wine and kept it by her in a jar, so that it
   could be put in her mouth at her death in place of the sacrament [VA 20] (?). So wine was on sale at Birka about
   850, to a woman with silver. Her daughter Catla took her alms to Dorestad; there four pennies were spent on wine
   to refresh the women who had gone round the holy places with her, and the purse was found full again, less the
   four [D:dorestad, R20].
4. **Raiders.** In 865 two hundred Northmen came up to Paris for wine, found none, and went back unharmed; in 866 part
   of the 4,000 pounds paid to the Northmen on the Seine was paid in wine [D:parisius, AB 865, 866].
5. **The English.** Wine came to Lundenwic "in silver-fir casks from the Rhine" [D:lundenwic]; to Ipswich, where 87
   shallow wells are known, many lined with old wine casks, one of oak that matches the Mainz area [D:gipeswic]; to
   Hamwic from the Seine, with the black and grey table ware of the Rouen country that is 18% of all the pottery
   there [D:hamwic]. English and Frisian merchants went to Saint-Denis for it [D:parisius, T 44, T 55]. About 1000
   the men of Rouen brought wine and blubber-fish to London [D:lundenwic; D:rotomagus, LT].
6. **The Irish and the western Britons.** Before 700 wine came from western Gaul with the pottery called E ware (?).
   For our years the texts are thin and late: a saint's Life has Gaulish merchants selling wine on the Shannon (?),
   and the 12th-century "War of the Irish with the Foreigners" makes the Norse of Dublin and Limerick pay Brian a
   tribute in wine about 1000 (?) [Cogadh]. Wine reached Ireland; how much, and from which river, is not known.

Not wanted: wine has no market among people who have never tasted it and have no church. In 793 that is the Baltic's
east shore and the far north. The table's "keenly wanted" for all of Scandinavia is too even (section 16).

## 3. Wine: the routes and the ships

**Down the Rhine.** Alsace and Worms to Mainz; Mainz to Cologne past the Bingen rocks and St Goar, where a skipper
who did not stop to pray lost a man or struck [D:mogontia, FCT]; Cologne to Duisburg, Xanten and the fork; the Lek or
the Kromme Rijn to Dorestad. Downstream on the current, with sweeps; upstream hauled from the bank by men, which is
why "the wine trade runs one way" [ships.md 578]. Wine goes down, and what comes up must be worth much for little
weight: cloth, silver, furs, silk.

**From Dorestad** (to 863; after that from Tiel, Deventer, Utrecht and straight from Cologne):

- north by the Vecht and the Almere to Medemblik and the terp coast, then outside or inside the islands to the Ems,
  the Weser, the Elbe, Ribe, and over the neck of Jutland to Hedeby; from Hedeby by the Baltic to Birka;
- west down the Lek to the sea and over to the Thames (Lundenwic), the Orwell (Ipswich), the Humber (York);
- south by Walcheren to the Scheldt, Ghent, Quentovic.

**Down the Seine.** Paris and Saint-Denis to Rouen; Rouen to Hamwic, to London, to Quentovic. Merchants came to the
fair "to buy wine in the ports and on the rivers" [D:parisius, T 55].

**The Loire and the Bay.** Tours and Angers to Nantes; from Nantes by Breton shipmen round the Breton coast (?).
Nothing in our years proves a regular wine run from the Loire to Ireland; it is likely and unproved.

**The ships** (see `Design/content/ships.md`): on the rivers, flat barges and the Utrecht kind of boat; at sea, the
Frisian coaster and from the 10th century the hulk and the big Norse cargo ship. A trade cask of the size found at
Dorestad and Hedeby could only be rolled aboard a ship with a hold, down planks or under sheers; it was a
barge-and-ship thing from start to finish. The small boat's wine went in what a man can lift.

## 4. Wine: the year

| When | What happens | For the price |
|---|---|---|
| September, October | The vintage, south first. Pressing. Tenants deliver their share. | Old wine is running out and going sour: dear, and bad. |
| Saint Denis (9 Oct) to about Saint Andrew (30 Nov) | The fair at Saint-Denis: the abbey sells, Frisians and English buy [D:parisius]. | The year's great buying on the Seine. |
| Martinmas (11 Nov) | The new wine is fit to drink and comes down the Rhine [D:mogontia; D:diusburg]. Martinmas and Easter are "the Frisians' terms" in Prüm's book [D:diusburg, PU]. | Cheapest of the year at Mainz, Cologne, Dorestad. |
| Winter | The sea is shut. The Rhine can freeze (thirty days in 821/22) or flood [D:mogontia]. Wine bought in November lies in a storehouse until spring. | Cheap at the river; nothing reaches the north. |
| Easter | The great feast; every church needs wine; the season opens and the first ships cross. | Dearest in England and the north just before the first ship; that ship makes the best profit of the year. |
| Summer | Wine in wood, without sulphur, in a warm hold, turns. By high summer last year's wine is thin or sour. | Dear where it is still good; sour wine sells as vinegar. |

Two things follow for the game. First, the wine year is two prices, not one: cheap at the source after Martinmas,
dear at the far end at Easter, and the winter lies between them (the season arbitrage the economy design already
names, `economy_and_trade.md` lines 160 and 441). Second, wine is not a thing that keeps a year and a half. The
king's account knows only "wine, new and old" [CV]: this year's and last year's. A cask from the vintage before last
is vinegar.

## 5. Wine: units, packing, and what the small boat carries

**Measures.** Wine was sold by the *modius* and the *sextarius* (sester), sixteen (?) sextarii to the modius, and at
the ale-house by the sextarius and the half [D:herio, E I.71]. How big they were is not known for sure. The Roman
sextarius was about half a litre; the Carolingian measures after the reform of 793/4 were larger, and scholars'
figures for the wine modius run from under 20 litres to about 68 (?) [Verhulst]. Use the names; do not print litres
in the game.

**Containers that are proved.**

| Container | Evidence | Size | Full weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| The great cask (Latin *tunna*, *cupa*) | Some 650 wells at Dorestad lined with Rhenish wine casks, of trees felled 685 to 835 [D:dorestad]; cask wells at Ipswich [D:gipeswic], a silver-fir cask head on the Strand at Lundenwic [D:lundenwic], casks in wells at Hedeby (?) | Staves about 2 m long and more, some 0.7 m and more across (?): 500 to 800 litres and upward (?). The project's own note gives 3 m by 0.66 m for Dorestad (economy_and_trade.md line 52). | 700 kg to a tonne |
| The small cask (*barriclus*) | "Good casks bound with iron" for carrying the king's wine [CV 68 (?)]; Charles the Bald's body was put in a pitched barrel wrapped in hides in 877 [D:parisius, AB 877] | Not known; a size two men lift | 60 to 120 kg (?) |
| The skin (*uter*) | Forbidden for the king's wine [CV 68 (?)], which shows it was the common way; a goat's skin, pitched inside | 20 to 40 litres (?) | 25 to 45 kg (?) |
| The tall jar (relief-band amphora, Badorf ware) | Up to 70 cm tall, "used to ship wine and oil" [D:colonia]; found at Medemblik [D:medemelacha], Dorestad [D:dorestad] | Perhaps 30 to 60 litres (?) | 50 to 90 kg with the jar (?) |
| The saddle-keg | London, about 1000: the Emperor's men give "two kegs of vinegar" among their dues [D:lundenwic] | A keg a pack horse carries | 40 to 60 kg (?) |
| The jug | Tating ware, black with tin-foil crosses: for the table, not for freight [goods table] | A litre or two | |

**The Gislinge boat: 660 kg beside her four men** [ships.md 4.3].

- One real Dorestad cask: no. It weighs more than all she can carry, and there is no way to get it into her.
- The table's cask ("about 100 litres", 115 kg): five (575 kg), with 85 kg left for bread and water. Five casks is
  500 litres, about two thirds of one real cask. At the table's 60 d that is a cargo of some 300 d: five eighths of
  the debt in one hold.
- In skins of about 32 kg: twenty (640 kg, some 600 litres). Skins stow better in a round-bottomed boat than casks
  do, but they leak, and they taint the wine with pitch.
- In tall Badorf jars of about 75 kg: eight (600 kg, some 350 litres), and they break.
- So the first boat is, as the ships paper says, "a pedlar by water: wine by the cask" [ships.md line 246]: she
  takes a few hundred litres to a minster or a hall that cannot wait for the big ships, and that is a true trade.

Wine in wood is heavy, low and safe as ballast if it is chocked: a cask that breaks loose in a seaway stoves a small
boat. A card, not a rule.

| about 838 | Déas, by the Loire mouth | At the market outside the monastery gate "a sextarius of wine cost one penny, unless I am mistaken"; pennies and halfpennies passed there | [D:herio, E I.71] |
| about 860 | Dorestad | Four pennies bought wine to refresh a party of women | [D:dorestad, R20] |
| 866 | The Seine | Part of the Northmen's 4,000 pounds paid in wine: wine reckoned as good as silver | [D:parisius, AB 866] |
| mid 11th c. | Koblenz toll | A ship from Duisburg gives a cake of wax and "a pennyworth of wine"; a ship from Cologne four pennies and wine | [D:diusburg, KZ] |
| about 1000 | London | Wine is named as the cargo of the men of Rouen; the dues of the Emperor's men include two kegs of vinegar | [D:lundenwic, C, Th] |

What that gives the game:

- The 806 law gives the **shape**: a price at the vintage, and double or triple in the dear months. The table's swing
  (0.85 cheap, 1.2 dear in `trade_rules.json`) is far too flat for wine.
- The Déas penny gives the **level**, loosely. If the sextarius there was the old half-litre, 100 litres cost about
  180 d over the counter; if it was one of the big Carolingian sesters of two litres or so, about 50 d. The table's
  60 d for a 100-litre cask "where it is neither made nor wanted" sits inside that range and can stand. Déas is in
  wine country and it is an ale-house price, so wine at the far end of a sea voyage should be well above it.
- The 806 law's own two pennies a modius is a vintage price at the press for grain or wine alike, and is an example,
  not a tariff.

No price of wine in England, Denmark or Sweden survives for these years (?).

## 7. Salt: where it was made and by whom

Salt was made in three ways, and the three products were not the same thing.

**A. Sun salt from sea pans (grey, coarse, cheap).** Only on the Atlantic coast from the Loire south, where the
summer is long enough.

- **The Bay of Bourgneuf and Noirmoutier.** The abbey of Saint Philibert had pans on the mainland: at Ampan from 677
  and at Scobrit, given in 839. Pans on the island itself in 793 are "only supposed" [D:herio, Ca3, P].
- **Guérande and Batz**, north of the Loire mouth: the cartulary of Redon has 54 gifts and transfers of rights over
  as many salt works in the 9th and 10th centuries, and the pans were already laid out much as they are today
  [Guerande, read this session; D:namnetis]. Saint-Aubin of Angers founded a priory at Saillé about 950 and soon
  owned pans [Guerande].
- Further south: the Saintonge and the Gironde (?). A Bordeaux charter dated 1027 knows a custom of three measures
  of salt at Saint-Macaire [D:burdigala].
- Made by tenants of abbeys and of the Breton lords, each with his few crystallising beds.

**B. Boiled brine from inland springs (white, fine, dear).** Wherever a salt spring rises.

- **Droitwich** (Saltwic, the Wich) in western Mercia: brine ten times as strong as the sea [Droit, read this
  session]. The king's from the first. In 716/17 King Æthelbald gave the church of Worcester a piece of ground south
  of the Salwarpe for salt works in exchange for six furnaces in two sheds on the north side [S102] (?). About 890
  Ealdorman Æthelred and Æthelflæd gave Worcester half their rights in the borough but kept for the king "the
  wagon-shilling and the load-penny" at Saltwic, "as they have always gone" [S223] (?). Domesday shows the brine
  pits, the lead boiling pans and furnaces, and salt rights tied to manors all over the west midlands, with renders
  in *mitts* of salt; Bromsgrove sent 300 cartloads of wood a year and had 300 mitts of salt for them [DB Worcs]
  (?).
- **The Cheshire wiches**: Nantwich (eight salt houses in Domesday [Cheshire, read this session]), Middlewich,
  Northwich, shared between King Edward and Earl Edwin before 1066, with a written custom of tolls and fines [DB
  Ches] (?).
- **The Saulnois in Lorraine**: Vic, Marsal and Moyenvic on the Seille, with salt works attested from the 6th
  century and Vic named in 754 and 777 [Vic, read this session]. The abbeys held pans here (Prüm's book of 893
  describes its works at Vic; Saint-Denis, Wissembourg, Gorze and the bishop of Metz had theirs (?)) [PU]. This, not
  the Alps, was the Rhineland's salt: down the Seille to Metz, down the Moselle to Trier and Koblenz (?).
- **Reichenhall** in Bavaria: springs worked since before Rome [Reich, read this session]; the dukes and the church
  of Salzburg held pans from about 700 (?). Its salt went down the Salzach, the Inn and the Danube: the salt ships
  of the Raffelstetten tariff [Raff].
- **Lüneburg**: first heard of in 956, when Otto I gave the toll of its salt works to the monastery of St Michael
  there (?) [DO I 183 (?)]. The two Wikipedia pages I read know nothing before the 12th century, so the date is from
  memory. From then on it is the salt of the Elbe mouth, Bardowick and in time the Baltic.
- **Halle on the Saale** (given with Giebichenstein to Magdeburg in 961 (?)), **Werl and Soest** in Westphalia (?),
  **Kołobrzeg** on the Wendish coast, whose name in Thietmar is "salt Kolberg" when its bishopric was founded in
  1000 (?) [Thietmar].

**C. Boiled from sea water, salt sand, salt mud or salt peat (grey to black, bitter, cheap, local).** Everywhere
else on the coast.

- **Peat salt of Zeeland and Frisia** (later Dutch *selnering*, *moernering*). The salt-soaked peat under the marsh
  clay was dug, dried and burned; the ash was mixed with sea water, the ash filtered out, the brine boiled and
  skimmed and boiled dry. A cubic metre of peat gave about 15 kg of salt. Roman traces on Walcheren; "in the 8th
  century the abbeys began" to make salt so, though the details are unclear; well recorded from about 1100; forbidden
  in Zeeland in 1515 because digging the peat let the sea in [Moer, read this session]. Lorsch held salt-boiling
  huts in Zeeland from 776 (?) [CL]. At Walcheren the dig shows "salt boiled from salt-soaked soil" and salt-boilers
  among the people [D:walichrum, KZ1]. At Medemblik burnt patches were read as peat-salt making and then doubted by
  the excavator [D:medemelacha].
- **The English coast.** Kentish charters give minsters ground for salt boiling with the wood to fire it: in 732
  King Æthelberht gave Abbot Dunn of Lyminge land by the Limen fit for boiling salt and a hundred and twenty
  wagon-loads of wood a year (?) [S23]. Domesday counts about 1,200 salt works, thickest on the coasts of
  Lincolnshire, Norfolk, Essex, Kent and Sussex (?) [DB].
- **Flanders** (the abbeys of Ghent held marsh at the Scheldt mouth [D:walichrum]) and the **Wadden coast** (?).
- **The north.** Norway, Denmark and Sweden made what little they made by boiling sea water over wood and by
  burning seaweed for "black salt" (?). There is no salt spring in Scandinavia. Fish was kept there by drying in the
  wind, not by salting: that is what stockfish is. The salt works of Læsø belong to the 12th century (?).

Fuel is the hidden cost of all boiled salt: a wagon of wood for a mitt of salt at Droitwich (?). A salt town eats a
forest. The sun costs nothing: that is why Bay salt was cheap, and why, centuries later, it beat the rest.

## 8. Salt: where it was wanted and why

- **The slaughter.** Beasts that cannot be fed through the winter are killed about Martinmas and salted: Bede's
  "blood month" [seasons line 48]. Every farm needs salt in November.
- **Butter and cheese**, May to August: salted to keep. Ælfric's salter, about 1000, says that nobody enjoys his
  dinner without him and that all the butter and cheese would be lost but for his craft (?) [Colloquy] (the
  Wikipedia page for the Colloquy was not found; from memory).
- **Fish.** Eels and salmon in barrels; herring above all. Herring will not dry; it must be salted within a day of
  the catch. Sea fishing for the market grows fast from about 1000 in England (the "fish event horizon") [barrett2008
  in the goods table], and with it the pull of the fishing shores on salt.
- **Hides**, salted green for the tanner; **the table**; **the church** (salt is blessed and put in the mouth at
  baptism (?)); **the ship's own stores**.
- **Who had none:** the uplands and every inland country far from a spring; the Slavs east of Bavaria (hence
  Raffelstetten: Bavarian salt out, wax, slaves and horses in [Raff]); the cattle country of Frisia in a wet summer
  when peat would not dry.

Salt is wanted by everyone and bought by few, because most coasts could make a poor salt for themselves. What was
traded far was the good white salt of the springs and the cheap grey salt of the Bay, and both went mostly inland,
up rivers, not across seas.

## 9. Salt: routes, ships, season, units

**Routes that are proved.**

- **Bay to Brittany, by sea.** About 838 to 840 Breton ships with yards, sails and anchors, a steersman and a bow
  lookout, came to Furca on Noirmoutier "to trade": they had brought grain and carried away "only salt" [D:herio, E
  I.81]. This is the one clear text for sea trade in salt in the whole of the game's years and waters: a short
  coasting run, grain out, salt home.
- **Bay to the Loire country, by river.** Salt went up the Loire in the abbeys' boats to Angers, Tours, Orléans
  and beyond; the kings freed such boats from toll by charter (Charlemagne for Cormery in 800: two boats free on the
  Loire and its rivers to fetch salt and other needs (?)) [Cormery].
- **Droitwich by the saltways**, on wagons and pack horses to the manors that owned a share of a pan, across
  Worcestershire, Gloucestershire, Herefordshire, Warwickshire and as far as Oxfordshire and Buckinghamshire (?)
  [DB]. Not by sea.
- **The Seille and the Moselle** to Metz, Trier and the Rhine (?).
- **The Danube**: salt ships from the Bavarian west down past Passau and Linz to the Moravian market [Raff].
- **Zeeland and Frisian salt** to Dorestad and up the rivers: "from the Frisian coast salt, fish, hides, furs,
  honey, dyes" [D:dorestad, N, T5].

**Not proved, and I think not true before 1066:** Bay salt carried to Norway, Sweden or the Baltic. That trade (the
Hanse's "Baie fleets") starts in the later 13th and 14th centuries (?). In our years Hedeby and the Wendish coast
had their own boiled salt and, from 956, Lüneburg's.

**Ships.** Salt is the cargo of the barge and the big-bellied coaster: dense, cheap, and ruined by a wet bilge. It
needs dry dunnage under it and hides over it. It was a return cargo (the Bretons' grain ships) or a river cargo.
Raffelstetten reckons a salt ship by her crew: "three men" make a ship of the ordinary kind for toll (?) [Raff].

**The year.**

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| May | The pans of the Bay are cleaned and flooded. Peat is cut and laid to dry on the marsh. |
| June to September | The sun makes the salt; it is raked and heaped. Peat is burned and the ash boiled. A wet summer makes little of either. |
| August to October | Shipping from the Bay [D:namnetis: "salt shipped August to October"]. Cheapest at the pans. |
| September to November | The herring; then the Martinmas slaughter. Dearest wherever it is used. |
| Ascension to Martinmas | The boiling season of the Cheshire wiches by the Domesday custom (?) [DB Ches]. |
| Winter | Spring salt can still be boiled if there is wood; sun salt and peat salt cannot be made at all. Stocks only. |
| Lent | The salt fish is eaten; no new demand for salt until the dairy starts. |

So salt at its source is cheapest at the end of summer and its buyers pay most in the two months after: the gap is
short, a few weeks, and it is a gap between places more than between seasons. (The project's own season paper says
the same, "salt at its dearest" in November; the goods table says the opposite. Section 16.)

**Units and packing.**

- By measure, not by weight: the *modius* in Francia; the *scafil* (bushel) at Raffelstetten [Raff]; in Domesday the
  *mitt* (of Droitwich; perhaps eight bushels (?)), the *sester*, the *amber* (in the south-east), the *summa* or
  *seam* (a pack-horse load) and the wagon [DB] (?). A buyer argues about heaped and level measure.
- Carried loose in the hold on boards, in wicker baskets (salt was drained in them (?)), in tubs and old casks;
  boiled salt also as hard cakes from the pan (?). I know of no evidence for sacks of salt in these years; a sack is
  a fair game unit but the word should be "load" or "measure".
- **The Gislinge boat's 660 kg:** thirteen of the table's 50 kg sacks; five or six pack-horse loads; about a dozen
  Frankish modii (?); about half a wagon. At the table's 3 d a sack the whole hold is worth some 40 d, less than one
  small cask of wine. One wave over the side and a tenth of it is brine.

## 10. Salt: prices and tolls

There is no market price for salt in any text of these years that I can name. There are tolls and rents, which show
what the lord thought a load could bear:

| Year | Place | What | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| about 890 | Droitwich | The king's due: a shilling on the wagon, a penny on the pack-load | [S223] (?) |
| 903 to 906 | The Danube at Linz | A salt ship from the west gives "three half measures", three bushels of salt, and then trades freely down river | [Raff], read this session |
| 903 to 906 | The Danube | Salt may be sold only at the lawful market places; the Slavs who come to buy pay by the load | [Raff] (?) |
| before 1066 | The Cheshire wiches | Toll on salt bought and carried away: a cart with four oxen or more 4 d, a horse-load 2 d, eight back-loads of a man on foot 1 d; less for men of the same hundred; a fine of 40 shillings for carrying it off untolled | [DB Ches] (?) all from memory |
| before 1066 | Droitwich | 300 cartloads of wood for 300 mitts of salt (Bromsgrove) | [DB Worcs] (?) |
| 1027 (charter's date) | Saint-Macaire on the Garonne | A custom of three measures of salt | [D:burdigala, GC-I] |

A guess at the level, to be tuned, not a fact: if a wagon paid a shilling and a toll was something like a tenth to
a twentieth, a wagon of white salt (half a tonne or more) was worth some 50 to 100 English pennies at the pit, which
is 5 to 10 pence for 50 kg. The Frankfurt price of 794 for wheat is 4 d the modius [frankfurt794 in the goods
table], and in later centuries salt cost about what wheat cost, measure for measure (?). So the table's 3 d (Bay), 4
d (Droitwich) and 5 d (spring salt) for 50 kg are of the right order; Droitwich's is, if anything, low.

## 11. Tolls, bans and laws on both

**Tolls.**

- **The king's tenth** at Dorestad, Quentovic and the Alpine passes: these three are the tolls that even a charter
  of freedom did not lift (Charlemagne for the church of Strasbourg, 775: free everywhere "except at Quentovic,
  Dorestad and the Clusae" (?)) [DKar 122 (?)]. Wine through Dorestad paid it.
- **Charters of freedom.** Abbeys got their boats freed: Saint-Germain-des-Prés in 779 (?), with a list of toll
  places that is a map of the trade (Rouen, Quentovic, Amiens, Maastricht, Dorestad (?)); Cormery in 800 for salt
  (?). A merchant sailing "for the abbot" sailed toll-free. This is the `show_charter` act made real.
- **The fair of Saint-Denis.** From the saint's Mass until the market ends every toll in the district of Paris is the
  abbey's: ships, harbours, carts, pack beasts, wheel, bridge and gate. In 753 King Pippin struck down the count's
  head tax of four pennies on every free merchant (five on a slave, who first had to swear he was one), because the
  merchants were staying away [D:parisius, T 55].
- **London, 889:** trade "on the public street or the trading shore" paid the king; the bishop of Worcester kept
  "the urn and scales" in his own court [D:lundenwic, S346]. **London, about 1000:** a small boat a halfpenny, a
  sailing ship a penny, a keel or hulk four; Rouen's men pay on wine and blubber-fish; the Emperor's men give at
  Christmas and Easter two grey cloths and one brown, ten pounds of pepper, five pairs of gloves, two kegs of
  vinegar [D:lundenwic, C, Th]. (The amount Rouen's wine ships paid is in the text, six shillings a great ship I
  think (?), and should be read from the source before it is used.)
- **Koblenz, mid 11th century:** toll partly in wine [D:diusburg, KZ].
- **Salt:** see section 10. Salt springs were the king's, everywhere: the Mercian kings at Droitwich, the king and
  the earl in Cheshire, the Bavarian dukes at Reichenhall, Otto at Lüneburg. Abbeys held pans by his gift.

**Laws.**

- **One measure.** The Admonitio generalis of 789 orders equal and just measures and weights everywhere (c. 74 (?));
  the synod of Frankfurt in 794 sets the new public modius and the grain prices [frankfurt794]. The Edict of Pîtres
  (864) complains of those who sell bread and meat by the penny and wine by the sextarius and cheat on both, and
  orders the measures checked against the palace's (c. 20 (?)) [pitres864].
- **No hoarding at the vintage.** Nijmegen, 806: buying wine or grain at the vintage to sell dear later is filthy
  gain (section 6). A law against exactly what the player does. It bound the conscience and the count; nobody is
  known to have been punished (?).
- **No Sunday markets** (capitularies of 809 and after (?)), except where they are old custom.
- **Drink.** Priests are not to go into ale-houses; nobody is to be forced to drink (several capitularies (?)).
  Fasting men drink no wine: a fast day and Lent cut the buying, and Easter brings it back.
- **The Mass wants true wine,** of the grape, with a little water added at the altar (?): a priest who is sold bad
  or false wine is a wronged man with a bishop behind him.
- **Bans on selling to the Northmen** (Pîtres 864) name arms and horses, not wine [trade_rules.json]. Wine could be
  sold to heathen men lawfully.
- **Salt** has no ban. Its law is the lord's due and the lawful market place [Raff].

## 12. Frauds, and how the goods were tested

**Wine.**

- **Water.** The oldest trick. Tested by tasting, and by the colour against the light in a glass beaker.
- **Old for new, sour for sound.** A turned cask topped up with new wine, or sweetened with honey and spice and
  sold as spiced wine. Tested by the nose at the bung and by asking to taste from the bottom of the cask, not the
  top, through the buyer's own reed or dipper (?).
- **Short measure.** A sester that is not the public sester; a cask not full to the bung (wine soaks into the wood
  and dries away, and a skipper's crew drinks). Tested with a dipping rod through the bung hole, and by the sound
  of a knuckle on the staves. Pîtres 864 is the law for it.
- **The small change.** At Déas a man was given a full measure and his change by mistake, came back to spend the
  halfpenny, swore by Saint Philibert that it was his own, and threw up the wine [D:herio, E I.71]. The period's own
  joke about a wine-seller's customer.
- **The wood.** A cask that held fish or tar taints the wine. A good cask is of silver fir or oak, bound with
  willow or hazel hoops; iron hoops are the king's order and a rich man's cask [CV].
- **The name.** Wine of a poor slope sold as wine of a good one. Nobody could test that but a man who knew the
  river.

**Salt.**

- **Damp.** Salt is sold by measure and by load, and wet salt fills the measure with water. Tested by the hand:
  dry salt runs through the fingers; damp salt cakes. A dealer keeps his salt near the fire; a cheat keeps it near
  the door.
- **Sand, ash and lime.** Peat salt carries ash and is grey to black and bitter; Bay salt carries the grey clay of
  the pan; white spring salt is clean. Tested by stirring a pinch into a cup of water: salt goes, sand and ash stay.
  And by the tongue: bitter means peat or seaweed.
- **Heaped and level.** Is the measure struck flat with a stick or heaped? The custom of the place decides, and the
  stranger does not know it. This is the commonest quarrel and the best card.
- **Passing peat salt as spring salt** by boiling it twice (?). A herring salter who is cheated this way loses his
  catch a month later, when the fish go soft; he remembers who sold it to him.

**The public tools.** The king's measure; the bishop's "urn and scales" at London (889); a reeve or toll-man as
witness to the sale (the Kentish law has warranty vouched "in the wic at the king's hall" [D:lundenwic]). These fit
the acts the game already has: `sample`, `haggle`, `witness`, `weigh_silver`.

The tests in this section are what a careful buyer of any age does; none is taken from a text of 793 to 1066 except
the Déas story, the king's rule on casks and the law of Pîtres. Treat the rest as invented in the period's manner.

## 13. How the trade changed, age by age

**793 to 834: the Frisians' river.** Charlemagne's peace. Wine from Alsace and Mainz to Dorestad and on to
England, Jutland and the Mälar; Frisian cloth back. Casks become wells at Dorestad by the hundred. Saint-Denis's fair
at its height. The monks of Noirmoutier hold their pans but flee the island every summer from 819. Tating jugs and
Rhenish glass in every northern market place.

**834 to 885: the raids.** Dorestad sacked from 834, gone by 863. Quentovic hit in 842, Nantes in 843, Bordeaux in
848 (?), Noirmoutier given up in 836 and a Northmen's base after. Walcheren a Dane's benefice from 841
[D:walichrum]. The Bretons take the Loire mouth and the salt country (851 (?)). Northmen on the Seine come to Paris
for wine (865) and are paid in it (866). The Rhine is raided to Cologne, Bonn and beyond in 881 to 882; Prüm is
burned twice; the Frisians' quarter at Mainz burns in 886. The wine still flows, because the churches cannot do
without it, but in fewer hands, by shorter stages, and behind walls. For the player: wine is dearer in the north and
the passage is the price.

**885 to 950: new towns.** The Rhine trade passes to Tiel, Deventer and Utrecht, and Cologne's own men begin to
carry. Badorf ware gives way to Pingsdorf about 900. Hedeby is at its greatest and its wells are lined with Rhenish
casks (?). Rollo's men hold Rouen from 911 and soon sell the Seine's wine themselves. Alan Twistbeard wins back
Nantes in 937 (?), and the Guérande pans fill the Redon charters. In England the burhs make markets; Droitwich's
dues are written down about 890; the Danube tariff is written at Raffelstetten in 903 to 906.

**950 to 1066: the kings' towns and the fish.** Lüneburg (956) and Halle (961) give the Saxon north its own white
salt. Kołobrzeg boils salt for the Wends (1000). The men of the Emperor (Cologne, Tiel, Bremen (?)) winter in
London and pay in pepper and vinegar; the men of Rouen bring the wine (about 1000). Alpert of Metz, about 1020,
calls the merchants of Tiel hard men who drink from dawn and hold feasts out of a common purse (?) [Alpert]. Sea
fishing for herring and cod becomes a business about 1000, and salt follows it to the fishing shores. Jewish wine
traders are at Mainz. Domesday (1086, looking back to 1066) is the first count of anything: about 1,200 English
salt works and forty-odd vineyards.

## 16. Against the tables: what `goods_793.json` and `trade_rules.json` lack or have wrong

### goods_793.json: wine

1. **The cask is not a real cask.** "cask (about 100 litres)", 115 kg. The casks in the wells of Dorestad and
   Ipswich are five to eight times that. Keep the 100-litre unit (it is the only wine a Gislinge boat can load)
   but call it a small cask or a keg, and say in the note that the big ships carry tuns. Five fit in 660 kg.
2. **`keeps: 540` (Rhenish), 450, 400 are too long.** Wine in wood without sulphur is new or old and then sour.
   I would give 330 to 365 days and let it turn to vinegar instead of vanishing.
3. **No vinegar.** It is in the king's accounts [CV] and it is a toll payment at London about 1000. It is what a
   wine cargo becomes, and it has buyers (kitchens, the sick, tanners (?)).
4. **Cheap and dear months.** `cheap [10, 11, 12]`: the vintage is September and October but the new wine is not
   sold before Martinmas; `[11, 12, 1]` is truer. `dear [6, 7, 8]`: the project's own season paper has wine
   "dearest at Easter" (line 51); the two files disagree. I would set `dear [3, 4, 8, 9]`: Easter, and the weeks
   before the vintage.
5. **One swing for all goods** (0.85 and 1.2 in `trade_rules.json`). The law of 806 speaks of a price doubling and
   tripling between the vintage and the dear months. Wine needs its own, wider swing.
6. **`rhenish_wine.wanted` is too even.** Jutland, Schleswig, Svealand, Vestfold and western Norway all at 3. The
   evidence is good for Hedeby and Birka (Rimbert, Tating ware), fair for Ribe and Kaupang, and none that I know
   for western Norway in 793. Missing altogether: `saxony` (new churches after 804), `danish_isles`, `zeeland`,
   `rhine_delta` itself (Utrecht's churches drink it; the table makes it there at 2 but nobody wants it there),
   `flanders`.
7. **`frankish_wine` lumps the Seine and the Loire** and then sends both to Ireland at 3. The Seine's wine went
   to Hamwic, London and Quentovic; if any wine went to Ireland it was the Loire's or Aquitaine's. Split them, or
   take Ireland off the Seine. The fair's buyers (Frisians) are in at 1, which is low for the people the charter
   of 753 names.
8. **`aquitaine_wine` has no evidence in these years.** The Bordeaux dossier found no text of 793 to 1066 for wine
   shipped from Bordeaux. `wanted: dalriata 2` rests on the E-ware trade, which ended about 700 (?). Mark the row
   `(?)`, lower its strengths, and end or weaken it after the sack of 848.
9. **No word on who may buy.** Wine is the lord's and the Mass's drink [BRIEF rule 2]; the table treats it as a
   thing any market takes by appetite. A church or a hall in the port should be what makes the demand.
10. **Missing goods around wine:** empty casks (sold on for wells: a small true trade), the tall Badorf jar as a
    wine container (`badorf_ware` is only "storage jars and jugs"), spiced or cooked wine and mulberry wine [CV].
11. **`src` is thin:** only Lebecq and McCormick. Add Ermold, Rimbert's Life of Anskar, the Saint-Denis judgment of
    753, the Capitulare de villis, Prüm's estate book, IV Æthelred.

### goods_793.json: salt

12. **`sea_salt` is called "Bay salt".** That is the name of the Hanse's trade of the 14th and 15th centuries (?).
    Call it grey salt or sea salt of the Loire.
13. **Its buyers are wrong.** `wanted: norway_west 3, vestfold 3, svealand 2, ladoga 2, prussia 2, jutland 2,
    gotland 2, ireland 2`. Nothing in 793 to 1066 shows Loire salt going to any of them but perhaps Ireland. The one
    text has Bretons taking it home for grain; the charters have it going up the Loire. The table needs a Breton
    region (it has none), and the Loire's own upper country and Neustria as buyers.
14. **Its makers are wrong.** `zeeland: 1` puts peat salt under sun salt. Peat and marsh salt is a different good:
    dark, bitter, made in Zeeland and Frisia (and at Walcheren by the dig), cheaper, and unfit for butter. It needs
    its own row. The salt works of the English coast (about 1,200 in Domesday (?)) are in no row at all.
15. **Its months contradict the project's own research.** `cheap [9, 10, 11], dear [4, 5, 6]`. The season paper
    (line 48) says salt is "at its dearest" in November, and so does everything else. Set `cheap [7, 8, 9]`, `dear
    [10, 11]`. Spring is not a dear time for salt; nobody is killing or fishing.
16. **`keeps: 0` and `risk: ""`.** Salt keeps for ever on land and not at all in an open boat in rain. It needs a
    wet-cargo risk.
17. **`unit: "sack"`.** No evidence for sacks; the sources measure salt by the modius, mitt, bushel, pack-load and
    wagon. "Load" would be truer. 50 kg is a fair size.
18. **`brine_salt` goes to the wrong places.** `wanted: northumbria 2, ireland 2`. Droitwich salt went by wagon and
    pack horse to the manors of the west midlands and the Thames valley, not by sea. Its row has no season, no toll
    (the wagon-shilling and load-penny), and no word of the Cheshire wiches, which are the salt of the table's own
    port of Chester (`legacaestir`).
19. **`rock_salt` is wrong three ways.** Reichenhall's salt was boiled from brine; mining at Hallein belongs to the
    end of the 12th century (?). It went east down the Danube, not to the Rhine, so `made: rhineland 0.5` and
    `wanted: saxony 1` have no road between them. And the Rhineland's real salt, from Vic, Marsal and Moyenvic on
    the Seille, is missing. Replace the row with "spring salt" in two forms: Seille salt (made `rhineland` or a
    Moselle region, all years) and Lüneburg salt (`from: 956`, made `saxony`, wanted `schleswig`, `wendland`,
    `danish_isles`).
20. **Salt is not tied to what needs it.** `herring`, `salt_pork`, `butter`, `eels` and `salmon` are made without
    it. The truest thing the table could say about salt is that a herring shore with no salt has no herring to
    sell. A price shock on salt in the herring weeks would do it without new machinery.
21. **The values (3, 4, 5 d for 50 kg) are of the right order** but mean a full hold is worth 40 to 65 d. That is
    true, and it should be said in the note so nobody "fixes" it: salt is not the small boat's cargo.
22. **`herring.note` says "the great Scanian fairs come later"**: right. It could add that sea fishing for the
    market grows from about 1000.

### trade_rules.json

23. **Tolls are one fraction for a whole zone.** Frankish 0.10 is right for Dorestad, Quentovic and the passes.
    English 0.05 has no basis I know: the evidence is a toll by the ship (London: a halfpenny, a penny, four pence)
    plus dues in kind, and at the salt towns a toll by the load. A toll by the hull hits a small boat with a rich
    cargo lightly and a big salt ship hard, which is the truth of it.
24. **No toll in kind.** Three measures of salt from a salt ship (Raffelstetten), a pennyworth of wine (Koblenz),
    two kegs of vinegar (London). A toll man who takes a keg is a better card than one who takes a tenth.
25. **No charters of freedom.** The abbeys' toll-free boats are the best-recorded fact of the whole trade, and
    the game already has `show_charter`. The rules have no place for it. Note that the great charters still left
    Dorestad and Quentovic to pay.
26. **The weight zone has `toll: 0.0`.** Hedeby's king took dues from merchants: it is why Godfred moved the
    traders of Reric there in 808 (?). The note says "to come"; wine is the cargo it will fall on.
27. **No law on measures and none on hoarding.** Add the public measure (789, 794, Pîtres 864) as a law the
    toll man can use against a short cask, and Nijmegen 806 as a law the count can use against a man who bought
    the vintage to sell at Easter. Both are real and both bear on exactly what the player does.
28. **No fair toll.** At Saint-Denis from 9 October every toll in the district is the abbey's; the head tax of four
    pennies was struck down in 753 [D:parisius]. `fairs_793.json` has the fair but the rules do not say whose toll
    it is.
29. **`cheap: 0.85`, `dear: 1.2` for every good.** See 5. And the months belong to the good, not to the place:
    wine is cheap at Mainz in November and dear in London in March in the same year. A source-side and a
    buyer-side season would carry the whole wine game.
30. **Nothing spoils by turning.** `keeps` is a number of days and then nothing; wine should become vinegar and
    wet salt should become less salt.
31. **Regions.** No Brittany, no Moselle or Lorraine, no Champagne or Burgundy. The first carried the salt, the
    second made the Rhineland's, the third fed the Seine fair.
