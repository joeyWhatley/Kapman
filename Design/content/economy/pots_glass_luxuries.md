# Pots, glass, beads, silk, spices, books and relics, 793 to 1066

Written 6 Oct 2026 for the content pass. One file, one writer. It is research for the goods table, the market panel,
the cards and "the word on the item". Nothing here changes a data file: section 16 lists what the tables lack or
have wrong, for whoever owns them.

**How to read it.**

- `(?)` marks a fact I hold from memory or from one weak witness and could not check tonight. Treat it as a lead.
- "Checked" in the source list means I read the page on 6 Oct 2026. Everything else is from the standard books,
  cited by author and year, and is as good as my memory of them.
- Money: d is the Frankish penny of 793/4 (about 1.7 g of silver), 12 d to the shilling of account, 240 d to the
  pound. An English mancus is 30 pennies. A dirham (about 2.9 g) is about 1.7 d by the game's own table. A Byzantine
  gold nomisma (about 4.4 g of gold) is 30 to 40 d at a gold to silver rate of 12 to 1 (?).
- Weights: the Carolingian pound after 793 is about 408 g, twelve ounces of 34 g. The Byzantine litra is about 324 g.
  In the lands of weighed silver the ore is about 24 to 26 g and the mark about 200 g (?).
- Where I give a price with no document behind it, it says "guess".

**The short of it.** These seven goods are two different trades in one hold.

1. **Pots and common glass** are cheap, heavy or bulky, and breakable. They travel as the tail of the wine trade:
   the Rhine sends wine in casks and big jars, and jugs and beakers to pour it and drink it. A small boat fills up
   long before it grows rich on them. The profit is steady and small, and a hard landing eats it.
2. **Beads, silk, spices, books and relics** weigh almost nothing and are worth a boat. The hold is never the
   limit. The limits are the purse, the stock (a market has a few pounds of pepper, not a sack), the law, and above
   all knowing who wants the thing and whether it is what the seller says. This is where "knowledge is the treasure"
   bites hardest: a relic is worth exactly what its letter and its seal are worth.

## 2. Pottery

### 2.1 Where it was made, and by whom

**The Vorgebirge potteries (Rhineland).** A ridge of good white clay runs west of the Rhine between Cologne and
Bonn. Villages along it made pots for sale in bulk from the 6th or 7th century onwards: Waldorf first, then
Walberberg and Eckdorf from the 8th century, then Badorf, Kierberg and Pingsdorf (de.wikipedia, Badorfer Keramik,
checked). The makers were countrymen working kilns on church and royal land (?); nobody names one potter.

- **Badorf ware**, 8th to 10th century: pale yellow, wheel-thrown, hard, often with rows of roller-stamp marks.
  Small jugs, cooking pots, bowls. Its great piece is the **relief-band amphora**: an egg-shaped jar about 70 cm
  high with raised clay bands and no proper foot, made to carry wine and oil, not to be sold for itself
  (de.wikipedia, checked). The project's own notes give Badorf "almost three-quarters of the wheel-thrown pottery at
  Dorestad" (Design/economy_and_trade.md, from the Dorestad excavation reports).
- **Hunneschans ware**, the bridge between the two, about 875 to 925 (?): Badorf shapes with the first red paint.
- **Pingsdorf ware**, late 9th to 13th century: yellow, sandy to the touch, painted with red-brown commas and
  strokes. Bellied pots, beakers, jugs. Made at Pingsdorf, Badorf, Walberberg and a growing ring of places, in the
  end as far as Brunssum and Siegburg. Carried by the Rhine "up to England, Scotland, Scandinavia and the
  Netherlands" (en.wikipedia, Pingsdorf ware, checked).
- **Mayen ware**, from the Eifel beside the quern quarries: hard, grey-brown, gritty with volcanic sand. Cooking
  pots that stand the fire. It rides with the quernstones (?).
- **Paffrath ware**, from about 1000 (?): grey-blue round cooking pots from the east bank near Cologne, common in
  the Low Countries and at London in the 11th century (?).

**Tating ware.** Black, burnished jugs and a few bowls and beakers, with patterns cut from **tin foil** and stuck
on: diamonds, bands, and on many a cross. Late 8th and early 9th century. Named for Tating in Eiderstedt, where one
was found. **Nobody knows where they were made**; the Rhineland, the Eifel near Mayen and northern France have all
been argued (de.wikipedia, Tatinger Kanne, checked: "places of manufacture ... currently still unknown"). Whole ones
come from graves in Scandinavia; settlements give sherds. They are found from the Main to Birka: Dorestad,
Hamwic, Ribe, Hedeby, Kaupang, Birka, Staraya Ladoga (?). Whether the cross makes them Mass jugs or only fine wine
jugs is not settled. For the game: a jug for the lord's wine, and a thing a priest going north would want.

**The Meuse.** Huy made pottery through the Carolingian age (?). **Andenne ware**, glazed, begins about 1050 to
1075 (?) and belongs to the very end of the game.

**Northern France.** Grey and black burnished wares from the Seine valley and the north (the kilns at La Londe near
Rouen are known (?)) are the commonest foreign pots at Hamwic (Hodges 1981 (?)). **Beauvais** made red-painted
pots from the 9th century (?).

**England.**

- **Ipswich ware**, from about 720 into the 9th century; one kiln at Buttermarket worked about 800 to 850. Built in
  coils, finished on a slow wheel, fired in a kiln: "the first wheel-turned and mass-produced pottery in post-Roman
  Britain". Jars, cooking pots, hanging pots, a few big bottles and stamped pitchers. Thick in East Anglia, thin
  "from Kent, north to York, and west to Oxfordshire" (en.wikipedia, Ipswich ware, checked).
- **Thetford ware**, about 850 to 1100 (?): hard grey pots thrown on a fast wheel, made at Ipswich, Thetford and
  Norwich. The Danish settlement did not stop the potters; it gave them towns.
- **Stamford ware**, 9th to 13th century: fine white clay under a clear or yellow **lead glaze**, put on with a
  brush. Jugs, spouted pitchers, small bowls. "Widely traded across Britain and the near continent"
  (en.wikipedia, Stamford ware, checked). The first glazed English pots since Rome; goldsmiths also used the clay
  for crucibles (?).
- **Winchester ware**, glazed, about 950 to 1100 (?). **St Neots** (shelly) and **Torksey** wares in the Danish
  shires (?).

**The coast and the north.** Frisians and Saxons made their own hand-built round pots, some tempered with crushed
shell (?). Jutland and Sweden made rough hand-built pots at home. **Norway hardly used pottery at all**: the cooking
pot there is soapstone, and it sells south to Jutland and Hedeby. That is a good fact for the game: do not carry
cooking pots to Norway.

**The Wends.** Slavic pottery (the types called Feldberg, Fresendorf, Menkendorf) reaches Hedeby and Birka, and
from about 1000 its child, **Baltic ware**, is made all round the western Baltic, in Denmark and Sweden too (?).
Pots come west as well as go east.

### 2.2 Where it was wanted and why

A wheel-thrown, kiln-fired Rhenish pot is harder, thinner and more even than anything made on the coast. But the
plain truth of the finds is that **the pots follow the wine**. The relief-band amphora is a wine jar. The Badorf
jug pours, the Tating jug pours at the high table, the glass funnel beaker drinks. Where a hall has Rhenish wine it
has the pots; where it has only the pots, somebody once had the wine. At Dorestad the wine casks themselves
survive: old barrels of silver fir from the upper Rhine, sunk as well linings (Dorestad excavations, van Es and
Verwers (?)).

So the market for pots is the market for wine, one step poorer: the Rhine mouths and Frisia (where nearly all
table pottery is Rhenish), the marts of Jutland (Ribe, Hedeby), Kaupang, Birka, and in England the wics of the
east coast and London. In England the sherds are few: a wic took Rhenish pots for the Frisians who lived there,
not for the English (?).

### 2.3 Routes and ships

Down the Rhine by river boat, flat-bottomed, steered with a sweep, poled or towed going up. Cologne is the loading
place. Before 863 everything changes hands at **Dorestad**, where the Rhine and the Lek part; after Dorestad's end
the same trade lands at **Tiel** and **Deventer**, and at **Utrecht**. From there by the Vlie and the Wadden
shallows to Ribe and across the foot of Jutland by Hollingstedt to Hedeby, then the Baltic; or along the Frisian
coast and over to the Thames, the Orwell and the Humber. Frisians carry it: Frisian merchants are named at York
(c. 773, Altfrid's Life of Liudger), at London (679, Bede), at Mainz (886, Annals of Fulda: the part of the town
where the Frisian merchants live burned), at Birka (Rimbert).

### 2.4 Season

Potters dig clay in autumn, let the frost break it over winter, throw and fire from spring to autumn (?): a wet
kiln yard fires nothing. New pots are plentiful from early summer and cheapest when the wine boats load after the
vintage, from Michaelmas to Martinmas. They are dear on the coast in late winter, when the river has been shut by
ice or flood and the year's breakage has done its work. Nobody ships pots in a gale.

### 2.5 Units and packing

No document tells how pots were packed. From later practice and plain sense: nested where the shapes allow, bedded
in straw or bracken, in wicker baskets or loose in the bottom of the boat on brushwood. A small Badorf jug or
cooking pot weighs about 1 to 1.5 kg (guess from the sizes); a relief-band amphora empty about 15 to 20 kg and it
holds some 30 to 50 litres (guess).

**In the small boat.** Pots fill a hold before they load it. With straw, a round pot takes about 8 litres of
room for its 1.2 kg. If the boat has 1.5 to 2 cubic metres to give (guess), she carries about 150 to 200 pots
weighing 200 to 250 kg, and still has 400 kg of lift for something dense under them: querns, salt, iron. That is
how they really sailed: stone at the bottom, pots and glass on top. The game's "crate of 10" at 20 kg and bulk 2
gives 16 crates, 160 pots: the same answer.

### 2.6 Prices

**There is no price for a pot in any text of the age that I know.** That silence is itself the evidence: pots were
too cheap to write down. A guess for tuning: a plain Rhenish pot one farthing to one halfpenny at the kiln, a penny
on the coast, two in the Baltic; a Tating jug four to eight; a relief-band amphora is sold with its wine. For
scale, the public price of grain at Frankfurt in 794 is 4 d for a measure of wheat and 1 d for a measure of oats.

### 2.7 Tolls and laws

No law names pottery. It pays the king's tenth like any cargo at Dorestad and Quentovic. At London about 1000 the
toll is by the ship: a small boat a halfpenny, a boat with a sail a penny, a keel or hulk fourpence (IV Æthelred
2). A boat of pots pays the same halfpenny as a boat of pepper. That is worth a line in the game.

### 2.8 Frauds and tests

- **Ring it.** Flick the rim with a nail. A sound pot rings; a cracked one clacks. Every buyer of pots has always
  done this.
- **Fill it.** An underfired pot sweats and softens. Leave water in it overnight.
- **The tin on a Tating jug is tin, not silver.** New, it shines like silver. A seller in the north may let a
  buyer think what he likes. Tin foil peels; a jug with bare patches is worth half.
- **Seconds.** Kiln wasters, warped or with fire-cracks plugged with clay and soot, sold at the bottom of the
  basket. (All four: plain practice, not from a text.)

### 2.9 Finds that prove it

Dorestad (Badorf in bulk, relief-band amphorae, Tating); Hedeby (Badorf, Pingsdorf, relief-band amphorae, Slavic
pots); Ribe; Kaupang (Badorf and Tating sherds (?)); Birka ("pottery from the Rhineland" in the graves,
en.wikipedia Birka, checked; whole Tating jugs in several graves (?)); Hamwic (northern French wares above all);
Ipswich (its own kilns, and Rhenish imports); York and London (Pingsdorf and later Paffrath, 10th and 11th century (?)).

## 3. Glass

### 3.1 Where it was made, and by whom

Until about 800 almost all glass in the west was **made once and melted many times**. The raw glass was cooked from
sand and natron in Egypt and the Levant and came west as lumps; northern workshops only reworked it. When that
supply failed, about the time of Charlemagne, the glassmakers of the Carolingian lands learned to make their own
from sand and the **ash of beech and fern**: "forest glass", greenish and yellowish (en.wikipedia, Forest glass,
checked: "approximately 800 AD ... the supply of traditional raw materials was costly and sporadic"). So the
potash and the beech woods become part of the trade, and old glass becomes worth carrying.

- **Rhineland and Meuse, northern Francia**: the drinking glass of the north. In 793 the shapes are the **funnel
  beaker** (a tall cone, held in the hand or set in a stand), the older **palm cup**, squat jars, and bowls with
  twisted two-colour rods laid on (reticella). Workshops have been dug at royal and church sites: Paderborn,
  Lorsch, and a glasshouse in the woods at Kordel near Trier (?). At San Vincenzo al Volturno in Italy a monastery
  workshop of about 800 made lamps and window glass from old Roman glass (Hodges (?)).
- **Window glass** is a church good. Benedict Biscop sent to Gaul for glaziers for Wearmouth in 675 because the
  English did not know the craft (Bede, Lives of the Abbots 5). In 764 Abbot Cuthbert of Wearmouth and Jarrow
  wrote to Bishop Lul at Mainz asking for a man who could make glass vessels well, "because we are ignorant and
  helpless in that art" (Boniface and Lul letters, no. 116; my paraphrase). England in 793 still buys its glass and
  its glassmen.
- **Glass smoothers**: dark glass buns the size of a fist, for pressing linen. Common in the towns of the north
  (?). A humble, sure seller to women.
- **Raw glass**: broken vessels (cullet), and **mosaic cubes** (tesserae) picked out of ruined Roman and Italian
  buildings, blue, green, white, and gold-foil cubes. These go north in bags to the bead-makers (section 4).
- **The east**: from the 9th century Islamic glass made with plant ash comes up the rivers as beads, and a very
  few vessels reach Birka and Hedeby (?).
- **Lead glass**: in the 10th and 11th century, yellow and green rings and beads of lead glass are made in the
  German lands and among the Rus (Kiev), and are common in Baltic towns by 1000 (?).

A word on a mistake to avoid: the **claw beaker** belongs to the 5th to 7th centuries. Nobody makes one in 793.

### 3.2 Where it was wanted and why

A glass beaker in a hall says the same as the wine in it: this lord has friends up the Rhine. Glass goes into the
richest graves at Birka. Churches want lamps, windows, and now and then a chalice (though church councils of the
9th century forbid chalices of glass, horn or wood (?)). Bead-makers want any glass at all.

### 3.3 Routes, season, packing

It rides with the pots and the wine, the same boats, the same season. Packed in straw in baskets or in a cask
with the head knocked in (guess). A funnel beaker weighs about 100 to 150 g; thirty of them in a basket weigh less
than the basket. Glass is the lightest cargo by room: the game's bulk of 3 is fair.

**In the small boat**: a dozen baskets, three or four hundred beakers, would fill her and weigh 60 kg. No market
in the north takes four hundred beakers. A real load is one or two baskets on top of something else.

Raw glass is the opposite: dense, tough, no care needed. A 10 kg bag of cullet or cubes can sit in the bilge.

### 3.4 Prices

**None in any text.** A guess: a funnel beaker 6 to 12 d on the Rhine, two or three times that at Birka. A bag of
raw glass by weight, perhaps 1 to 2 d a pound (guess).

### 3.5 Laws

None on the trade. A church may not sell its treasure, and a merchant may not buy it (section 9).

### 3.6 Frauds and tests

- **Glass gems.** The age made glass to look like garnet, sapphire and emerald, and set it in brooches and book
  covers; a 10th-century craft book, the one that goes under the name of Heraclius, gives recipes (?). Sold honestly
  it is "glass stone"; sold as a gem it is a fraud. Test: a file or a hard stone scratches glass and not a gem;
  a true stone is cold on the lip and stays cold; glass has bubbles when held to the light.
- **Sick glass.** Forest glass with too much ash clouds and flakes in damp. A cloudy beaker bought cheap on the
  Rhine will not last a winter in a turf house.
- **Mended beakers**: a crack stopped with pitch. Hold it to the light and fill it.

### 3.7 Finds

Funnel beakers at Dorestad, Hedeby, Ribe, Kaupang, Birka (whole in graves), Helgo; reticella bowls from Valsgarde
to Borg in Lofoten (?); window glass at Jarrow and Wearmouth; the workshop at San Vincenzo; glass smoothers in
York, Dublin, Hedeby, Birka (?).

## 4. Beads (glass, stone, amber, jet)

### 4.1 Where they were made, and by whom

**The bead-makers of the northern marts.** From about 700 a bead-maker sat at the summer market of **Ribe** with a
small clay hearth, a bag of bought glass and an iron rod, and wound beads one at a time. The same craft is found
at Ahus in Skane, Paviken on Gotland, Helgo and Birka, Kaupang, and Hedeby (Feveile 2006; Sindbaek 2007). They did
not make glass; they melted what the Frisians brought: blue, white and green cubes from old mosaics, gold-foil
cubes (melted for their clear glass (?)), and broken beakers. The Ribe makers had fashions that can be dated to a
few years: blue beads with red and white trails, and the striped black and yellow "wasp" beads of the later 8th
century (?).

**The flood from the east.** In the last years of the 8th century the market changes. Cheap beads made in the
Caliphate by drawing a glass tube and pinching it into segments, some with silver or gold foil inside, arrive in
the Baltic in such numbers that the local bead-makers at Ribe all but stop (Sindbaek; the Northern Emporium
excavation at Ribe, 2017 to 2018 (?)). This is the same road and the same years as the first dirhams. **The game
starts in 793, on that hinge.** A Ribe bead-maker in 793 is a man watching his trade die.

**Stone beads.** **Carnelian** (red) and **rock crystal** (clear), cut round or with many faces, come from India
and Persia with the silver, a few before 800, many from about 860 to 970, and almost none after the silver stops
(Callmer 1977 (?); Noonan 1998 for the silver). They mark the eastern road: thick on Gotland, in Svealand and
among the Rus, thin in Denmark, rare in England.

**Amber** is gathered on the beaches of Jutland and above all of Prussia (Truso, Wiskiauten) and worked into
beads, pendants and gaming pieces at Ribe, Hedeby, Dublin and York (?). It goes east and south: the Caliphate
buys it.

**Jet** is picked from the cliffs at Whitby. Bede knows it: it is black, it drives off snakes when burned, and
when warmed by rubbing it holds what is put to it, as amber does (Ecclesiastical History 1.1). York works it in
the 10th century, and it goes to Dublin and Norway (?).

**Cowrie shells** from the Indian Ocean hang on bead strings on Gotland and in the east Baltic (?).

### 4.2 Where they were wanted and why

Everywhere, by women above all: a string between the two brooches on the breast shows what a household can spend.
Further north and east a bead is nearer to money. Ibn Fadlan saw the Rus on the Volga in 922: their most prized
ornaments are green glass beads, and "they string them as necklaces for their women" (en.wikipedia, Ahmad ibn
Fadlan, checked for those words); he adds that they pay **a dirham for a single bead** (the Risala itself; not on
the page I checked (?)). Beads are also the small change of the fur trade with the Finns and the Sami (?).

### 4.3 Routes and season

West to east until about 790: raw glass from Italy and the Rhineland by Dorestad to Ribe and the Baltic, made up
at the summer markets and sold on the spot. After about 790 east to west as well: finished beads up the Volga to
Staraya Ladoga, across to Birka and Gotland, and on to Hedeby and Kaupang. The eastern boats come home in late
summer and autumn; the beads are cheapest at Birka then and dearest in spring.

### 4.4 Units and packing

Strung on a thread, or loose in a leather purse and sold by count or by the handful. A glass bead weighs 1 to 3 g;
a string of a hundred about a quarter to a half kilo. **A whole year's bead trade for a small merchant fits in a
hat.** The boat does not notice it. The purse does.

### 4.5 Prices

- **A dirham a bead** on the Volga in 922, for the green ones the Rus liked best (Ibn Fadlan (?)). About 1.7 d.
  This is a far-end price for a favourite kind, among men heavy with silver.
- Nothing else. A guess for the rest: ordinary glass beads ten to twenty for a penny at Ribe, five for a penny at
  Birka, one or two for a penny among the Finns; a carnelian bead 1 to 3 d; a faceted crystal 3 to 6 d.

### 4.6 Frauds and tests

- **Glass sold as stone.** Red glass for carnelian, clear glass for crystal. Test: crystal is colder and harder;
  it scratches glass. Look down the hole: a stone bead is drilled from both ends and the bores meet a little off;
  a glass bead has a smooth round hole.
- **Amber**: true amber floats in strong brine and smells of pine when touched with a hot pin; rubbed on wool it
  lifts chaff. (Bede knows the rubbing.) Pressed resin and horn fail these.
- **Jet**: true jet is light, warm, and leaves a brown streak on a rough stone; shale and bog oak leave black or
  none.
- **Foil beads**: the gold and silver foil beads of the east hold a leaf of metal thinner than breath. A seller
  who weighs them as gold is a thief.

### 4.7 Finds

Ribe (workshop floors with tesserae, rods and failed beads, c. 700 to 800); Ahus; Paviken; Kaupang ("hundreds of
glass beads", the project's notes); Birka graves; Staraya Ladoga; the Hon hoard in Norway (c. 875, beads strung
with Frankish gold (?)); Gotland's bead strings with carnelian, crystal and cowries.

## 5. Silk

### 5.1 Where it was made, and by whom

- **Constantinople.** Imperial workshops and private guilds under the city Prefect. The Book of the Prefect, a
  rule book of the 10th century, gives separate chapters to the silk-cloth merchants, the importers of silk from
  Syria and Baghdad, the raw-silk merchants, the raw-silk dressers and the silk dyers (en.wikipedia, Book of the
  Eparch, checked). The best purples are the emperor's own and may not be sold or taken out of the city at all.
- **The Caliphate**: Syria, Iraq, Persia; and **Central Asia** (Sogdiana), whose patterned silks go north with the
  fur and slave trade.
- **Spain**: silk is woven at Almeria and Cordoba from the 9th century (?).
- Nobody north of the Alps makes silk. The north **re-makes** it: English and Frankish women embroider it with
  gold; in Scandinavia a bolt is cut into strips a finger wide and sewn on as trim.

### 5.2 Where it was wanted and why

- **The Church** first: vestments, altar cloths, and wrappings for relics (a relic is always folded in silk). In
  764 Abbot Cuthbert thanks Lul for a cloth of pure silk for the relics of Bede (Boniface and Lul letters 116 (?)).
- **Kings and courts**: Charlemagne sent Offa in 796 a belt, a Hunnish sword and two silk cloaks (Alcuin, letter
  100). Notker tells how Charlemagne took his courtiers hunting in the rain and brambles in the silks and
  peacock-feather cloaks they had just bought from Venetians at Pavia, while he wore a sheepskin (Notker 2.17 (?)).
- **The north**: silk reached Norway by 834 at the latest: the Oseberg burial held "more than a hundred tiny
  fragments of silk", most of it cut in strips (en.wikipedia, Oseberg Ship, checked). Birka's graves hold silk,
  "Byzantine embroidery with extremely fine gold thread" and brocade (en.wikipedia, Birka, checked). In the 10th
  century women in York, Lincoln and Dublin wore plain **silk caps**; at York and Lincoln two caps seem cut from
  the same bolt (Coppergate and Saltergate finds (?)).

### 5.3 Routes and ships

Two roads, and which one is open decides who in the north has silk.

1. **The southern road.** Constantinople to Venice by sea; Venice to **Pavia** up the Po; then mules over the
   passes: the Great St Bernard to the Rhone and Burgundy, the Septimer and the passes of Chur to the upper Rhine;
   then by river to Mainz and Cologne and so to the Frisians. English pilgrims and merchants buy at Pavia and
   Rome on the way home. No ship of the game's waters carries silk on this road until it reaches the Rhine.
2. **The eastern road.** From Persia and Central Asia up the Volga through Bulghar, or from Constantinople up the
   Dnieper, to Staraya Ladoga and the Baltic: Gotland, Birka, then Hedeby and Kaupang. Busy from about 800,
   at its height 900 to 950, failing after 970.

Hedeby is where the two meet. In the west silk is always a church and court good; in the Baltic for a hundred and
fifty years a rich farmer may have a strip of it.

### 5.4 Season

The passes are open to pack beasts from about midsummer to Martinmas. Merchants and pilgrims cross in companies.
Silk bought at Pavia in summer is on the Rhine by Michaelmas and in the Frisian ports before the winter. The
churches buy before the great feasts: Easter above all, then Christmas, and before a new church is hallowed. On
the eastern road the boats come back to Birka in late summer.

### 5.5 Units and packing

By the **piece** (a pallium: a length for a cloak or an altar), by the garment, and for scraps by weight. A piece
of patterned silk weighs 1 to 2 kg (guess). Wrapped in linen and waxed cloth and laid in a chest, because
salt water ruins it. In the north, also sold by the strip.

**In the small boat**: weight is nothing. Ten pieces weigh as much as a child and are worth more than the boat,
the debt and the crew together. The risks are wet, thieves, and the tollman's eye.

### 5.6 Prices

- **944**: the treaty between the Rus and the Greeks lets a Rus merchant buy silk only up to **50 gold pieces**
  for one piece, and what he buys must be shown to the emperor's officer and sealed (Primary Chronicle, year 6453;
  in the project's chronicle). At 30 to 40 d the gold piece, the lawful ceiling is about **1,500 to 2,000 d a
  piece**. So fine silks cost that much and more.
- **Before 909** Gerald of Aurillac, coming home from Rome, was told by Venetian merchants at Pavia that the cloak
  he had bought in Rome would have cost more in Constantinople; he sent the difference back to the seller
  (Odo of Cluny, Life of Gerald 1.27 (?); I do not have the figures). The point for the game: Pavia merchants know
  the Constantinople price, and say so.
- **808**: Charlemagne caps the price of the best lined **wool** cloak at 20 shillings (240 d), a plain one at
  10, a marten or otter robe at 30 (Capitulary of 808, c. 5 (?)). Silk stands above all of these.
- The game's 360 d for a bolt is right for a plain piece and far too low for a figured or purple one.

### 5.7 Laws and bans

- **Byzantium**: the forbidden cloths (the imperial purples and certain weaves) may not be exported; other silk
  above a set value must be declared and sealed (Book of the Prefect). In 968 the customs men took five purple
  cloths from Liudprand, bishop of Cremona and Otto's envoy, as he left; he answered that in the west Venetian and
  Amalfitan pedlars sold such cloth freely (Liudprand, Embassy 53 to 55 (?)). So the ban leaked, by Venice.
- **The Rus cap of 944**, above.
- **The Church** scolds clergy and nuns in silk and bright colours again and again (Council of Clofesho 747;
  Alcuin's letters after 793 (?)). No law forbids a layman.
- At Pavia the king's chamber took a tenth from merchants coming over the passes, and the English quarrelled with
  the officers over the opening of their bags (section 9).

### 5.8 Frauds and tests

- **Half-silk**: a silk weft on a linen or wool warp looks whole from the right side. Test: pull one thread from
  each way of the cloth and burn it. Silk shrinks from the flame, smells of burnt hair and leaves a black bead
  that crushes; linen burns like a wick. (Plain craft knowledge; no text of the age gives it.)
- **Weight**: silk sold by weight can be kept damp. Buy it dry and warm.
- **The seal**: silk bought lawfully at Constantinople carries the Prefect's lead seal. A piece without one was
  smuggled, which a buyer in the north does not mind, or stolen from a church, which he should.
- **Purple**: true shell purple does not run. Orchil and madder-over-woad purples do. Wet a corner and press it
  on white linen.
- **A relic wrapping** sold as "silk that touched the saint" is two frauds in one (section 8).

### 5.9 Finds

Oseberg (834); the Birka chamber graves; Valsgarde; Mammen (970/1 (?)); the silk caps of York, Lincoln and Dublin;
the vestments in St Cuthbert's coffin (a stole and maniple worked at Winchester between 909 and 916 for Bishop
Frithestan, given by King Athelstan in 934, and Byzantine silks added later (?)); the Galloway hoard (buried about
900, found 2014: silver, and a lidded vessel wrapped in several silks (?)); relic wrappings in the treasuries of
Sens, Aachen, Maastricht.

## 6. Spices

### 6.1 What, from where

**Pepper** from the Malabar coast of India; **cinnamon** and cassia from Ceylon and China; **cloves** from the
Moluccas; **ginger**, **galingale** and **zedoary** from India and China; **costus** and **spikenard** from the
Himalaya; **cumin** from the Mediterranean (the cheap one); **incense** and **myrrh** from Arabia; **balsam** from
Judaea and Egypt, which the Church must have for the chrism; **musk** and **camphor** from further Asia. All of it
passes through the Caliphate. Ibn Khurradadhbih (c. 870) says the Jewish Radhanite merchants bring west "musk,
aloes, camphor, cinnamon, and other products of the Eastern countries" and take east "eunuchs, female slaves,
boys, brocade, castor, marten and other furs, and swords" (en.wikipedia, Radhanite, checked).

### 6.2 Who wanted it and why

- **Medicine** first. The Old English leechbooks of the 10th century call for pepper in recipe after recipe,
  and now and then ginger, cinnamon, zedoary, galingale (Bald's Leechbook (?)). The Patriarch of Jerusalem sent
  King Alfred remedies, among them balsam, theriac and a white stone (Leechbook 2.64 (?)).
- **The table** of abbots, bishops and kings.
- **The Church**: incense at every Mass, balsam in the chrism, both scarce north of the Alps.
- **Gifts**. Bede, dying in 735, shared out the little treasures in his box among the priests of the house:
  **pepper, napkins and incense** (Cuthbert's letter on the death of Bede). In the 740s the Roman deacon Gemmulus
  sent Boniface a few ounces of cinnamon and costus and two pounds of pepper, and Lul sent pepper, incense and
  cinnamon to English friends (Boniface and Lul letters (?)). A monk at Jarrow owns pepper; he owns it by the
  pinch.

### 6.3 Routes

- **Until about 700**: by sea to Marseille and Fos, and up the Rhone. In 716 King Chilperic II confirmed to the
  abbey of Corbie its yearly draw on the toll house at Fos: 10,000 pounds of oil, 30 pounds of pepper, 150 of
  cumin, 2 of cloves, 1 of cinnamon, 2 of spikenard, 30 of costus, 50 of dates, 100 of figs, 100 of almonds, 20
  of rice, and 50 quires of papyrus, among other things (the Corbie diploma of 716; Pirenne made it famous).
  Whether Corbie still drew any of it in 716 is doubted. By 793 this road is all but dead.
- **In the game's years**: Alexandria and Constantinople to **Venice**, Venice to **Pavia**, over the passes to the
  Rhine. Also through Spain and the Jewish merchants of the Rhone. A list tacked on to the statutes of Corbie (the
  statutes are Adalhard's of 822; the list may be a century younger (?)) shows the monks buying their spices at
  **Cambrai**: pepper, cumin, ginger, cloves, cinnamon, galingale and more.
- **Mainz, about 965.** Ibrahim ibn Yaqub of Tortosa found it strange that in a town so far in the west he saw
  dirhams struck at Samarkand and spices that grow only in the far east: pepper, ginger, cloves, spikenard, costus
  and galingale (in al-Qazwini's extract; McCormick 2001; Wikipedia's page on him does not give the passage (?)).
- **London, about 1000.** The men of the Emperor who come in their ships pay at Christmas and at Easter two grey
  cloths and one brown, **ten pounds of pepper**, five pairs of gloves and two kegs of vinegar (IV Æthelred 2).
  Pepper reaches London **by the Rhine**, in the ships of Cologne, Tiel and their neighbours. This is the one hard
  fact that puts pepper in a North Sea hold.
- **By the eastern road** spices may have come to the Baltic with the silver, but nothing proves it (?).

### 6.4 Season

It comes over the passes with the silk in summer, reaches the Rhine in autumn, and is sold for the **Christmas**
table and the winter's sicknesses, and again for **Easter**. The London toll is taken at exactly those two feasts.
Incense and balsam are dearest in Lent, before the chrism is blessed on Maundy Thursday. Cheapest on the Rhine
between Michaelmas and Martinmas.

### 6.5 Units and packing

By the **pound** and the **ounce**, weighed in the hand scales. Carried in leather bags, sewn linen bags inside
waxed cloth, small wooden boxes (Bede's was a little box, a capsella), and horn or glass phials for balsam and
musk. Kept from damp and from the smell of fish and tar.

**In the small boat**: a great lord's whole year of pepper is Corbie's thirty pounds: 12 kg. A merchant with one
pound of pepper is carrying a day's good trade; with ten he is carrying the Emperor's men's toll. Weight never
matters. **A market's stock must be small**: two to ten pounds at a Rhine town, an ounce or two at a wic, none at
all at a beach market.

### 6.6 Prices

**No price for pepper in pennies survives from the north in these years.** What there is:

- The London toll of ten pounds twice a year: a lot, but payable by a ship's company. Pepper is dear, not fabulous.
- An old yardstick: in Diocletian's price edict (301) a pound of pepper costs as much as eight measures of wheat.
  At the Frankfurt price of 4 d a measure that would be about 32 d a pound. In 12th-century England pepper sold for
  roughly 7 d to 2 s a pound (?). So the game's 48 d a pound for 793 is a fair guess, and it should fall towards
  20 to 30 d by 1050 as Venice and the Rhine towns grow.
- Cumin was cheap: Corbie drew five times as much cumin as pepper.

### 6.7 Tolls and laws

No ban. The spice trade pays the tenth at the passes (the Clusae) and at Pavia, where each Venetian merchant also
owed the master of the king's treasury every year a pound each of pepper, cinnamon, galingale and ginger, and his
wife an ivory comb and a mirror (Honorantie civitatis Papie, written about 1020 of the customs of the century
before (?)). Toll in spice is the rule at both ends of the road, Pavia and London.

### 6.8 Frauds and tests

- **The mouse.** Notker tells of a Jewish merchant who, to shame a vain bishop, stuffed a house mouse with
  spices, painted it, and offered it as a rare beast from Judaea; the bishop bid three pounds of silver, then ten,
  then twenty, and the merchant took a bushel of silver and carried it to Charlemagne, who shamed the bishop
  before the court (Notker 1.16 (?)). The tale is two things: the type of the spice fraud, and the proof that
  a Frank thought a merchant from the south could ask any price for a thing nobody had seen.
- **Pepper** stretched with juniper berries (Pliny already says so, Natural History 12.14 (?)), with seeds rolled
  in clay, or wetted to weigh more. Tests: bite one, a true corn burns the tongue and keeps burning; crush one, it
  is pale inside and smells at once; throw a pinch in water, sound corns sink and husks and berries float (the last
  from later practice (?)).
- **Cinnamon**: cheap cassia sold as cinnamon. Cassia is thick, rough and hot; cinnamon is thin, rolled and sweet.
- **Balsam** cut with oil and turpentine. The old tests (Pliny, Dioscorides (?)): a drop on water should stay
  whole and can be lifted; a drop in milk curdles it; it leaves no stain on wool.
- **Musk** pods packed with dried blood. Old spice loses its strength: all spice should be bought by the nose.
- **False weights.** A pound is not a pound: Charlemagne's pound of 793 is heavier than the old one, the
  Byzantine pound lighter than both. A merchant who buys by the litra and sells by the Frankish pound must know it.

### 6.9 Finds

Spices leave almost nothing in the ground. The proof is in the texts (above) and in the leechbooks. Walnut shells
and a few southern seeds in northern town layers and in the Oseberg burial show the same road in the humblest
goods (?).

## 7. Books

### 7.1 Where they were made, and by whom

In the writing rooms of minsters and abbeys, by monks and (for some houses) nuns. In 793: **Wearmouth and Jarrow**,
**York** (whose library Alcuin praised in verse), **Canterbury**, **Lindisfarne**; in Ireland and the islands
**Iona**, **Armagh**, **Kells** after 807; in Francia **Tours** (which under Alcuin and after turned out whole
Bibles, two or so a year (?)), **Corbie**, **Saint-Denis**, the court school at **Aachen**, **Fulda**, **Lorsch**,
**St Gall**, **Reichenau**; from 822 **Corvey** in Saxony, and from it Hamburg and Bremen. In the 10th century
**Winchester** and **Canterbury** again, **Fleury**, **Echternach**, **Cologne**, **Trier**.

What a book costs to make. The **Codex Amiatinus**, one of three great Bibles made at Wearmouth and Jarrow about
700, has about 1,030 leaves, weighs "over 75 pounds (34 kg)", and took "the skins of 515 cattle" (en.wikipedia,
Codex Amiatinus, checked). An ordinary gospel book takes 40 to 80 calf or sheep skins (?), a psalter 20 to 30 (?),
and a scribe half a year. The colours are bought: orpiment, red lead, vermilion, verdigris, and blues from woad or
indigo; true lapis blue is rare before the 10th century (?). Gold leaf and silver for the best.

### 7.2 Where they were wanted and why

- **New churches.** Every church needs at least a mass book, a lectionary, a psalter and a book of penance; a
  bishop tests his priests on having them (Admonitio generalis 789, c. 72, orders the books corrected and well
  copied). The conversion of Frisia and Saxony (to about 804), of Denmark and Sweden (Ansgar from 826), and the
  rebuilding of England after 880 are each a great hunger for books.
- **Other libraries.** A house wants the books it lacks. It asks to borrow one, copies it, and sends it back.
- **The heathen** want the covers. Gold and garnets are torn off and the leaves thrown away or sold back.

### 7.3 How a book really moved

**A book was seldom bought in a market.** It moved in four ways, and all four suit the game.

1. **Gift**, king to church or church to church. Ceolfrith set out for Rome in 716 with the Amiatinus as a gift
   for the Pope and died on the road at Langres.
2. **Loan for copying.** About 852 Lupus, abbot of Ferrieres, wrote to Abbot Altsig of York asking for the loan of
   books (Quintilian, Jerome, Bede) to be sent to his cell of **Saint-Josse near Quentovic**, copied there, and sent
   back; in the same year he asked King Ethelwulf for lead for his roof, to be landed at Etaples (Lupus, letters
   (?)). That is a book crossing the Channel on a merchant ship, with a monk's letter and a promise.
3. **Commission**: a house or a lord pays an abbey to write one.
4. **Loot and ransom.** A gospel book now at Stockholm carries a note in Old English of the 9th century: the
   ealdorman Alfred and his wife Werburg "obtained these books from the heathen army with our pure money, that was
   with pure gold", and gave them to Christ Church (en.wikipedia, Stockholm Codex Aureus, checked). In 1007 the
   great gospel book of Kells was stolen from the church; it was found after some months under a sod, its gold
   stripped (Annals of Ulster 1007).

And they were lost at sea: when Ansgar first sailed for Birka in 829 pirates took the ship, and with it the king's
gifts and **about forty books** gathered for the mission (Rimbert, Life of Ansgar 10).

### 7.4 Season

Writing is summer work. In 764 Abbot Cuthbert told Lul he could not send all the books of Bede he had promised
because the winter had been so cold that the scribes' hands could not work (Boniface and Lul letters 116 (?)).
Skins are plentiful after the autumn slaughter, at Martinmas. Books travel with the first ships of spring and
with pilgrims; a new church wants its books before its hallowing day.

### 7.5 Units and packing

By the book. A psalter or a pocket gospel weighs 1 to 2 kg; a gospel book for the altar 5 to 8 kg; a whole Bible
20 to 35 kg. Carried in a leather **satchel** (the Irish hung them on pegs), wrapped in linen or waxed cloth, in a
chest for the sea. Parchment by the skin and the quire (four sheets folded, eight leaves). Wax tablets for notes.

**In the small boat**: forty books, Ansgar's whole mission library, would weigh about 150 to 200 kg. The danger
is damp: parchment cockles and ink lifts.

### 7.6 Prices

- **About 690**: King Aldfrith of Northumbria gave the abbey of Wearmouth **eight hides of land** by the river
  Fresca for one book of the cosmographers that Benedict Biscop had bought in Rome (Bede, Lives of the Abbots 15).
  Eight hides is a living for eight households.
- **Mid 9th century**: the Codex Aureus bought back from the heathen army "with pure gold"; the sum is not given.
- **About 890**: each copy of King Alfred's Pastoral Care went out with a pointer (an aestel) worth **50
  mancuses**, 1,500 pennies; the pointer, not the book, but it shows what dressed a king's book (Alfred's preface).
- **About 1040** (?): a countess of Anjou is said to have paid for one book of homilies two hundred sheep, a
  measure each of wheat, rye and millet, and some marten skins (an often-told tale; I could not check it).
- A guess for the game: a plain psalter or mass book 120 to 360 d; a gospel book for an altar 500 to 1,500 d;
  a great Bible or a jewelled book is not for sale, only for ransom or gift. A hundred skins of parchment 40 to 80 d.

### 7.7 Laws

A church's books are church treasure: selling them without the bishop is forbidden, and buying a stolen one is
sacrilege (section 9). Many books carry a curse on whoever takes them away.

### 7.8 Frauds and tests

- **Stolen books.** The owner's mark is scraped off the first or last leaf. Look for a thin, rough patch there.
- **Missing quires.** Count the gatherings; a book sold as a whole gospel may lack John.
- **A bad copy.** Full of errors, written fast by a boy. Only a reader can tell: the merchant needs a clerk he
  trusts, or the tongue and the letters himself (the game has `learn` and `tongue`).
- **Stripped covers**: boards and bare leather where the silver was.

### 7.9 Finds

The Codex Amiatinus (Florence); the Stockholm Codex Aureus and its note; the Lindisfarne Gospels and St Cuthbert's
pocket gospel; the Book of Kells; the Faddan More Psalter (about 800, found in an Irish bog in 2006, its leather
cover lined with papyrus (?)); book mounts and reliquary fittings from Ireland and Northumbria turned into brooches
in Norwegian women's graves of the 9th century.

## 8. Relics

### 8.1 Where they came from, and who dealt in them

**Rome**, with its miles of catacombs, is the great source; then the old churches of Gaul and Italy; then, from
the raids on, anywhere a saint lies unguarded. A relic may be a whole body, a bone, a tooth, hair, dust from the
tomb, oil from the lamp above it, or a cloth that has lain on it (a brandeum). Most relics that moved were the
small kinds.

The trade had professionals. **Deusdona**, a deacon of Rome with a house near St Peter in Chains, sold Einhard's
notary Ratleik the bodies of Marcellinus and Peter in 827, which went to Seligenstadt; he came north again and
supplied Fulda in the 830s. He worked with his brothers, crossed the Alps in spring with the merchant companies,
and timed his coming to the feasts and fairs when a church would pay best (Einhard, Translation of Marcellinus and
Peter; Rudolf of Fulda; Geary 1978). A Frankish cleric called **Felix** peddled bodies to bishops in the same years
(Geary). In the 10th century English kings were buyers: Athelstan gathered relics from Brittany and the continent
(William of Malmesbury (?)).

Others simply took them. Venetian merchants carried **St Mark** out of Alexandria in 828, under a load of pork,
the story says, so that the Muslim customs men would not look (the Translatio of St Mark). A monk of Conques
served ten years at Agen to steal **Sainte Foy** about 866. In 1058 the monk Balger of Bergues, weather-bound in a
Sussex harbour, stole the body of **St Lewinna** from a minster and took her home across the Channel by ship (Drogo
of Bergues (?)): a relic theft by merchant ship in the game's own waters.

And they moved to be safe: Philibert's monks left Noirmoutier in 836 and wandered to Tournus by 875; Cuthbert's
body left Lindisfarne in 875 and was seven years on the road.

### 8.2 Where they were wanted and why

- **The law of the altar.** The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 ruled that a church may not be hallowed without
  relics: "after 787, all new Christian churches had to possess a relic before they could be properly consecrated"
  (en.wikipedia, Translation (relic), checked). The Frankish church took the rule up (?).
- **Saxony**, newly made Christian and with no saints of its own, is the hungriest: St Vitus from Saint-Denis to
  Corvey in 836, St Liborius from Le Mans to Paderborn in 836, St Alexander from Rome to Wildeshausen in 851
  (brought by Waltbert, grandson of Widukind).
- **Frisia, Flanders, England** want them too; later Denmark and Norway.
- A saint brings pilgrims, gifts, oaths sworn on him, and a fair on his day. A relic is the one good that makes
  its own market.

### 8.3 Routes and season

Over the passes in spring and early summer with the pilgrims coming home from Easter at Rome, down the Rhine or
across Francia, to be received before the saint's feast or the hallowing of the church. Translations are public:
the road is lined, the sick are healed, and everybody knows which merchant's boat she came on.

### 8.4 Units and packing

Wrapped in **silk**, tied, sealed with the giver's wax seal, with a strip of parchment naming the saint (the
label, the "authentic"), in a purse, a horn, a small box or a house-shaped shrine. Old labels of the 7th and 8th
centuries survive by the hundred at Sens and Chelles (?). A whole body goes in a chest on a bier. For the boat it
weighs nothing.

### 8.5 Prices

- **Gift for gift** is the lawful form: the seller "gives" the saint and the buyer "gives" silver, a horse, a mule,
  land. Einhard paid Deusdona's costs and made him gifts; no sum is written.
- **1022** (?): Archbishop Aethelnoth of Canterbury, coming home from Rome, bought at **Pavia** an arm of St
  Augustine of Hippo for **a hundred talents of silver and one of gold**, and gave it to Coventry (William of
  Malmesbury, writing about 1125). If a talent is a pound, that is 24,000 d and more: fifty times the player's
  debt, for one arm.
- A guess for the game: a labelled scrap or tomb-dust 60 to 240 d; a named bone with a bishop's letter 500 to
  2,500 d; a whole body is a chain, not a price.

### 8.6 Laws

- Roman law forbade it flat: nobody may move a buried body, and **nobody may trade in a martyr** (Theodosian Code
  9.17.7, of 386); a decree of Theodosius allowed only the moving of a whole coffin (en.wikipedia, Translation
  (relic), checked).
- **Mainz, 813**, c. 51: no saint's body may be moved from place to place without the counsel of the prince and
  leave of the bishops and the synod (?).
- **Nijmegen, 806**, c. 4: bishops and abbots must guard their church treasures, "because Jewish and other
  merchants boast that they can buy from them whatever they please" (Capitulary for the missi, 806 (?); my
  paraphrase).
- Selling a holy thing for money is **simony**. So in the game a relic is never "bought": it is given for a gift,
  with a letter, or it is stolen.

### 8.7 Frauds and tests

This is the richest ground for cards.

- **The pedlar of bones.** Ralph Glaber tells of a common man, in his own day (about 1020 to 1040), who went from
  land to land under changing names digging bones out of any grave, putting them in boxes and selling them as
  martyrs and confessors, till in the Alps he sold a nameless body as "St Justus" to a new church and the sick were
  carried to it; the bishops let it stand (Glaber, Histories 4.3 (?)).
- **Trial by fire.** A doubtful relic was laid on coals or in a flame: a true one does not burn. Archbishop Egbert
  of Trier tried the body of St Celsus so about 979, and Bishop Meinwerk of Paderborn tried relics of St Felix so
  in the 11th century (Head (?); Geary).
- **The papers.** Ask for the label, the seal, and a letter from the bishop of the place it came from. Ask where
  the rest of the saint lies: a second head of the same saint is a known joke of the age.
- **The saint's own will.** If the saint did not wish to go, the boat would not move, the bearers could not lift
  the bier, or the thief fell sick. A calm crossing is the saint's consent. This is how the age told a holy theft
  from a crime, and the game can use it straight: the weather on the crossing is the proof.
- **Deusdona's own trick**: he promised Einhard's man one saint and tried to deliver another, and Abbot Hilduin's
  priest stole a share on the way home (Einhard). Even the best dealer cheats, and the buyers cheat each other.

### 8.8 Finds

The shrine Einhard built at Seligenstadt; the relic labels of Sens and Chelles; Cuthbert's coffin at Durham; the
house-shaped Insular shrines carried off to Norway, one with a later owner's name cut in runes on the base,
"Ranvaik owns this casket" (National Museum, Copenhagen (?)).

## 11. How the trade changed, age by age

**793 to 840: the Rhine's high tide.** Dorestad is the gate. Badorf pots, relief-band wine jars, Tating jugs and
funnel beakers go north in every Frisian boat. Glassmakers are changing from Egyptian soda glass to their own ash
glass. At Ribe the bead-makers lose their market to the first cheap beads from the Caliphate (from about 790), and
the first dirhams reach the Baltic (about 800). Silk in the west is a church and court good from Pavia; Oseberg
(834) shows it already in Norway. Relics pour into Saxony (827 to 851). Books are being made faster than ever
before or for centuries after: Tours, Corbie, the court. Ansgar loses forty of them to pirates (829).

**840 to 900: the raids.** Dorestad is plundered again and again from 834 and is gone by 863. Tating ware ends.
Books and relics become **loot and ransom** (the Codex Aureus), and saints take to the road (Philibert 836,
Cuthbert 875). Northern minsters stop writing. The eastern road swells: carnelian, crystal and silk come to
Birka and Gotland in quantity from about 860. Hedeby takes Ribe's place. In England Ipswich ware gives way to the
wheel-thrown pots of the new towns (Thetford, Stamford) from about 850. Pingsdorf's red paint appears on the Rhine
at the end of the century. Alfred has books written again (c. 890).

**900 to 970: the silver years.** Samanid dirhams by the hundred thousand; silk caps on the heads of York and
Dublin; beads by the string in every Baltic grave. Pingsdorf ware rules the Rhine trade, landed now at Tiel,
Deventer and Utrecht. Stamford's glazed jugs are the first fine English tableware. The Rus are held to 50 gold
pieces of silk (944). A traveller sees eastern spices and Samarkand silver at Mainz (965). English reform
(Dunstan, Ethelwold, from the 960s) sets Winchester and Canterbury writing and buying.

**970 to 1066: the turn to the west.** The eastern silver fails (about 970) and Birka with it (about 975); the
carnelian stops. German silver from the Harz takes its place, and the south-to-north road through the Rhine towns
carries more every year: Venice has its charter at Constantinople (992), the Emperor's men pay pepper at London
(about 1000), the merchants of Tiel boast of their own law (1020), Cnut bargains down the tolls of the passes
(1027). Relics are bought openly for great sums (Pavia, 1022). Baltic ware spreads from the Wends. Lead-glass
rings and beads come from the Rus and the German lands. Paffrath cooking pots and, at the very end, glazed
Andenne ware join Pingsdorf. Sigtuna and Schleswig stand where Birka and Hedeby stood. By 1066 a merchant from the
Rhine mouth sells pepper, Pingsdorf jugs and wine in London under the king's written law, which is a long way from
a beach at Ribe in 793.

## 16. What `goods_793.json` and `trade_rules.json` lack or have wrong

### 16.1 Wrong, or at odds with the evidence

1. **`glass_vessels` note: "claw beakers".** Claw beakers are of the 5th to 7th century. In 793 the Rhenish
   shapes are the funnel beaker, the palm cup, squat jars and reticella bowls. Change the note.
2. **`tating_ware`: `made` gives `frisia: 1`, and `to: 900`.** Nobody knows where it was made; nothing points to
   Frisia. It is of the late 8th and early 9th century. Make it `rhineland: 2` only (with a `(?)`), and `to: 850`.
3. **`badorf_ware`: `to: 0`.** It runs on beside `pingsdorf_ware` (from 900) for ever. Give it `to: 900` (or 925
   to overlap). Its name "Rhenish pottery" hides which ware it is; the note joins Badorf and Mayen, which are
   different wares from different places.
4. **`ipswich_ware` note: "wheel-thrown".** It is coil-built and finished on a slow wheel. Say "kiln-fired and
   wheel-finished". There is no good to follow it after 850 (see 16.2).
5. **`glass_beads`: one row for 273 years.** It is made only in `jutland`, `schleswig`, `rhineland`, `italy`.
   From about 790 the cheap beads come from the Caliphate by `ladoga` and `svealand`, and Ribe's own making fades.
   Either split it (northern wound beads to about 850; eastern drawn beads from 790 to about 1000, made
   `caliphate: 3`, `rus_volga: 1`, `ladoga: 0.5`, `svealand: 0.5`, `gotland: 0.5`) or the table tells the story
   backwards. `finns` and `norway_north` should want them.
6. **`glass_beads` value against the one real price.** 12 d the hundred is 0.12 d a bead. Ibn Fadlan's price on
   the Volga is a dirham a bead (about 1.7 d) for the favourite green ones. The market rules cannot reach that
   (`wanted` 3 gives 1.7 times, `price_max` 2.2). Either add a dear kind ("green beads", or stone beads), or accept
   that the Volga price is out of range and say so in the note.
7. **`carnelian`: `from: 0`, `to: 0`.** Few before 800, plenty from about 860, almost none after about 970 to
   1000. It needs years, or an era table (16.4). `rus_volga: 1` as a maker is a transit, like the `0.5` hubs.
8. **`silk`: the makers' hubs.** `italy` is missing, and Pavia and Venice are the main western door for the whole
   period. `svealand` and `gotland` should be `0.5` hubs from about 830 to 970 (Birka), and `ladoga: 0.5` should
   end about 970. `jutland`, `schleswig`, `vestfold` and `ireland` (Dublin, 10th century) should want it. One value

9. **`pepper`: `ladoga: 0.5`.** Nothing proves pepper came by the Rus rivers. Mainz (965) and London (c. 1000) put

10. **`sugar`.** The brief says no sugar as a common thing. `neustria: 0.5` as a source and a want of 2 in

### 16.3 `trade_rules.json`: what it lacks

1. **Toll by the ship, and toll in kind.** The English zone has a flat `toll: 0.05`. The one English tariff there
   is (London, c. 1000) charges by the size of the ship (½ d, 1 d, 4 d) and takes pepper, cloth, gloves and vinegar
   from the Emperor's men twice a year. A small boat with silk aboard pays a halfpenny. I know no witness for a
   twentieth as the English rate, except on the Rouen men's fish.
2. **Where the tenth is taken.** The Frankish `toll: 0.10` reads as a rate on every sale. By the charter of 828 it
   is taken at Quentovic, Dorestad and the passes, and the emperor's own merchants are free elsewhere.
3. **A church's goods pass free.** Toll-free charters for abbeys (Saint-Germain 779 and many more) have no rule.
   The engine has `show_charter`; the rules have nothing for it to do.
4. **Pilgrims pay no toll; merchants among them do** (796). No rule, and it is the best tollman card there is.
5. **No law for silk.** Wanted: `{"law": "purple", "zones": ["byzantine"], "rule": "no export"}` and the 944 cap
   for Rus buyers. Without it the richest good in the table has no risk.
6. **`sacred`: "no sale", from 0.** Wanted beside it: a `gift` or `translation` rule (a relic may pass with a
   bishop's letter, for a gift), the Mainz rule of 813 (no moving without leave), and the Nijmegen rule of 806 (a
   merchant may not buy church treasure). As it stands the player can never lawfully carry a relic.
7. **No breakage.** Nothing in `market` or elsewhere loses pots or glass in a hard blow or a bad landing.
8. **No damp.** Silk, books, parchment and spices are ruined by salt water; `keeps: 0` says they last for ever.
9. **No small stocks for dear goods.** `stock_value: 150` deniers per size step gives a market three pounds of
   pepper, which is about right, but also "0.4 of a silk". The rounding for goods worth more than the stock value
   needs a rule (`stock_min: 1` then puts a bolt of silk in every market that has a 0.5).
10. **Zones never change.** Coin, toll and law are fixed for 273 years. For this group the big breaks are: the
    eastern silver and its goods end about 970; Scandinavian kings strike coin from about 995; Pavia's and the
    passes' tolls are eased for the English and Danes in 1027. The `laws` have `from` and `to`; the `zones` and the
    goods' `made` and `wanted` do not.
11. **`start.coins` gives 6 dirhams at Dorestad in 793.** The first dirhams reach the Baltic about 800 and are
    very rare on the Rhine. Not my subject, but it belongs to the same eastern road as the beads.
12. **The Byzantine zone uses `hacksilver`**, noted "to come". Silk and spice prices at source are in gold; until
    the nomisma is in, the 50-gold-piece cap cannot be said in the game's money.
