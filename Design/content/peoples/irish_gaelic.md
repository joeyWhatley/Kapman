# The Irish and the Gaels of Dál Riata, 793-1066: their monks above all

For the writers of dialogue. Quotations with a source were checked against the printed translations; `(?)` marks a fact not re-checked. **AU** Annals of Ulster (Hennessy 1887; true years, one later than his); **Meyer** *Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry* (1911); **Thes.** *Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus* II (1903); **Cormac** Cormac's Glossary, c. 900 (O'Donovan and Stokes 1868); **Kelly** *A Guide to Early Irish Law* (1988), never quoted. Game ids: people `irish`; ports `hi`, `duiblinn`.

## 1. Who they are, and how that changes

- **793.** They are *Goídil*, Gaels; in Latin, and to every neighbour, *Scotti*; "Scotia" is Ireland. One tongue, one law and one learned class from Kerry to Mull, but some 150 small kingdoms (*túatha*); "king of Tara" is a claim, not a state. The great monasteries (Armagh, Clonmacnoise, Kildare, Hí) are the only towns. Dál Riata is the Gaels of Argyll and the Isles, under Pictish over-kings (?).
- **794-825.** "Devastation of all the islands of Britain by Gentiles" (AU 794). Hí burned, 802; sixty-eight of its community killed, 806; its abbot builds Kells inland, 807-814.
- **841-900.** "A fortress at Dubhlinn" (AU 841). The Northmen become more kings to fight, hire and marry. Cináed mac Ailpín (d. 858) rules Picts and Gaels; by 900 AU says "King of Alba".
- **902-1066.** Dublin emptied (902), retaken (917), beaten at Tara (980). Ireland's first coin is struck there c. 997. Brian dies at Clontarf, Good Friday 1014; afterwards Dublin is a prize among Irish kings.

## 2. How they live

- **House.** A round house of woven hazel inside a ring-bank (*ráth*). A small farmer's is 19 feet across, with seven cows and a quarter share in a plough (Críth Gablach, c. 700 (?)). A monastery: a wooden church, cells, a guest-house, a bank around all; bell-towers only from c. 950 (AU).
- **Food and drink.** Milk, butter and curds in summer; oat bread, porridge and salt bacon in winter. Ale is everyone's, mead for feasts; wine comes from Gaul for the Mass and for kings. The strict monks of Tallaght ate no meat but deer and wild pig (Monastery of Tallaght 6).
- **Dress.** A linen shirt (*léine*) under a wool cloak (*brat*) pinned with a ring brooch; length and colour say rank. Monks: undyed wool, a hood, the Roman tonsure.
- **The year** turns on four days: 1 February (Brigit), lambing and ploughing; 1 May (Beltaine), cattle to the hill pastures; 1 August (Lugnasad), first reaping and the fairs; 1 November (Samain), cattle home, slaughter, food-rents due (?).

## 3. Law and rank

- **Law** belongs to jurists (*brithem*), not kings: written in Irish by c. 700, the same in every kingdom.
- **Honour-price**, the price of a man's face, stands where wergild stands elsewhere: a province king 14 *cumals*, a king of a *túath* 7, a strong farmer 5 *séts*, a small farmer 3 (Kelly). It fixes the fine for insulting him, the weight of his oath and the size of bargain he may make. Killing any freeman costs a flat 7 *cumals* besides.
- **Counting.** A *cumal* is a slave-woman, worth three milch cows; a cow is an ounce of silver, or two *séts* (Kelly (?)).
- **Oaths.** The higher man's oath beats the lower's. A great man who will not pay is fasted against at his own door (Kelly).
- **Court.** The *airecht* judges; the *óenach* is assembly, races and fair in one. Never a "thing".
- **Slavery** is ordinary; Patrick was a slave. Heathens carried off many women from Howth in 821 (AU (?)).
- **Women** are under father, husband or sons, at half the husband's honour-price. But a wife of equal property can undo his bad bargains; either may divorce, and she leaves with her share (Kelly). Adomnán's Law (697) protects women, children and clergy. Attested at work: embroiderers, physicians, a poet (Uallach, d. 934 (?)), abbesses of Kildare. No woman merchant is named (?).

## 4. Faith

- Christian since the fifth century (Palladius, 431; Patrick). Hí kept its own Easter until 716; since then nobody differs from Rome.
- **The church is monasteries run by kin.** The abbot is the saint's heir (*comarba*), often of the royal house, sometimes married; monasteries hold tenants and fight battles. The *Céli Dé*, clients of God (Tallaght; Máel Ruain, d. 792), are the strict ones. The hardest penance is to leave Ireland for good.
- **Attested of the old religion:** only what monks wrote. Druids are low-ranked sorcerers in the laws (Kelly); the Breastplate hymn prays "against spells of women and smiths and wizards" (Thes. 357). Cormac recalls Beltaine as "two fires which Druids used to make with great incantations", the cattle driven between; Brigit as "the goddess whom poets adored"; Manannán as "a celebrated merchant who was in the Isle of Mann", "the best pilot that was in the west of Europe", whom Irish and Britons called god of the sea.
- **Later invention:** a "Celtic Church" against Rome; white-robed druids (Pliny, on Gaul); the tree calendar (1948); Patrick's shamrock (?).
- **Feasts:** Brigit 1 February, Patrick 17 March, Colum Cille 9 June. Wednesday and Friday are fasts.

## 5. How they trade

- **No coin** before Dublin's, and no town but the Norse ports. Prices are said in cows, *séts* and ounces; silver is weighed, and after 850 cut.
- **Where.** At a monastery's gate; at the *óenach* (Tailtiu, at Lugnasad); on a beach under a king's steward.
- **Bargains** need witnesses and sureties who pay if you do not (Kelly).
- **Credit** is cattle: a lord lends a herd; the client repays in food-rent, labour and winter guesting (Kelly).
- **Hosts and gifts.** Turning a guest away costs honour-price. A hospitaller (*briugu*) keeps open house by the road and ranks with a king for it (Kelly).
- **Strangers.** A man from over sea is a grey dog (*cú glas*), with no honour-price until a host stands for him (Kelly).
- **They sell** hides, wool, cloaks, slaves; **they want** wine, salt, silver, swords, glass; monks also pigments and books.

## 6. Ships

The *curach*: a wooden frame, tanned ox-hides, a mast and sail (Voyage of Brendan 4 (?)). The pilgrims' boat of 891 "was made of two hides and a half" (ASC). Dál Riata levied two seven-benchers from every twenty houses (Senchus fer nAlban (?)). By 1000 Irish kings keep Norse-built fleets: Skuldelev 2, a 30 m warship sunk at Roskilde, was built near Dublin of Irish oak in 1042.

## 7. Names

One name, then *mac* (son of) or *ingen* (daughter of) and the father's name: Cináed mac Ailpín, Gormlaith ingen Donnchada. *Ua* is grandson; fixed surnames only begin c. 950-1050 (Ua Briain). Devout names: *Máel-*, shorn servant of a saint, for either sex (Máel Brigte, Máel Muire); *Gilla-*, lad of a saint, from c. 950. Nobody is plain Patrick, Brigit or Mary. *Cú-* hound, *Dub-* black, *Muir-* sea; women's *-laith* lady, *Bé-* woman. Bynames: Niall *Glúndub* (black-knee). Norse names cross over (Amlaíb, Ímar, Sitriuc).

**Men** (AU, deaths 801-1064, in order): Bresal, Connachtach, Cellach, Áed, Blathmac, Óengus, Niall, Fedelmid, Indrechtach, Cináed, Máel Sechnaill, Causantín, Cerball, Suibne, Domnall, Cormac, Flann, Muiredach, Máel Brigte, Muirchertach, Congalach, Dub dá Leithe, Brian, Murchad, Donnchad. ASC 891: Dubsláine, Mac Bethad, Máel Inmuin.

## 8. How they speak in the sources

A learned man opens his answer "Not hard to tell" (Meyer).

1. "Charles, King of the Franks, or rather Emperor of all Europe, slept in peace." (AU 813)
2. "Great abundance of acorns." (AU 806)
3. "Bitter is the wind to-night, / It tosses the ocean's white hair" (Meyer; in `texts_793.json`)
4. "To go to Rome / Is much of trouble, little of profit" (Meyer)
5. "I and my white Pangur / Have each his special art" (Meyer)
6. Of his house: "Never shall it be closed against any one, / Lest Christ close His house against me." (Meyer)
7. "Three sounds of increase: the lowing of a cow in milk; the din of a smithy; the swish of a plough." (Triads; Meyer)
8. "Three false sisters: 'perhaps,' 'may be,' 'I dare say.'" (the same)
9. "Three signs of ill-breeding: a long visit, staring, constant questioning." (the same)
10. "I was a listener in woods, / I was a gazer at stars" (Instructions of King Cormac; Meyer)

## 9. Oaths, insults, jokes

- **Oaths** are sworn on a saint's relics: his bell, staff or gospel book. The sagas' oath by the god my people swear by is literature about the past (?): not for a living mouth.
- **Insults** are law. Satire costs honour-price: mocking a man's looks, a nickname that sticks (Kelly).
- **Jokes** are dry. Scribes' margins, c. 850: "New parchment, bad ink. O I say nothing more."; "The ink is thin."; "Alas! my hand." (Thes. xx-xxii). Two abbots on ale: Máel Ruain, "the liquor that causes forgetfulness of God shall not be drunk here"; Dublitir, "my monks shall drink it, and they shall be in Heaven along with thine" (Monastery of Tallaght 6).

## 10. Neighbours

- **Northmen:** "Gentiles", then "Foreigners" (*Gaill*); soon allies and in-laws.
- **English** (*Saxain*): old pupils and friends. **Picts:** Dál Riata's over-kings, then one kingdom with them.
- **Franks:** the road to Rome and a living for scholars.
- **Frisians:** not named in Irish sources (?). But Willibrord, who converted them, studied twelve years in Ireland first (Alcuin, *Vita Willibrordi* 4 (?)).
- **Said of them.** Notker: two Scots landed in Gaul with British traders, showed no wares and cried "Ho, everyone that desires wisdom, let him draw near and take it at our hands; for it is wisdom that we have for sale". ASC 891: three came to Alfred "in a boat without any oars from Ireland", to be pilgrims "they recked not where". Bede III.27: "The Scots willingly received them all", and taught them "gratis". But bishops distrust wandering Irish clergy (Chalon 813, Chelsea 816 (?)), and Northmen sell them.

## 11. Burial

Unburnt, head to the west, shrouded, no goods, in the ground of the kin's church; kings at Clonmacnoise, Armagh or Hí, to rise beside the saint. Clonmacnoise slabs bear a cross and *ÓR DO*, "a prayer for", and a name (?). Graves with swords and scales at Dublin are Norse.

## 12. What a Frisian merchant must know

1. Bring scales. Nobody counts your pennies; they weigh them, and price in cows.
2. Find a host first: the monastery's guest-master, a hospitaller, the king's steward. Until then you are a grey dog.
3. Name sureties before goods change hands.
4. From Saturday evening to Monday morning move nothing: "An ounce of silver on a man who travels with a load on that day, and his clothes to be burned, and his load to be forfeited" (Cáin Domnaig 23; O'Keeffe 1905).
5. Mock nobody: the fine goes by his rank.
6. Eat what is set before you, and bring news: they write it down.
7. Latin serves with monks; in Dublin after 841, Norse.

## 13. Ten things never to write

1. "Celts", "Celtic"; "Scotland" for north Britain, or Scots and Irish as two peoples.
2. Kilts, tartans, clan chiefs, bagpipes.
3. A living pagan: druid priests, an oath by Lug, offerings at stones.
4. A church at odds with Rome, or the old Easter after 716.
5. Surnames in O' or Mac before c. 1000.
6. Coins, shops, inns or towns outside the Norse ports.
7. Whiskey, potatoes, shamrocks, leprechauns.
8. A high king ruling Ireland, or "Ireland against the Vikings": Irish kings burned monasteries too.
9. Monks who are all gentle hermits: the abbot is a lord with kin and spears.
10. Stage Irish ("begorrah"), or a load carried on Sunday.
