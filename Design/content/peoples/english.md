# The English, 793-1066: Mercians, West Saxons, Kentish, East Angles, Northumbrians

For the writers of dialogue. Quotations were checked against the printed translations; `(?)` marks a fact not re-checked. **ASC** Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (Ingram 1823; its annal years, the true year in brackets); **Laws** Thorpe 1840; **Bede** (Stevens, revised Giles); **S** Sawyer charter number; **WM** William of Malmesbury (Sharpe 1815).

## 1. Who they are, and how that changes

- **793.** Kingdoms, not one people. Offa of Mercia (d. 796) is overlord south of the Humber: Kent and Lundenwic are his, he beheads the East Angles' king, Beorhtric of Wessex is his son-in-law (ASC 787, 792). Northumbria kills its own kings. A man is a Mercian or a Kentishman first.
- **825-851.** Wessex beats Mercia and takes Kent (ASC 823 [825]). Heathen men kill in London (839 [842]) and first winter on Thanet (851).
- **865-886.** The great army takes York, East Anglia and half Mercia; Halfdan "divided the land of the Northumbrians" (ASC 876). Alfred holds Wessex and takes London (ASC 886). Now *Angelcynn*, the English kin, means something.
- **900-954.** Wessex wins back the Danish lands; York falls for good in 954. One king, two laws: fines are priced "among the Danes" and "among the English" (Laws).
- **973-1066.** One coinage (973); tribute from 991; a Danish king (1016), an English (1042), a Norman. Earls replace ealdormen.

## 2. How they live

- **House.** A timber hall, thatch, a middle hearth, wall benches; stone is for churches. WM: they "consumed their whole substance in mean and despicable houses".
- **Food and drink.** Bread, bean pottage, leeks, bacon; fish on fast days. A food-rent lists loaves, ale, honey, cheeses, butter, salmon, eels (Ine 70.1). Ale is everyone's, mead for feasts, wine for lords and the Mass: the boy in Ælfric's Colloquy (c. 1000) cannot afford it.
- **Dress.** Men: knee-length tunic, belt and knife, bound hose, a cloak pinned at the right shoulder. Married women: a long gown and head-cloth.
- **The year.** Plough after Christmas, sow in March, shear in June, reap from Lammas, slaughter at Martinmas (calendar pictures, Cotton Julius A.vi). Freemen rest twelve days at Yule and "in harvest the whole week before St. Mary-mass" (Laws, Alfred 43).

## 3. Law and rank

- **Wergild**, a man's price. "A ceorl's wergeld is by the Mercian law 200 shillings. A thane's wergeld is six times as much" (Laws); the same in Wessex. Northumbria counts in thrymsas (ceorl 266, thegn 2,000); Kent in its own shillings (100; Æthelberht 21).
- **Oaths** weigh by rank: "A twelve-hynde man's oath stands for six ceorls' oaths" (Laws). Cases are won with oath-helpers; later by ordeal.
- **Courts.** In 793 the folk-moot under the king's reeve; later the hundred every four weeks (Laws, Edgar) and the shire. In the Danelaw, the wapentake.
- **Slavery** is ordinary. The thegn Imma was sold at London to a Frisian (Bede IV.22). WM: the powerful sold common people "into foreign countries".
- **Women.** The morning-gift is the wife's own. Women hold, sue for and will land: Wynflæd won her suit c. 990 with women among her oath-helpers (S 1454). Æthelflæd ruled Mercia until 918. In trade they are attested as weavers, cheese-makers and owners of town plots; no English woman merchant is named (?).

## 4. Faith

- Christian since 597-686: "the holy Pope Gregory sent us baptism" (ASC 565). In 793 nobody remembers otherwise.
- **Attested of the old religion:** the day names; Bede's months (Eostre, Hretha, Modranecht; *De temporum ratione* 15); place names (Wednesbury, Harrow); Woden in the kings' pedigrees and the Nine Herbs Charm; the priest Coifi profaning his own shrine (Bede II.13).
- **Later invention:** an English pantheon copied from the Icelandic Eddas; Wyrd as a goddess; Eostre's hare and eggs.
- The Danes bring heathenism back and are baptised within two generations (ASC 878).
- **Saints:** Gregory (12 March), Cuthbert (20 March), Edmund (20 November, from c. 895), Swithun (15 July, from 971), Ælfheah, whom drunken Danes "overwhelmed ... with bones and horns of oxen" (ASC 1012).
- **Dues and bans:** church-scot at Martinmas. "If any one engage in Sunday marketing, let him forfeit the chattel" (Laws, Edward and Guthrum 7).

## 5. How they trade

- **Coin.** South of the Humber only the king's silver penny, 240 to the pound; pounds and shillings are counting words (a West Saxon shilling is 5 pence, a Mercian 4). Northumbria strikes base stycas until 867. From Æthelstan "one money over all the king's dominion" (Laws); after 973 foreign coin is re-struck, so a Frisian's deniers go to the moneyer first. In the Danelaw, c. 870-930, silver also goes by weight (Cuerdale hoard).
- **Witnesses.** No buying "out of port" without "the port-reeve's witness" (Laws, Edward I 1).
- **Strangers.** A trader going up-country brings his men before the king's reeve (Alfred 34). A stranger off the road who neither shouts nor blows a horn is taken for a thief (Ine 20). A host answers for a guest kept three nights (Hlothhere and Eadric 15).
- **Tolls.** The king's toll on ships at Lundenwic (S 86 (?)). At Billingsgate c. 1000 a small ship pays a halfpenny, a keel or hulk four pence; the Emperor's men also give cloth, pepper, gloves and vinegar at Christmas and Easter (IV Æthelred 2).
- **Sell them** wine, Rhenish quern-stones (Charlemagne's "black stones" (?)), glass, pots; later pepper and silk. **Buy** cloaks (Charlemagne asked Offa for the old length, 796), slaves, lead, tin, hides, honey, wool.
- **Credit and gifts.** Debts are sworn, not written (see 8). Give the reeve his gift before he asks.

## 6. Ships

Clinker-built oak, one square sail, the steering oar to starboard. The Graveney boat (Kent, c. 895-950, about 14 m) carried hops and Rhenish querns (Fenwick 1978 (?)). Alfred's long ships of sixty oars "were not shaped either after the Frisian or the Danish model"; three Frisians died in their crews (ASC 897). Words: *scip*, *ceol*, *bat*, *hulc*; a Danish warship is an *æsc*.

## 7. Names

Two parts, each a word: Æthel- (noble), Ead- (wealth), Ælf- (elf), Wulf-, Ecg- (edge), Leof- (dear), Beorht- (bright). Men end in -ric, -red, -wulf, -stan, -weard, -mund, -wine, -noth; women in -gifu, -flæd, -thryth, -burh, -gyth, -swith, -wynn, -waru. Kin share a part (Æthelwulf's sons Æthelbald, Æthelred, Ælfred). Short names are common (Offa, Abba). No surnames: a byname (Eadric *Streona*, "the grasper"), a father ("Odda's son") or a place. Norse names join in the Danelaw after 880.

**Men.** ASC: Offa, Ecgfrith, Beorhtric, Ecgberht, Æthelheard, Burgred, Eadmund, Ælfred, Eadweard, Æthelstan, Eadgar, Dunstan, Eadric, Godwine, Leofric. Maldon: Byrhtnoth, Byrhtwold, Dunnere, Leofsunu, Wulfmær. Others: Higbald, bishop of Lindisfarne in 793 (Alcuin's letters); Beaduheard, the reeve killed c. 789 (Æthelweard); Abba, a Kentish reeve (S 1482); Helmstan, a thief (Fonthill letter, S 1445); Wulfstan, who sailed to Truso (`texts_793.json`).

## 8. How they speak in the sources

1. "The present life of man, O king, seems to me ... like to the swift flight of a sparrow through the room wherein you sit at supper in winter." (Bede II.13)
2. "The reve then rode thereto, and would drive them to the king's town; for he knew not what they were; and there was he slain." (ASC 787)
3. "Dear is the welcome guest to the Frisian wife, when the vessel stands." (Exeter Maxims; Thorpe 1842, p. 339)
4. "Frost shall freeze; fire wood consume; earth bring forth; ice break." (the same)
5. "If any Kentish-man buy a chattel in Lundenwic, let him then have two or three true men to witness, or the king's wic-reeve." (Laws, Hlothhere and Eadric 16)
6. "And if a merchant thrived, so that he fared thrice over the wide sea by his own means, then was he thenceforth of thane-right worthy." (Laws, Of people's ranks, c. 1000)
7. "... as I here for N. in true witness stand, unbidden and unbought, so I with my eyes over-saw, and with my ears over-heard, that which I with him say." (Laws, Oaths 8)
8. "In the name of the living God, I owe not to N. sceatt or shilling, or penny or penny's worth." (Oaths 11)
9. "Goes Weird as she must go!" (Beowulf 455; Hall 1892)
10. "The bolder be each heart, / Each spirit sterner, valor more, now that our strength is less!" (Maldon; Lumsden 1902 (?))

## 9. Oaths, insults, jokes

- **Oaths** are sworn on relics: "By the Lord, before whom this relic is holy, I will be to N. faithful and true" (Laws, Oaths 1). A ring-oath is Danish: at Wareham they swore "on the holy bracelet, which they would not before to any nation" (ASC 876).
- **Insults.** *Nithing*, a man without honour: the king and the whole army named Earl Swein one (ASC C 1049; Ingram has "outlaw"). *Wealh* means both Welshman and slave. Byrhtnoth to the Danes' herald: "To you they give no tribute but the spear" (Maldon).
- **Jokes.** Riddles with a decent and an indecent answer (the Exeter onion and key); Gregory's pun on Angles and angels (Bede II.1).

## 10. Neighbours

- **Welsh:** foreigners beyond the dyke; their name is the word for slave.
- **Franks:** wine, books, fashions. Offa and Charlemagne shut their ports to each other c. 790 (Alcuin, Ep. 7).
- **Old Saxons and Frisians:** kin, of one blood and one bone (Boniface, Ep. 46). English monks converted Frisia; Frisian merchants lived at York until one killed an earl's son and all fled, c. 772 (Altfrid, *Vita Liudgeri* I.11-12).
- **Danes:** "heathen men", "the army"; in 1002 the king ordered "all the Danes that were in England" slain (ASC).
- **Said of the English.** Papal legates, 786: they eat horse, dress like heathens and settle suits by lot (Alcuin, Ep. 3). WM: "accustomed to eat till they became surfeited, and to drink till they were sick". Welsh and Irish call them all Saxons.

## 11. Burial

Unburnt, east-west, no goods, in a minster's graveyard, later the parish's. Soul-scot is paid at the grave; a thegn's horses and weapons go to his lord (Cnut II 71). Perjurers lie out of holy ground (Æthelstan II 26). Danish settlers first burn or mound their dead (Repton, 873-4).

## 12. What a Frisian merchant must know

1. Go to the reeve first, and say how many you are.
2. Buy and sell before witnesses; after 900, only in a port.
3. Change your coin; nobody weighs silver here until the Danes come.
4. The king is your only kin: he takes two-thirds of a slain foreigner's wergild (Ine 23).
5. Keep to the road, keep Sunday, keep Lent.
6. They call you kin, and remember that you buy slaves.

## 13. Ten things never to write

1. "Anglo-Saxons", "England" before c. 1000, a "king of the English" before 927.
2. "Vikings" for Danes. *Wicing* means pirate.
3. An English heathen, or an oath by Woden.
4. A "thing" in English mouths: moot, hundred, shire.
5. "Danegeld": it is *gafol* from 991, *heregeld* from 1012.
6. An English "earl" before Cnut, a "sheriff" before c. 990, a "queen" in ninth-century Wessex (Asser 13).
7. A pound or shilling as a coin; a broad silver penny struck at York before c. 895.
8. Knights or castles: in 1055 "the English people fled, because they were on horses" (ASC).
9. Horse-meat, Sunday markets, meat in Lent.
10. Surnames, "sir", "my lady": the attested polite word is *leof*, "dear" (Fonthill letter).
