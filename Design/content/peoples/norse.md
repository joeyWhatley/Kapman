# The Northmen of Norway and the lands west over sea, 793-1066

For the writers of dialogue. `(?)` = not checked tonight. **late** = written in the 12th-13th century about earlier days (sagas, Snorri, law books): colour, not fact. Game ids: people `norse`; ports `skiringssal`, `ogvaldsnes`, `duiblinn` (Norse from 841).

## 1. Who they are, and how that changes

- **793 to c. 870: no Norway.** A man is a Hörðr, a Rygr or a Háleygr before he is a Northman. England's first three ships (entry for 787) are "Northmen from the land of robbers", Hörðaland (ASC).
- **c. 870 to 930: one king in the west, and the leaving.** Harald Fairhair wins at Hafrsfjord (traditionally 872). Shetland, Orkney, the Hebrides and Man are settled; Dublin is a ship-fort from 841 (AU); Iceland is taken from about 870, with one law and no king from 930 (Ari). Ohthere's account, about 890, has the first written *Norðweg*.
- **930 to 995:** Hákon, fostered in England (c. 934-961); then the earls of Lade under Danish kings.
- **995 to 1066: Christian kings:** Olaf Tryggvason (995-1000), Olaf Haraldsson (1015-28; killed at Stiklestad, 29 July 1030), Magnus (1035-47), Harald Hardrada (1046-66). Greenland from about 985; Newfoundland by 1021.

## 2. How they live

- **House:** one long room, earth floor, a hearth down the middle, smoke out through the roof, benches along the walls for sitting and sleeping. The chief's hall at Borg in Lofoten was 83 m long.
- **Food:** barley porridge, flat bread, butter, cheese, sour milk, dried cod, meat from the autumn slaughter. Ale for all, mead at feasts, wine hardly ever. Ohthere, a great man, had 20 cattle, 20 sheep, 20 pigs and 600 reindeer.
- **Dress:** wool and linen. A woman's strap dress hangs from two oval brooches, beads between, keys at the belt (graves). A man: tunic, breeches, cloak.
- **The year:** sow, then go to sea; hay at high summer; home for harvest; slaughter and the Winter Nights feast in mid-October; Yule at midwinter; cod in Lofoten in late winter (Egils saga, late).

## 3. Law and rank

- **The thing.** Free men meet armed: the Gulathing in the west (10th century), the Althing in Iceland (from 930, two weeks at midsummer), where a lawspeaker says the law from memory (Ari). In Iceland only the winner enforces a verdict.
- **Rank** (Gulathing law, written c. 1100-1260 from older custom): thrall, freedman, farmer, *hauldr* (born to inherited land), king's landed man, jarl, king. A man's price for an injury doubles each step: freedman 6 ounces, farmer 12, hauldr 3 marks, landed man 6, jarl 12 (?). A killing is paid kin to kin. A foreign trader has a farmer's right (?).
- **Proof** is an oath, with three, six or twelve men swearing beside you.
- **Thralls** are bought, born or taken, many Irish (women carried off from Howth, AU 821). A freed thrall gives a freedom-ale.
- **Women** keep the keys and the farm; inherit where there is no son and can leave a husband (late). Unn the Deep-minded had a ship built and took her household to Iceland (Landnámabók). Oseberg, the richest ship grave (834), is two women's. Scales lie in women's graves too (Stalsberg 1991 (?)): she may weigh your silver.

## 4. Faith

- **Attested in its own time:** Thor, Frey, Njord and Ull in Norwegian place-names, Odin seldom (?); Thor's hammers on a cord (10th century); kings welcomed by Odin in Valhöll (Hákonarmál, c. 961); a king "drinking Yule" at sea (Haraldskvæði, c. 900); an autumn sacrifice to the elves behind shut doors, strangers turned away (Sigvat, c. 1019); a feasting hall hung with cattle skulls (Hofstaðir, Iceland).
- **Late or invented:** Snorri's tidy family of gods (c. 1220); temples with priests; the blood eagle; Valhalla for every fighting man; fortune-telling runes.
- **Conversion:** Olaf Tryggvason forced the coast, 995-1000. Iceland took baptism by law in 999 or 1000, keeping for a time horse meat, the exposing of infants and private sacrifice (Ari). Olaf Haraldsson and Bishop Grimkel gave church law at Moster, c. 1024. The Kuli stone (1034) says: twelve winters had Christendom been in Norway.
- **Saints and feasts:** St Olaf, 29 July, from 1031; St Hallvard (died 1043). Yule keeps its name: the law orders Christmas ale blessed *til árs ok friðar*, for a good year and peace (Gulathing).

## 5. Trade

- **Silver by weight.** 1 mark (about 214 g) = 8 ounces = 24 *ørtugar*. Every trader carries folding scales and lead weights and nicks silver to test it: your pennies are bullion. First Norse pennies: Olaf Tryggvason, c. 995, a handful; Dublin 997; a real coinage under Harald Hardrada (?). In Iceland cloth is money: six ells of homespun make a legal ounce (Grágás).
- **They sell:** whetstones (at Ribe before 750: Baug and Skre 2019), soapstone pots, iron, antler, furs, down, walrus tooth and walrus-hide rope (Ohthere), dried fish, thralls; from Iceland cloth and shaggy cloaks.
- **They want:** grain, malt, wine, honey, fine cloth, glass, Frankish swords (Haraldskvæði), silver.
- **Markets:** Skíringssalr (Kaupang), about 800 to the mid-900s, with a Frisian trading household in the 9th century (Skre 2010); later Nidaros and Oslo.
- **Gifts, hosts, credit:** a gift asks a gift. A stranger needs a host who answers for him; a shipmaster winters as a guest. A bargain is a handclasp before witnesses (Grágás). King Harald took one grey cloak as a gift and the Icelanders' whole cargo sold (Heimskringla, late). Partnership (*félag*) is on Danish and Swedish stones about 1000; in Norway written only later.

## 6. Ships

Clinker-built, iron-riveted, one square wool sail, the rudder on the right. Oseberg: 21.6 m, 15 pairs of oars (buried 834). Gokstad: 23.8 m, 16 pairs (c. 890). The sea trader (*knörr*): Skuldelev 1, 15.8 m, pine, built in Sogn c. 1030, crew 6-8. Game classes: `norse_small_boat`, `norse_allround_ship`, `norse_longship`. Hålogaland to Skíringssalr: over a month, anchoring nightly; on to Hedeby five days (Ohthere). Norway to Iceland: seven days (Landnámabók). Nobody sails in winter.

## 7. Names

**Men:** Þórir, Hallvarðr (Kuli stone, 1034); Engli, Arnsteinn, Bjórr, Eyvindr, Erlingr, Gunnarr (stones N 62, 184, 210, 252, 84 (?)); Gautr, Sandulfr, Þorvaldr, Þorleifr, Oddr, Þorsteinn, Þorbjörn, Grímr, Heðinn, Sigurðr, Ófeigr (crosses of Man, c. 930-1000); Óttarr (Ohthere); Óláfr, Ívarr (AU 853, 857); Hákon (ASC 1030); Ketill, Ingólfr (Landnámabók).

**Women:** Gunnvör, Ástríðr (Dynna stone, c. 1040); Jórunn (Alstad, N 61 (?)); Sigríðr (Oseberg bucket (?)); Arinbjörg, Fríða, Hlífhildr, Þuríðr (Man); Auðr, Hallveig, Þórunn, Steinunn, Ásgerðr, Ljót, Þorgerðr, Arndís, Geirríðr, Vigdís, Þórdís, Helga, Gróa, Guðríðr (Landnámabók); Hildr, Gunnhildr, Álfhildr (in verse, c. 900-1035).

**Built** from two known pieces; they need not make sense together. First: Þór- (commonest), Ás-, Arn-, Ey-, Guð-, Gunn-, Hall-, Ing-, Sig-, Stein-. Men's ends: -steinn, -björn, -ólfr, -leifr, -valdr, -grímr, -kell. Women's: -dís, -gerðr, -hildr, -ríðr, -unn, -björg, -veig. Then the father (Ketils son, Hrólfs dóttir) and a nickname others gave: Flat-nose, the Black, Neck. No Jón or Pétr before about 1000; the first Magnus is born in 1024. In the west Gaelic names marry in: Mael Brigde, Malmury (Man).

## 8. How they speak: ten lines

Short, concrete, understated; a proverb ends an argument. 1-9: Hávamál (H. A. Bellows 1923; stanzas as in `texts_793.json`; caesura marks dropped). Manuscript c. 1270, but line 8 also opens the last stanza of Hákonarmál (c. 961).

1. "Wits must he have who wanders wide, / But all is easy at home." (5)
2. "Less good there lies than most believe / In ale for mortal men." (12)
3. "Love becomes loathing if long one sits / By the hearth in another's home." (35)
4. "A man is master at home." (36)
5. "Away from his arms in the open field / A man should fare not a foot." (38)
6. "Gift-givers' friendships are longest found." (41)
7. "The live man catches the cow." (70)
8. "Cattle die, and kinsmen die, / And so one dies one's self; / But a noble name will never die, / If good renown one gets." (76)
9. "Give praise to the day at evening, ... / To ice when it is crossed, to ale that is drunk." (81)
10. "He said that no man abode north of him." (Ohthere to King Alfred, c. 890; J. Bosworth 1859)

## 9. Oaths, insults, jokes

- **Oaths** are sworn on a ring (Hávamál 110; the Danes to Alfred, ASC 876). The peace-oath casts a truce-breaker out wherever fire burns, ships sail and the hawk flies (Grágás (?)).
- **Insults are law.** Words that cost full payment or outlawry: that a man has borne a child, was used as a woman, or is a mare or a bitch (Gulathing 196). When a Danish steward seized an Icelandic cargo, Iceland voted a mocking verse a head against his king (Heimskringla, late).
- **Jokes** are dry, in nicknames and understatement: King Eystein "Fart", Halfdan "Open-handed and Stingy with Food" (Ari); Sigvat shut out by three farmers of one name in one evening (c. 1019); Thormod at Stiklestad, pulling the arrow out: the king fed us well, there is fat about my heart (Heimskringla, late).

## 10. Neighbours

**They of others.** The Sami pay skins, down and hide rope (Ohthere) and are feared for sorcery (late). The Danes are kings who want the Vik (ARF 813). The Svear are heathen and shut their doors (Sigvat). The English give bishops and coin; the Irish thralls, wives and allies; the Franks the best blades. The Frisians are traders, and fair game abroad: Olaf Tryggvason "gave the blood of many a Frisian" to the wolves (Hallfreðr, c. 996; Vigfusson and Powell 1883).

**Others of them.** "the harrowing inroads of heathen men" (ASC 793). An Irish monk, c. 850: "Bitter is the wind to-night, / It tosses the ocean's white hair: / To-night I fear not the fierce warriors of Norway" (K. Meyer 1911). Irish annals: *Gaill*, foreigners. Dicuil (825): "the Northern robbers" (J. Anderson 1873). Adam of Bremen (c. 1075): a poor land that sent its men to sea, quiet since baptism; Icelanders have no king, only law (?).

## 11. Burial

Heathen: burnt or unburnt, under a mound on the farm, with tools, weapons, scales, horse and dog; the great in a ship (Oseberg 834, Gokstad about 900). Christian: the churchyard, nothing in the grave; outlaws and suicides at the tide mark (Gulathing 23).

## 12. What a Frisian merchant must know

1. Bring scales and weights: every penny is weighed and nicked.
2. Skíringssalr lies five days beyond Hedeby; Frisians keep a house there until Dorestad fails.
3. Find a host before you unload: his word is your safety, your gift his toll.
4. Never sell a sword or mail-coat: Frankish law forbids it (805), on pain of death from 864.
5. Thralls are offered like other goods, Christians among them.
6. A heathen who has taken the "first signing" may eat and deal with Christians (Egils saga, late).
7. Never joke about a man's manhood.
8. Leave before the Winter Nights or stay till spring.
9. Before 870 ask whose man he is, not which king's. An Icelander never has a king.

## 13. Never

1. "Viking" as their own name: a *víkingr* is a man out raiding.
2. Horned or winged helmets. The one helmet found (Gjermundbu, c. 970) is a round iron cap with an eye-guard.
3. Skull cups (a 1636 mistranslation), or the blood eagle as custom.
4. "Norway" as a kingdom before about 900; Nidaros before 997, Oslo before 1000, Bergen before 1070.
5. Coins of their own before 995; coin prices in Iceland.
6. Iceland before 870, Greenland before 985, Vinland before 1000.
7. Christian names, churches or crosses in Norway before about 950.
8. Gods explained like a handbook; "Valhalla!" as a war cry; the 24-rune row (they use 16).
9. "Drakkar" (19th-century French); berserks on mushrooms (1784); Ragnar Lothbrok as a known man.
10. A compass; sailing in winter; the word "adventure".
