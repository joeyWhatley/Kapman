## The story in five sentences

Between 947 and 954 York has a new king almost every second year, each with his own penny, and a merchant's safe-conduct dies with the king who gave it.
A townsman of mixed blood, Ulfketil, takes the player under his roof and shows him the three doors a stranger can knock at: the king's hall, Saint Peter's minster, and the Dublin ships at the landing.
The player pays (or slips, or carries) the silver the town owes King Eadred for the burning year of 948, carries combs and jet to Dublin for Olaf's men, buys or refuses Eric's peace on the river, carries the minster's letter towards its archbishop in the English king's prison, and is offered a purse at Bamburgh for one piece of news.
In the summer of 954 the lawmen meet without the king, Eric means to ride west over Stainmore, and the player knows something Eric's shipmaster does not.
He can stand with the town, sell the moor road to the earl, take the king down the Ouse by night, or call the Dublin ships over the hills: four ends, two of them history as it was and two of them history changed.

## How a player meets it

- It starts by itself at Eoforwic on any call from 947 to 951: card `s01_whose_penny` (weight 1.5, once). Asking who is king is enough to begin.
- A place writer may add `card:harbour.chain_york_954.s01_whose_penny` to the York mint or landing site.
- After step 1 the story stays at York, with three side roads a player may or may not sail: Duiblinn (step 5), London (step 7) and Bebbanburg (step 8). The Dublin ending needs step 5; the others do not need any side road.
- A player who starts and then stays away is not left with nothing: anyone past step 2 gets the Stainmore ending at York from August 954 to 958.

## The real history under it

**The kings.** Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, D and E texts (quoted from J. Ingram's translation of 1823, as printed by the Avalon Project, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/ang10.asp, read 6 Oct 2026):

- 947: "This year came King Edred to Tadden's-cliff; and there Archbishop Wulfstan and all the council of the Northumbrians bound themselves to an allegiance with the king. And within a little space they abandoned all, both allegiance and oaths."
- 948: "This year King Edred overran all Northumberland; because they had taken Eric for their king", and Ripon minster was burned; the army in York fell on his rear at Castleford; "the king was so wroth, that he would fain return with his force, and lay waste the land withal; but when the council of the Northumbrians understood that, they then abandoned Eric, and compromised the deed with King Edred." (Step 3. How the payment was raised is not recorded (?).)
- 949: "This year came Anlaf Curran to the land of the Northumbrians." (Step 4: Olaf Sihtricson, Amlaíb Cuarán, king at Dublin 945 to 947 and 952 to 980.)
- 952: "This year the Northumbrians expelled King Anlaf, and received Eric the son of Harold. This year also King Edred ordered Archbishop Wulfstan to be brought into prison at Jedburgh." (Step 6. The Old English has Iudanbyrig; where that was is not known (?).)
- 954: "This year the Northumbrians expelled Eric; and King Edred took to the government of the Northumbrians. This year also Archbishop Wulfstan received a bishopric again at Dorchester." (Both history endings quote one sentence each.)

**Stainmore.** Roger of Wendover, Flores historiarum, under 950 (a 13th-century writer using lost northern annals): "rex Eilricus in quadam solitudine quae 'Steinmor' dicitur, cum filio suo Henrico et fratre Reginaldo, proditione Osulfi comitis, a Macone consule fraudulenter interempti sunt": King Eric was killed in a lonely place called Stainmore with his son and his brother, by the treachery of Earl Oswulf, by Earl Maccus (our rendering). Elsewhere Maccus is "son of Olaf". Whether that Olaf was of the Dublin house is a guess that cannot be proved (?) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Bloodaxe, read 6 Oct 2026). Oswulf of Bamburgh was earl of all Northumbria afterwards (Historia regum 952 for 954; De primo Saxonum adventu). The month of Eric's fall is not recorded: the game puts step 10 between April and July 954 and the endings from August (?).

**Who Eric was.** The Chronicle calls him only "Eric the son of Harold". That he was Eric Bloodaxe, son of Harald Fairhair of Norway, is the sagas' telling, two and a half centuries later, and has been doubted (?). No card uses the byname. The Life of Saint Cathroe has a king "Erichius" at York with a wife who was the saint's kinswoman (?); Egil's saga puts Egil and his friend Arinbjorn in Eric's hall (13th century): neither is used.

**The money.** `realms/northumbria_york.json` (coins 939-954), from Blunt, Stewart and Lyon, Coinage in Tenth-Century England (1989) and Blackburn 2004: Olaf Guthfrithson's raven and ANLAF CVNVNC; a three-cornered knot and a war-banner for Olaf Sihtricson; English pennies of Edmund and Eadred between; ERIC REX, the second time with a sword. The moneyer Ingelgar struck for Olaf Sihtricson and for Eric, and is known from his coins alone; that he also struck for Eadred after 954 is not checked (?).

**The town and the road.** York's long fenced plots, laid out from about 900 (Hall 1984, Coppergate), its comb makers and jet cutters, its silk; Dublin's plots, plank paths and earthen bank of about 950 (Wallace, Wood Quay), its trade in hides, cloaks and slaves (`dossiers/eoforwic.md`, `dossiers/duiblinn.md`). In 951 the foreigners of Dublin plundered Kells and carried off three thousand people (Annals of Ulster 951, by way of `realms/irish_sea.md`; not opened tonight (?)). The road between the two towns went over the Pennines, by Stainmore among other ways. The four lawmen of York are Domesday's judices, read back a century (?). King Eadred's sickness (he could only suck the juice from his food) is in B.'s Life of Saint Dunstan, c. 20 (?).

**What is invented.** The player's part in all of it. That a stranger traded through a host; that the payment of 948 was gathered from hulls on the landing; that Oswulf bought news from a merchant; that a letter went from the minster to the archbishop in prison; every ordinary person below.

**What would follow from the changed endings** (for later cards to read the flags):

- `changed_stainmore_eric_lives`: a king without a town holds Orkney and the Isles with a fleet for some years more, so the east coast from the Tees to the Tay is raided in the later 950s when it was in fact quiet; his sons have a father's fleet behind them when they go back to Norway against Hakon the Good (about 961 in the sagas (?)), and may win sooner. Oswulf is still earl, but an earl with a living enemy at sea: Bebbanburg is hostile to the player (`ch_york_954_oswulf_feud`).
- `changed_york_dublin_kingdom_954`: York and Dublin stay one kindred's kingdom. The hides, slaves and silver go on crossing the hills; York keeps a Norse king's penny; Eadred (who dies in November 955) leaves the north unfinished to his nephews, and Edgar's law of about 962 for "the Danes" would be made with a king at York, not an earl. The English king's ports search the player's hulls (`ch_york_954_eadreds_enemy`). Olaf in fact ruled Dublin to 980, so he had the years for it.

**Why a merchant could have won these.** Toll freedom and the king's peace given by hand are what Domesday records as custom at York (read back (?)); a seat among sworn witnesses to sales is what Edgar's law for the Danes requires a few years later; plots at York and Dublin changed hands in exactly these years; a felag, the sworn fellowship of two owners, is the Norse merchants' own form; a ship or a gold ring is what a sea-king gave a man he owed; a factor is a trusted man with a key.

## How long it takes

Seven years at the longest (a start in 947, an ending in August 954 or later), about three and a half at the shortest (a start late in 951). The waits are real: ten days, thirty days, then the years themselves: 948 for the payment, 949 for Olaf, 952 for Eric, the spring of 954 for the choice and the August after it for the end. The chain asks for five or six calls at York over those years and leaves the rest of the time for ordinary trade and other chains.
