## The story in five sentences

In the week after Easter 1066 a star with hair stands over every harbour of the North Sea, and a skipper in from London can name the three men it might be for: Harold, hallowed king of the English the day King Edward was buried; William, duke of the Normans, who is building ships; and Harald, king of the Norwegians, who is saying nothing.
Each of the three needs what a merchant house has: the English king needs bread for a fleet that has lain off Wight since May, the duke needs rope, hulls and hired transports, and the Norwegian king needs a pilot for the Humber and pays by weight because his own pennies are two parts copper.
The house can sell to any of them or to none, and twice it stands where the year turns: in London in the first week of September, when the English fleet's food is gone and 600 pennies of Rhine corn would keep it at sea, and at York on the Sunday night before Stamford Bridge, when a carter has seen King Harold's army at Tadcaster and the Norwegians mean to go to the bridge next day without their mail.
If the house does nothing, the year goes as it went: the fleet goes home on 8 September, the Norwegian king dies on the 25th and twenty-four ships of three hundred go home, the duke lands on the 28th, and King Harold is killed on 14 October.
At midwinter the duke is hallowed king at Westminster while his guard burns the houses outside, and the house reckons up: a thegn's right under a King Harold who kept his ships, a writ from King William, a seat at a Norwegian king's Yule in York, or its own year written on one skin for the heirs.

## How a player meets it

- It starts by itself: `s01_star` is drawn (weight 8, once) at any port from April to June 1066.
- Each road has its own door, and none of them needs step 1: `s03_wight` at London (May to August), `s04_rouen` at Rouen (March to August), `s05_solund` at Ögvaldsnes (May to August), `s02_tostig` in the Kentish havens (May). A house that met none of them still meets the turns (`s06_larder` at London in August and September, `s07_saint_valery` at Quentovic or Rouen in September, `s08_riccall` at York in September), the aftermaths (`s09_dover`, `s10_twentyfour`) and an ending.
- Every house that is afloat at midwinter 1066 gets an ending: `end_house` is drawn at any port from December 1066 for everyone who has not earned one of the other three.
- A place writer may add `card:harbour.chain_year_1066.s03_wight` to London's hythe, `card:harbour.chain_year_1066.s04_rouen` to Rouen's yards, `card:harbour.chain_year_1066.s05_solund` to the landing at Ögvaldsnes and `card:harbour.chain_year_1066.s08_riccall` to the staith at York.

## The real history under it

**The death of Edward and the star.** King Edward died on 5 January 1066 and was buried on Twelfth Day in his new church at Westminster; Harold was hallowed king the same day (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle C, D, E 1066; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-01-05-edward-dies). A sign was seen in the heavens such as no man had seen before; some said it was the star *cometa*, which some call the long-haired star; it appeared first on the eve of the Greater Litany, 24 April, and shone all the week (ASC C, D: our rendering; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-04-24-halley; the Bayeux Tapestry).

**Tostig.** Earl Tostig, driven out of Northumbria in 1065, wintered with Count Baldwin in Flanders (ASC D 1065). Soon after the star he came from over sea into Wight with as large a fleet as he could get, was given money and food there, did harm along the coast as far as Sandwich, and took of the boatmen (*butsecarlas*) with him, some willingly and some unwillingly. King Harold in London gathered a ship-force and a land-force greater than any king in this land had gathered before. Tostig went north with sixty ships into Lindsey; the earls Edwin and Morcar drove him out; the boatmen forsook him; he went to Scotland with twelve small ships (ASC C 1066: our rendering). Copsi joining him from Orkney with seventeen ships is Gaimar (?).

**The fleet off Wight.** Harold lay at Wight all the summer and the harvest, with a land-force kept everywhere by the sea. When it was the Nativity of Saint Mary (8 September) the men's food was gone and no man could keep them there any longer; the men were let go home, the king rode up, and the ships were sent to London, and many were lost before they came there (ASC C 1066: our rendering; `realms/england_south.md`, 1060s: "the fleet sent home three weeks too soon").

**The Norwegians.** Harald, king of Norway, came with three hundred ships (ASC D and E); Tostig joined him. Snorri (Haralds saga Sigurðarsonar 79 to 87, written about 1230: saga (?)) has a half levy of the whole land, the ships gathered in the Solund isles, his son Magnus left as king and Olaf taken west, and the mail left at the ships on a hot day with a third of the host. The earls fought them on the vigil of Saint Matthew, 20 September, at Fulford and were beaten; York gave hostages and food and agreed to go south with them. King Harold came on the Sunday to Tadcaster, on the Monday went through York and came on them unawares beyond the bridge; Harald and Tostig were killed. Olaf, the bishop and the earl of Orkney swore peace and were let go home with twenty-four ships (ASC C, D: our rendering; John of Worcester 1066; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-09-25-stamford-bridge; `realms/norway.md`, scene 10). The Norwegian king's penny of the 1050s and 1060s is about one third silver (the coins themselves; `realms/norway.md`, 1050s); Halldor Snorrason throwing down his pay is Morkinskinna (?).

**The duke.** William had ships built and gathered through the spring and summer, fed a great host for a month at the mouth of the Dives at his own cost and forbade plunder, moved to Saint-Valery in the Somme with losses (the drowned were buried in secret), waited there for a wind, had the body of Saint Valery carried out of the church, sailed by night with a lantern at his masthead, and landed at Pevensey (William of Poitiers, Gesta Guillelmi II.1-8: our rendering; the weathercock is the Carmen de Hastingae Proelio (?); the Ship List gives the magnates' quotas, in a 12th-century copy (?); 12 to 27 September at Saint-Valery: `realms/francia.md`). He landed on Michaelmas eve, 28 September (ASC D; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-09-28-pevensey). King Harold was killed on 14 October, with his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine (ASC D; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-10-14-hastings). Ships that went astray landed at Romney and their crews were killed; Dover surrendered and was fired by the duke's squires, and he paid for the damage; many of his men fell sick there of a flux (WP II.26-28).

**Midwinter.** Archbishop Ealdred of York hallowed him king at Westminster on midwinter's day; he swore to rule as well as the best kings before him, and nevertheless laid a heavy tax on the people (ASC D 1066; `chronicle_793.json` 1066-12-25-william-crowned). The English and the French were each asked in their own tongue, and the guard outside, taking the shout for treachery, set fire to the houses near the church (WP II.30). His writ to London of about 1067 makes the burghers, French and English, worthy of all the laws they were worthy of in King Edward's day, and every child his father's heir (our rendering).

**The merchants.** The men of the Emperor who came in their ships were held at London worthy of good laws like the Londoners, and gave at Christmas and at Easter two grey cloths and a brown one, ten pounds of pepper, five pairs of gloves and two casks of vinegar; the men of Rouen came with wine (IV Æthelred 2.5-2.10, about 1000: from memory (?)). The merchants of Tiel trade with Britain, judge their own suits and drink together at fixed feasts (Alpert of Metz, De diversitate temporum II.20, about 1020). "If a merchant throve, so that he crossed the wide sea three times at his own cost, he was thenceforth worthy of a thegn's right" (the tract Geþyncðo 6, Archbishop Wulfstan, between 1002 and 1023: our rendering after Thorpe, Ancient Laws and Institutes, 1840 (?)).

**Which ending.** The first one reached is the only one: each asks that none of the four is set. A house that fed the fleet has E1 and cannot have E2 or E4. A house whose warning was believed has E3 at York and cannot have E4; if it also has the duke's freight owing it may take E2 in London instead, and the card then says there is a second king at York. A house with the duke's freight owing has E2 in London and E4 anywhere else. Everyone else has E4.

**Roads through.** The king's baker: 1, (2 word), 3, (4 count), 6 stake, E1. The duke's carrier: 1, 4 hire, 7 cross, 9 supply, E2. The Humber pilot: 1, 2 willing, 5 pilot, 8 warn, E3; or, not believed, 10 and E4. The house that served nobody: 1, any of the doors declined, 9 fugitives or 10 carry, E4. The hard roads cross over: the count taken at Rouen (4) or carried out of the Somme (7), sold in London (3), makes the stake at step 6 sure and opens the third choice of E1; the Humber learned under Tostig (2) opens the pilot's berth (5) and so the third choice of E3; the hurt carried home (10) opens the third choice of E4.

**No dead ends.** Every card has a choice that costs only time or pride (see to the mooring; hide with the reeve; decline; drink with the wrights; keep out of it; say no; sell to the camp; keep the hatch shut; water and go; run south; and every ending has at least two choices with no check). A failed chance hurts and goes on: pressed by Tostig, she still learns the Humber; caught counting, a fine and half a count; paid in thin pennies, 25 d for 70; the corn come late, 380 d back of 600 and the fleet goes home as it did; caught on the Somme bar, a fine and the crossing made unpaid, which still leads to E2; laughed at by the ships, the day goes as it went and step 10 follows. Each danger warns first: the skipper of step 1 says to keep clear of the Kentish havens; the clerk at Rouen says a hired ship does not leave before the wind does; the farm wife says to bite the king's pennies; the elder says a king's debt is good while there is a king and that late corn is only corn; the Flemish skipper says the guard boats row the bar all night; the Norwegian steersman says why nobody will believe the carter; the king's man at York says what a toll man makes of mail in a merchant's hold.

**Why a merchant could have won these.** A thegn's right for a merchant who crossed the sea three times at his own cost is the English law's own word (Geþyncðo). A hythe on the Thames by the king's gift is how Queenhithe was shared out in 889 and 898 (S 346, S 1628). A writ of a hand's length making a townsman worthy of the law he had in King Edward's day is exactly what London got from William. A transport was the one thing the duke had too many of after Michaelmas. Guest-right with a Norwegian king, and silver by weight in rings, is how such a king paid a man who had served him; an arm ring from his own arm is the skalds' commonest gift. Ground on the staith at York is the king's to pace out. The peace of Olaf the Quiet (1067 to 1093) for a house that carried his father's men home is the saga's picture of him: a king of markets, not of fleets. A seat among the sworn merchants of Tiel is Alpert's company. A foster son is the oldest way of binding two houses. A year written on one skin is what a house with a priest for a neighbour could buy for a few pennies.

**Costs that last.** `ch_year_1066_cost_kings_service` (E1, with the thegn's right): the house owes the king a ship and her crew when the fleet is called out, and so does the heir. `ch_year_1066_cost_english_grudge` (E2, the writ or the hythe): the hythe trades with the house and will not drink with it. `ch_year_1066_cost_dead_mans_ground` (E2, the hythe): the widow across the lane. `ch_year_1066_cost_two_kings` (E3): the king in the south has been told whose guest the house is. `ch_year_1066_bought_cheap` (step 9) and `ch_year_1066_bought_dead` (step 10): cloth from a burned town, mail off the dead; people saw. `ch_year_1066_tostig` (step 2): she sailed with an outlawed earl, willingly or not.

**What really happened** is E2 and E4: the annals read the same for both, and they differ only in where the house stood.

**History changed, and what follows.**

- `changed_1066_fleet_kept` (step 6) and `changed_1066_william_turned_back` (E1). The Chronicle for 1066 then has no entry for the Nativity of Saint Mary, and for Michaelmas a sea fight in the Channel. What follows: Harold II is king at midwinter 1066 and the game still ends there. In later years the duke, who holds Normandy and has spent his barons' ships and a summer's food, turns back to Maine and Brittany, and may try again; the claim of Swein of Denmark (who did send a fleet in 1069) is the next danger; England's trade stays turned to Flanders, the Rhine and the north instead of to Rouen; there is no Norman castle in London and no Domesday. Later cards may read the flag for the name of the king on the pennies and for whose men hold the tolls.
- `changed_1066_stamford_bridge` (step 8) and `changed_1066_york_norse` (E3). The Chronicle then has the Norwegians ready beyond the bridge and a day's fight that neither side wins. King Harold falls back on Tadcaster and goes south on the news from Pevensey with a mauled army; Hastings is fought and lost as it was; William is hallowed at Westminster and Harald Sigurdsson keeps Yule at York with Tostig as his earl. What follows: England is two kingdoms at the Humber, as it was between Edmund and Cnut for a few weeks in 1016 and under the kings of York before 954; the war between them is 1067's; York's trade turns to Norway and the Irish Sea; the harrying of the north in 1069 does not happen in the same way. Later cards may read the flag for the realm of `eoforwic`.
- Both may be set in one game (a house that fed the fleet and whose warning was believed): E1's lines then say that the king was thrown back from the bridge and holds the south and the sea, and E3's first line still stands; the first ending reached is the house's. Then Harold is king in the south, Harald in the north, and the duke is back on the Somme.

## How long it takes

From the star (24 April) to midwinter: eight months of 1066, about 245 game days. The waits are the year's own and cannot be shortened: May for Tostig, the summer for the three buyers, 8 September for the fleet, Michaelmas for the two landings, mid October for Hastings, and midwinter for the reckoning. A house that takes one road sees five or six cards; one that works two roads at once (the count from Rouen sold in London) sees eight or nine and has to cross the Channel four times between Lammas and Michaelmas.
