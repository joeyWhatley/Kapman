## The story in five sentences

The count at Dorestad reads out the emperor's order for a watch on the river mouths, and the house's small boat is counted with the rest; the watch itself, at Walichrum, is a fire on a dune, four boats (two of them barges) and farmers who are each "the seventh part of a soldier".
In the Danes' harbours every rope walk is sold out for two hundred keels, and a merchant who listens can carry the word to a Frisian mound before the ships come.
In June 810 the fleet is on the islands: three fights are lost, a hundred pounds of silver is weighed out on the strands by the Danes' own weights, and the mound at Emuthon must find its part while the emperor's host eats the river bare and his elephant dies at the Rhine crossing.
Then the Danish king is killed by one of his own men, the emperor's envoys come down the coast asking where the watch was, and in the hardest winter anyone remembers three men want the boat for three different springs.
The house ends as the emperor's shipman among the new hulls at Ganda, as a neighbour of the mound that paid, or as the one man believed on both banks when twelve Franks and twelve Danes swear peace on the Eider.

## How a player meets it

- It starts by itself at Dorestad, the home port: `s01_muster` is drawn on a call there in 808, 809 or the first five months of 810, once the house has made two calls (weight 1.2, once). Every answer starts the chain, including the one that costs nothing ("my boat is too small").
- Only two cards are needed to reach an ending: step 1, and step 10 (the winter card at Dorestad, November 810 to August 811). Everything between is optional and is met by trading where the year's news is: Walichrum, the Danish harbours, the Frisian landings, the Rhine ports while the host is out.
- A house that did the tribute card at Emuthon and then missed the winter card still gets the mound's ending, from September 811.
- The spine's flags `gf_warned` and `gf_paid_share` (thread `godfreds_market`) are set here, so other cards of that thread can know.

## The real history under it

- **The watch and the fleet, 800 to 811.** In March 800 Charles went along the coast of the Gallic sea, set up a fleet and posted guards against the pirates who infested it (Annales regni Francorum, ARF, 800; the revised text calls them Northmen). "On making ships ready along the sea shore" stands in the capitulary for the envoys of 802 (MGH Capitularia I no. 34 c. 13a (?)); ships "which we have ordered built" in the agenda of 808 (no. 51 c. 9-10); "on timber for building ships" in 810 (no. 64 c. 16). Einhard: he had ships built on the rivers of Gaul and Germany that run into the northern sea, and set stations and watches at all the harbours and river mouths where ships could come in (Vita Karoli 17).
- **Who had to serve.** For the Frisians the memorandum of 807 orders that counts, royal vassals and all horsemen come to the host themselves, and that of the poorer men six equip a seventh (MGH Capit. I no. 48 c. 3 (?) for the chapter number). The farmer who is "the seventh part of a soldier" is that law.
- **810.** "A fleet of two hundred ships from Nordmannia" landed in Frisia, ravaged all the islands off the coast, fought the Frisians three times and won, and laid on them a tribute of which a hundred pounds of silver had already been paid when the news reached the emperor at Aachen; King Godfred himself stayed at home (ARF 810; Chronicon Moissiacense 810). A hundred pounds is 24,000 pennies. Einhard adds that Godfred counted Frisia and Saxony as his own provinces and boasted he would soon come to Aachen (Vita Karoli 14). The sun was eclipsed on 7 June 810 (ARF 810), a Friday three weeks after Pentecost.
- **The host.** Charles called out the host, crossed the Rhine at Lippeham and waited there for his troops, and the elephant that the caliph Harun had sent him died there suddenly; he camped where the Aller meets the Weser; there he heard that the fleet had gone home and that Godfred had been killed by one of his own following (ARF 810). A cattle plague killed nearly all the host's oxen that year (ARF 810), and people said men from Benevento were spreading a poison dust (Agobard, De grandine et tonitruis 16).
- **811.** The winter was so hard that the roads between the two peoples were shut, and the peace Godfred's nephew Hemming asked for was sworn only on weapons until spring; then twelve nobles of each people met on the Eider and swore it, each after his own rite and custom. The annal names all twenty-four: Count Wala son of Bernhard led the Franks; Hemming's brothers Hankwin and Angandeo, and Osfrid of Skane, were among the Danes (ARF 811). In the autumn the emperor went to Boulogne to see the fleet he had ordered built the year before, restored the old Roman lighthouse there and had a fire lit on it by night; then he came to the Scheldt at Ghent and looked at the ships built there, and was back at Aachen about mid November (ARF 811). The capitulary given at Boulogne orders that when a fleet is sent the lords go aboard themselves (MGH Capit. I no. 74 c. 11).
- **After.** Hemming died in the winter of 811-812 and 10,940 men are said to have died in the fight for his seat (ARF 812); the peace was sworn again in 813 (ARF 813); Northmen raided Frisia again in 813 (Chron. Moiss. 813). The fleet is not heard of again. In 834 to 837, with the emperor's sons at war with him, Dorestad was plundered four summers running, and after Walcheren fell in 837 Louis held an inquiry at Nijmegen into those he had set to guard the coast (Annales Bertiniani 837). The inquiry card of this chain (s09) is INVENTED on that model.
- **Emuthon in 810.** A trading mound on the Ems with large ewe flocks and wool cloth, small street houses, no church yet (the first timber church dates after 941); the marsh island Bant off the river mouth; the nearest chapel Liudger's at Leer (dossiers/emuthon.md). Count Egbert, who built Esesfeld in March 810 and swore on the Eider in 811, is the count tied to this district (same dossier).
- **Foul gain.** Buying a needy man's goods far under their worth, to sell dear, is condemned in the capitulary of Nijmegen of 806 (MGH Capit. I no. 46 c. 15-17 (?) for the chapters).
- **Not known, and invented here.** How the hundred pounds was shared out (a part for each mound, four pounds for Emuthon); what a watch post looked like; what the fleet bought and where it gathered; any warning; any hearing at Dorestad; the number of ships at Ghent (dossiers/ganda.md: "no dig has found it"); the bands of red and pale stone on the tower at Boulogne are from much later drawings of the Tour d'Ordre (?).

**What really happened, and what the player changes.**

- What really happened is the plain road of each ending: the fleet was built, lords were ordered aboard, and it is not heard of again; the coast paid, mended and was raided again in 813; the oath on the Eider spoke of kings and borders.
- `changed_frisia_warned_810` (card 4): a Frisian landing is warned in time. The three fights are still lost and the hundred pounds still asked, but that mound's cattle are on the high ground and its best silver is hidden. What would follow: little, beyond a mound that is less poor in the plague year, and a people who know that a merchant's word came and a count's did not. It is what makes the next two changes believable.
- `changed_coast_watch_frisian` (ending 1, the hard road): the watch is kept by sworn Frisian keels, forty days a summer by turns, paid in freedom from the toll at Dorestad. Nothing like it is recorded; the order of 811 put lords aboard instead. What would follow: a watch whose men live on the water and are paid by something they want does not stop when the emperor's sons go to war in 833; the fleets of 834 to 837 find boats at the river mouths and word going up the river ahead of them. The chain `dorestad_834` may read this flag. The cost is real and lasts: `ch_coast_watch_810_watch_duty`, forty days of the house's own summer every year.
- `changed_frisian_beacons_811` (ending 2, the hard road): the mounds between the Ems and the Lauwers swear to one another, without a count, to keep a fire and a watcher each and to stand together. What would follow: sworn Frisian districts that answer for their own defence are heard of only from the 11th and 12th centuries (the "free Frisians", the meetings at the Upstalsboom (?)); here the habit begins three hundred years early, and the counts will not like it when they notice.
- `changed_eider_peace_merchants` (ending 3, the hard road): the oath of 811 says that traders of both peoples may cross the border with their goods and buy and sell in peace. That clause was really agreed between the Danes and the East Franks only in 873 (Annales Fuldenses 873). What would follow: sixty years in which a Frisian at Sliesthorp and a Dane at Dorestad stand under a sworn peace, not under a host's goodwill; when the kings are at war the merchants have something to point to. The chain `lost_brother` adds its own clause to the same oath (`changed_eider_peace_captives`); the two do not clash.

**Why a merchant could have won these.** A hull from a royal yard: the ships were built by order on every river, and a master wright with a keel too many is the kind of thing no annal records. Toll freedom at Dorestad by the emperor's writing: such charters exist for abbeys' boats (Saint-Germain, 779), and service is how a layman would earn one. The count's shipman and the emperor's man: a known pilot, called first. A plot or a sworn man on a Frisian mound: given by the meeting, which is how land on a mound was held. A fostering: the ordinary way two houses tied themselves together. Freedom of the Danish king's gate and a guest's right: Godfred himself moved merchants to Sliesthorp for their tolls, and his heirs wanted the market full. A Danish partner: the felag of the North, hand in hand before a crew.

## How long it takes

From a year to four years of game time. The earliest start is 808; the fleet comes in June 810; the winter card is November 810 to August 811; the endings fall in 811 (March for the Eider, May for the mound, September to November for Ganda) and stay open through 812. A house that starts in May 810 can still be done in eighteen months. The waits are the real ones: the summer of the fleet, the autumn of the inquiry, the winter that shut the roads, the spring of the oath, the autumn of the ships.
