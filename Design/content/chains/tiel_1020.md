## The story in five sentences

In the summer of 1018 the emperor's army, sent at the asking of the merchants of Tiel, is cut down in the ditches at Vlaardingen, Count Dirk keeps his toll at the river mouth, and the Tiel men stop looking to any court but their own bench.
The player sees one of them swear a debt away with a closed fist and six friends, is taken down the Waal to the morning cask, and learns the price of a seat: two brothers to swear for him, a pound of pennies in the common chest, and a year with no brother's cause against him.
The year tests him from four sides: the bench's man in London needs pepper for the king's reeve, the count's steward buys ears, the monk Alpert asks questions with a wax tablet on his knee, and on the oath day a Cologne wine seller is owed for nine casks that the player saw rolled ashore.
In winter the chest is opened, and the player may drink his pound like everyone, or stand up and say what nobody says: buy from the emperor the charter you have always sworn you have.
It ends on the bench with the cup (and, for the honest, a key of the chest), or before the church door with a real seal that history never had, or in the bishop's hall as the man behind a monk's "it is said", or under the count's fort in the marsh.

## The real history under it

**Alpert's chapter.** Alpert, called of Metz, a monk, wrote *De diversitate temporum* on the years 990 to 1021 and dedicated it to Bishop Burchard of Worms; he also wrote the miracles of Saint Walburg at Tiel; he died in or after 1024 (Wikipedia en "Alpert of Metz" and nl "Alpertus van Metz", read 6 Oct 2026). Where he lived is not stated there: the diocese of Utrecht, perhaps the abbey of Hohorst (?). His book II, chapter 20, says of the merchants of Tiel, in our rendering and from memory (?): they are hard men, used to almost no discipline, who judge not by law but by their own will, and say the emperor handed this down and confirmed it by charter; if one of them has borrowed and is asked to repay, he denies it and swears at once; a thing that can be hidden in one closed hand is sworn away with the other; they give themselves to drinking from early morning, and the man who tells foul stories loudest to make the common people laugh is most praised; they put money together, share it out for gain, and from it pay for feasts at set times of the year, and on the high feasts get drunk as if it were a solemn duty (`chronicle_793.json` 1020-tiel-merchants; `lore/law_tolls.md`; `realms/frisia_lotharingia.json`, year 1020). The cards quote none of it; the monk's read-back in step 5 gives the sense of one sentence. The word "guild" is never used (BRIEF rule 2).

**The charter.** Alpert writes "they say". No charter of an emperor for the merchants of Tiel is known. That is the hinge of the chain: ending 2 makes it exist.

**Vlaardingen, 1018.** Count Dirk III built a fort in the marsh at Vlaardingen and took toll of all who went down to the sea. The Tiel men complained to Henry II that they could no longer go to Britain nor the Britons come to them, and that the emperor's own tolls would fail. At the Easter court at Nijmegen (6 April 1018) the emperor ordered Duke Godfrey and Bishop Adalbold of Utrecht to throw the count out; on 29 July the army landed among the ditches, panicked at a shout and was cut down at the boats; the duke was taken and the bishop got away by boat. The toll stood (Alpert II.20-21; Thietmar VIII (?); `chronicle_793.json` 1018-07-29-vlaardingen; `places/noviomagus.json`, story `noviomagus.tiel_complaint`). The Dutch Wikipedia page on Tiel adds that Tiel lost the emperor's protection after this (read 6 Oct 2026). The chain begins the month after.

**London.** The London toll rules known as IV Aethelred (about 1000; Liebermann, *Gesetze der Angelsachsen* I) say that the men of the emperor who come in their ships are worthy of good laws as the Londoners are, and that at Christmas and at Easter they give two grey cloths and one brown, ten pounds of pepper, five pairs of gloves and two kegs of vinegar (from memory, not opened tonight (?); also `realms/frisia_lotharingia.md`, the 1000s). Cnut is king from 1016. The store below the bridge and its keeper are ours.

**The bishop.** Adalbold II was bishop of Utrecht from 1010 to 27 November 1026. In 1026 King Conrad II gave the bishop the count's rights in Teisterbant, the land of Tiel (`realms/frisia_lotharingia.json`): from then the man who cares about the Tiel oaths also holds their court. Burchard of Worms, to whom Alpert sent his book, compiled the *Decretum*, which has a book on perjury (book XII (?)). That the bishop questioned river merchants about Tiel is ours (?). His successor Bernold (1027 to 1054) is from memory (?).

**The count.** Dirk III, count in West Frisia from 993, died 27 May 1039; he kept fort and toll, and the emperor let it be. A round fort about 27 m across lay under the church hill (`later_ports/lowlands_saxony.md`). That he drained the marsh and that he bought word of the bench are ours (?).

**Magdeburg, 975.** Otto II freed the merchants of Magdeburg of toll everywhere except at Mainz, Cologne, Tiel and Bardowick (DD O II 112, `lore/law_tolls.md`): the chaplain in step 9 uses it to show what an emperor will sign.

**Invented, and said so.** The terms of a seat (two oath-helpers, a pound, a clean year); seven hands to the oath (the common number of oath-helpers, not Alpert's); the chest with three keys; that the chest buries the drowned and feeds widows (after the agreements about alms, fire and wreck that the capitulary of Herstal, 779, c. 16, allows without an oath (?)); the Cologne suit; the vote on the year's gain; the chaplain and the price of a charter; the fixed yearly sum to the count. The story told on the bench is Exeter riddle 25, the onion (`texts_793.json` exeter_25_onion), in a paraphrase of our own. People: Wigbold (Wig- + -bold), Saewine (Sae- + -wine), Everwin (Ever- + -win), Meinward (Mein- + -ward), Wezelo (a short Rhenish name of the 11th century (?)), Dodo (a short name, dead before he is met), and the unnamed ale-wife, chaplain, clerk and priest of Saint Walburg's.

**What really happened** is ending 1 (and its guest's variant): Alpert's page stands as he wrote it, the charter stays a thing people swear to, and in 1026 the bishop becomes count over Tiel. Ending 3 is the same history with the player as the unnamed mouth behind the chapter; what the bishop then does at Tiel is ours. Ending 4's plain options are also the true years: the toll stays.

**History changed, and what follows.**

- `changed_tiel_charter_1020` (ending 2). Tiel has, in the 1020s, the first written merchants' law of the Low Countries, a hundred years before anything of the kind is known there (?). If it is the fair writing (`_charter_fair`), strangers' claims go before the king's man, Cologne sends its wine down more gladly, and when the bishop becomes count in 1026 his court takes strangers' cases and leaves the bench its own: Alpert's sentence "they say the emperor gave them a charter" becomes "the emperor gave them a charter", and his complaint loses its sting. If it is the wide writing, the bishop has a sealed sentence to fight, and the 1030s at Tiel are a lawsuit. Either way a town with a charter is harder to pass over: when later kings move tolls (Tiel's goes to Kaiserswerth in 1174, after the game), the Tiel men have something to hold up. Later cards at Tiel should show the seal on its nail, and the long card `long.tiel.sworn_merchants` should not say "nobody has seen it".
- `changed_tiel_toll_peace` (ending 4, hard road). The toll war of 1018 ends in a bargain and not in a habit: the bench pays the count a fixed sum at Martinmas and its keels are not searched. Dirk's house is then the Tiel men's partner at the river mouth rather than their grievance, and Henry III's campaign against Dirk IV in 1047 has no merchants' complaint behind it (?). The house has a seat at Tiel and the count's goodwill, which nobody else has together.
