## The story in five sentences

A fowler at Gipeswic says a gale has broken one of the old kings' mounds on the hoe above the Deben, beside the gallows the king's reeve keeps there, and that something yellow shows in the sand.
The boat lands, and the four men of the crew want four things: the Frisian steersman wants the gold made clean by a bishop, the Northman wants the hill shut and its dead left alone, the Saxon knows whose it is because his own father lies under such a hill, and the English lookout remembers a saint who lived in a robbed mound and a song about a dragon and one stolen cup.
A fever aboard, a goldsmith at Dorestad who knows old work, and an old woman of the old kings' kin, who says the long hill at the river end is Rædwald of the two altars, if it is anybody.
Then the reeve hangs a potter's lad over the broken mound and asks after boats, and on a second night landing the great ship mound lies whole beside the spoiled one.
The master has the last voice: back into the earth, to the bishop, to the king's reeve, or to the crucible.

## How a player meets it

- It starts by itself at **Gipeswic** from 793 to 811: card `s01_fowlers_word` is drawn on a call there (weight 0.8, once). Gipeswic is the East Angles' emporium, a plain crossing from Dorestad, so a trading house finds it early.
- **A player who says no is still in the story.** The men heard the fowler, and the boat goes up the Deben on the next sailing whatever was answered. At the mound itself he may touch nothing. Every later card has words for the man who took nothing.
- Steps 2 and 8 are sea cards drawn with a high rate just after she sails from Gipeswic (the Deben is the next river north). Steps 3, 4 and 9 are crew cards at sea. Steps 5, 6 and 7 are drawn with a high weight at their ports. The four endings are scheduled.
- For other chains: `changed_sutton_hoo_opened`, `ch_barrow_mound_breaker`, `ch_barrow_kin_enmity`, `ch_barrow_ravn_gone`, and the four `ch_barrow_end_*` flags.

## The real history under it

**The place.** Sutton Hoo: about twenty mounds on a spur over the tidal Deben, 11 km from the sea ("hoo": a hill shaped like a heel spur). The game calls it "the hoe". (Wikipedia, "Sutton Hoo", read 6 Oct 2026.)

**The gallows.** A gallows stood on Mound 5, and two groups of executed people were buried there, one round that mound and one at the cemetery's edge, from the 8th and 9th centuries onwards (the same page; M. Carver's excavations). Christian kings made the heathen kings' burial place the hanging place. Cards 1, 7 and 8 rest on this. The reeve's line "it saves a cart" is ours.

**The robbing.** Most of the mounds were dug into, at dates nobody knows. Mound 2's robbers left a blue glass cup in pieces, gilt-bronze discs, a silver buckle, the tip of a sword blade and silver-gilt mounts of drinking horns. Mound 17 (a young man and his horse) had a sword, a belt with garnet work and a pouch with garnets. Mounds 5 to 7 were cremations in bronze bowls (from memory (?)). The game's broken mound is a nameless one with goods of this kind: INVENTED, and consistent with what happened.

**The great mound.** Mound 1 held a ship 27 m long with a chamber amidships: the helm, a sword with gold and garnet at the hilt, the great gold buckle, the shoulder clasps, a purse with thirty-seven small gold coins, a great silver dish with the stamps of the emperor Anastasius (491-518), silver bowls and spoons, drinking horns, a lyre, a whetstone with faces. In the 16th century robbers sank a pit into it that narrowly missed the burial. It lay whole until 1939. Card 8's failure leaves exactly such a pit.

**Who lies there.** Rædwald, king of the East Angles (died about 624), has been thought the likeliest since 1940; it is still disputed. Leofwaru says so: "Others say it is his son. The hill does not say." Bede, Historia ecclesiastica II.15: Rædwald was baptised in Kent, and at home kept in one temple an altar for Christ and a small one for offerings to devils; King Aldwulf saw that temple as a boy. It is retold in cards 6 and 11 and never quoted. It is the chain's theme in one king: the Christian and the heathen did not agree inside him either.

**The kings.** Wuffa, Tytila, Rædwald, Eorpwald, Anna, Ælfwald (died 749): Bede and the Anglian collection of royal pedigrees. Æthelberht, king of the East Angles, struck a penny with the she-wolf and twins (`dossiers/gipeswic.md`) and was beheaded at Offa's order in 794 (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle s.a. 792). Offa died in July 796. Eadwald is known only from about twelve coins, king from about 796; Coenwulf of Mercia had the kingdom again between 798 and about 805 (Wikipedia, "Eadwald of East Anglia", read 6 Oct 2026). The reeve's and the old woman's lines change with the year: before 794, 794 to 796, 797 to 798 (?), from 799.

**The crew's four reasons.**
- Aldger (the church makes it clean): Pope Gregory's letter to Mellitus, 601, in Bede I.30: do not pull down the temples of the English; cast out the idols, sprinkle holy water, set up altars. The bishop retells it in card 11.
- Ravn (the dead keep what they were given): ship burial under a mound was the north's own custom. Storhaug on Karmøy, by Ögvaldsnes, is a ship mound of 779 (from memory (?)), so a Northman of 793 could have seen one heaped. That the mound's dweller walks after its robber is the belief of the sagas, written four centuries later, and is set back here (?). The mounds of Oseberg and Gokstad were themselves broken into in the 10th century (?).
- Brunger (the Saxon): Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae c. 22 (about 782 to 785): the bodies of Christian Saxons are to be carried to the church's cemeteries and not to the mounds of the pagans; c. 7 forbids burning the dead on pain of death (MGH Capitularia I no. 26).
- Beornwine (the oblate of Gyrwe): Felix, Life of Guthlac, written 730 to 740 for King Ælfwald of the East Angles: Guthlac's mound at Crowland was "a mound built of clods of earth which greedy comers to the waste had dug open, in the hope of finding treasure there" (as given on Wikipedia, "Guthlac of Crowland"; the cards retell it and do not quote it). Guthlac came there in 699 and died in 714. The dragon, the hoard of three hundred winters and the one stolen cup are Beowulf, fitt XXXII, which card 3 gives as a text (`texts_793.json` `beowulf_32`).

**The fever.** The shaking sickness of marsh country that comes every third day: the "spring sickness" of the Old English leechbooks (?). The cure of warm ale and wormwood is ours (?). It passes on the third day whatever the master does, and the men credit what was done.

**The gold.** Garnet cut in steps and set in cells over stamped gold foil is the work of the 6th and 7th centuries, and stops when the garnets stop coming, about the middle of the 7th (the usual account (?)). One of gold for twelve of silver is the Carolingian reckoning; the Edict of Pîtres (864) fixes it (the chapter from memory (?)). The mount, about 18 g, is 160 d.

**Whose is found gold.** That treasure found in the earth is the king's is first written in England in the 12th century (Leges Henrici Primi 10.1 (?)). A finder's third is INVENTED. The king's leaf is modelled on King Æthelbald's remissions of the toll on a ship at London to Rochester, Minster-in-Thanet and the bishop of London in the 730s and 740s (Sawyer 86, 87, 88, 91, 98, 1788; numbers from memory (?)): Latin on parchment with crosses, and no seal, since sealed writs come later.

**The bishop.** Dommoc has been the East Angles' see since Felix, about 630. In 793 the bishop is Ælfhun, who died in 798 (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle 798); then Tidfrith (?). The cards do not name him. Botolph began his minster at Icanho (Iken on the Alde) in 654 (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle 654). A cloth laid on a saint's tomb is the commonest relic of the age.

**Invented, and said so.** Sæwine the fowler (Sæ- + -wine), Leofwaru of the old kin (Leof- + -waru), Dudda the potter's lad (a common short name; Gipeswic is the town of the Ipswich-ware potters), Folcmar the goldsmith of Dorestad (Folc- + -mar). The reeve and the bishop are unnamed. The gale, the thorn tree and the broken mound.

**History changed.** `changed_sutton_hoo_opened` is set in card 8 when the great mound is opened, whichever of the three roads the goods then take. What would follow in later years, for cards that read the flag:
- In 1939 the men who open the long mound find the lines of a ship's rivets in the sand and an empty chamber with an old pit down the middle of it. There is no helm, no buckle, no purse; the English seventh century keeps the name of a dark age a good while longer, and nobody argues about Rædwald.
- By the bishop's road the helm lies in the relic chest at Dommoc. The Danes take the East Angles' kingdom in 869 and 870, and the sea takes Dommoc after that: a later card may have a Dane wear it, or an heir of the house see it once more.
- By the reeve's road a king's whole gear is shared out in a Mercian hall, and the East Angles know a Frisian carried it there. When they have a king of their own again (Eadwald about 796, Æthelstan in the 820s) the house's name is remembered at Gipeswic.
- By the crucible the Greek silver is pennies within the year, and the garnets turn up one by one in brooches along the Rhine.
- Nothing recorded is contradicted except the mound itself.

The other small change: with the bishop's cross on the hoe (`unlock_work_gipeswic_hoe_cross`) the gallows leaves the mounds. In fact it stayed into the 11th century.

The honest road pays least in silver and most in things that last: it is the only ending with two grand rewards and no cost. The greedy road pays most in silver and takes a man out of the boat.

Why a merchant of the time could have won each:
- **A toft at Gipeswic.** The town's plots ran down to the Orwell, and its wells were lined with old wine casks from the Rhine (`dossiers/gipeswic.md`). Frisians lived in English ports (the Frisians at York in the Life of Liudger). A widow of standing could give land before witnesses.
- **The Deben bar.** A shingle bar that moves every winter; a fowler's knowledge of it is a pilot's.
- **A ring of the old kin.** Gold rings given for service are the commonest gift of the age; this one "came out of no hill".
- **Saint Felix's man.** Churches had ships that went free of the king's toll, by charter; the bishop's three lines put the boat among them, as Saint Martin's does at Dorestad in other chains.
- **A cloth from Saint Botolph's tomb.** A contact relic, which a bishop could give to a layman without robbing an altar.
- **A cross on the hoe.** Standing crosses were set up at burial and meeting places across England in the 8th century.
- **The toll at Gipeswic, and the king's merchant.** Æthelbald's London charters remit the toll on one named ship; Charlemagne's letter to Offa of 796 promises protection to merchants who come lawfully. A layman and a foreigner getting it is the game's reward for a king's ransom in gold.
- **The pommel, the helm.** Kept, not won. An heir of the house may be asked one day where the helm came from.
- **A keel's place.** Twenty-five hundred pennies is most of a sea-going trader, and a goldsmith's brother-in-law on the slip is how such things were arranged.

## How long it takes

At the least about 190 game days: the night after the landing, 6 days to the fever, the passage to Dorestad, 45 days before the old woman speaks, 60 more before the reeve asks (and only from March to October), the second landing, and 20 to 30 days to the ending. A house that starts it in a summer finishes it the next spring or summer, with a winter between, beside ordinary trading.
