## The story in five sentences

The pilot of the Maas mouth, old Gerlef of Witla, is going blind, and he will give the bars from the Maas to the Vlie to one man: not for silver, but for a spring, a summer and an autumn of that man's boat, for silence about his eyes, and for an oath to put all of it into his grandson when the boy can carry the pole.
The merchant sounds the bar with him after the winter floods, runs north along the dunes while the old man says the shore as a verse he can no longer see, and finds at the Vlie that the last line of the verse has been false for years: the channel moved, and nobody sent word.
That is the secret: not where the channel is, but how a man asks the water where it is, with a pole, a lead with tallow in its foot, the birds, the colour of the stream and the place of the moon.
Three others want it: Thiadbod, who has rented the pilot by the year and thinks he bought what was in his head; the count, who has a list of river mouths with an empty space beside the Maas; and the schoolmaster of Saint Martin's, who has Bede's page on the tides open and a boy with a pen.
The marks end in the grandson's hands, on a leaf at the back of a book, or with a master up the river, and the pilot's house remembers which.

## How a player meets it

- It starts by itself at Witla, the strand market at the Maas mouth: `s01_ask_the_water` is drawn on a call there between March and October, 793 to 806, once the house has called there before (weight 3, once). Every answer starts the chain, including offering silver, which he refuses.
- The Witla places file points at it already: the site `witla.pilot_shed`, the pilot's line "Ask me about the water", and the story "a merchant who asks Gerlef about the water, and not about his price, can be taught what Thiadbod only rents".
- The straight road is seven cards (s01, s02, s03, s04, s06, s08, s11) and an ending. Four cards are optional and colour the endings: Thiadbod's offer (s05), the verse at sea (s07), the schoolmaster (s09), the count (s10).
- A house that swore or gave its seasons (s03) and then stayed away is still asked the question from 810, when the old man is blind and tells from the doorway. A house that only began (s01) is met by the grandson from 813 and can still finish, on the plain road of the first ending.

## The real history under it

- **The pilot.** Old English *ladmann* (a guide), Old Norse *leidsogumadr* (a way-teller). Where banks shift each winter a stranger hires one (`lore/sea_lore.md` 2). No pilot of the Maas mouth or the Vlie is recorded for these centuries; Gerlef, his daughter Wilsuit and his grandson Gerbrand are the invented people of the Witla places file and the spine.
- **The craft.** The pole and the lead: a hollow in the foot of the lead is filled with tallow and brings up the bottom; Old English has *sundgyrd* (sounding rod) and *sundline* in glossaries of the 10th and 11th centuries (?); the Bayeux Tapestry shows men sounding from the bow (about 1075) (?). Acts 27:28: Paul's shipmen "sounded, and found it twenty fathoms", then fifteen, and "wished for the day": every priest knew it. The rest (birds on a shoal, brown smooth water over the deep, a wrinkle over a bank) is ordinary seamanship that no early text lists (`sea_lore` 3).
- **The moon and the tide.** Bede, De temporum ratione 29 (written 725): the sea follows the moon; the flood is later each day by four fifths of an hour, as the moon is; each shore has its own hour, known to those who live there (our words; chapter number and fraction from memory (?)). A rule like "full moon in the south-east, high water at Witla" is how later pilots said it; such rules are first written in the North in the 14th and 15th centuries, and the bearing here is invented (`sea_lore` 5).
- **Nothing was written.** The oldest sailing account of the North is Ohthere's and Wulfstan's, set down at King Alfred's court about 890. The oldest directions for these very bars are in the Low German Seebuch of about 1470, full of depths and of what the lead brings up (`sea_lore` 3) (?). Before that the craft went by mouth, which is the ground this chain stands on.
- **The Maas mouth and Witla.** Witla is "the emporium near the mouth of the river Maas" that the Northmen burned in 836 (Annales Fuldenses 836): one sentence is the whole record of it. That toll was taken there from ships on the Maas and the Waal is Dijkstra's reading, not a record (Dijkstra 2011, p. 324). The estuary is the Romans' Helinium; there were no dykes (`dossiers/witla.md` 1).
- **The Rhine mouth.** The old Rhine reached the sea at Katwijk and was silting in these centuries; it was shut in the 12th (?). A Roman fort there, later called the Brittenburg, was seen in the surf at very low tides from 1520; what stood of it in 800 is not known (?). "The work of giants" is what the English poets called Roman stone.
- **The Vlie and the Almere.** The Fli is the boundary of the Frisians' law of about 802: its western part runs from the Sincfal to the Fli (Lex Frisionum). The Vlie was then "a narrow water between Friesland and Wieringen"; Texel and Wieringen were not yet islands; behind it lay the Almere, fresh to weakly brackish (`dossiers/medemelacha.md` 1). Boniface crossed "the water which in their tongue is called Aelmere" on his way to his death in 754 (Willibald, Vita Bonifatii 8).
- **Medemelacha and Saint Martin.** The king took toll at Medemelacha from every trader sailing past, and a tenth of his income there (the *regalis decima*) was Saint Martin's of Utrecht: it stands in the church's goods register of about 900 and is most likely Pippin's grant of 753 (`dossiers/medemelacha.md` 1 and 5) (?). "No bar, pilot or sea-mark is recorded" there: the moved channel and the saint's boat on the mud are invented.
- **The coast watch.** In March 800 Charles left Aachen, went along the shore of the Gallic sea, set up a fleet and posted guards against the pirates (Annales regni Francorum 800); stations and watches at all harbours and river mouths (Einhard, Vita Karoli 17). The Frisian's duty to follow the king "no further than the Weser to the east and the Fli to the west, out with the ebb and up with the flood" is from the Old Frisian laws, written from the 13th century and older in matter (`sea_lore` 5) (?).
- **Runes on yew.** Frisian rune inscriptions on yew sticks of about this time are real (Westeremden, Britsum) (?). Cutting sea marks on one is invented.
- **Invented, and said so.** Every person; the oath at the cross; the verse of the marks; that Gerlef had the Vlie from his wife's father; the count's list of river mouths; the leaf at the back of the book of times; Thiadbod's price.

Thiadbod's 150 d (card 5) or the count's 100 d (card 10) opens the third. Taking the boy north (card 6) costs his mother's good opinion and gives him a line at sea.

**What really happened, and what the player changes.**

- What really happened is the first ending: the craft went by mouth to one heir, nothing was written, and the places file's Gerbrand is the pilot of Witla after 812. The kept ending and Thiadbod's road change who holds the secret and change no history.
- `changed_bars_written` (ending 2, every road): sailing directions for a northern shore are written about 800, ninety years before Ohthere's account and more than six centuries before the Seebuch. What would follow: Saint Martin's boats, and in time any steersman who can find a priest to read to him, cross two bars without a hired man; other churches with boats ask for a copy; and a leaf can be carried off. In 834 a fleet came "by Utrecht to Dorestad" (Annales Bertiniani 834): whoever takes Saint Martin's books then has the way in. The chain `dorestad_834` may read this flag.
- `changed_bars_sounded_yearly` (ending 2, the hard road): the leaf holds a method and a rule that it be amended every Easter. What would follow: a dated record, year under year, of how two river mouths move. In a generation the monks can see the Rhine mouth closing and the Vlie widening before any skipper says so; after the flood of 26 December 838 (Annales Bertiniani 839) there is a book that says what the shore was the Easter before. Nothing like it exists before the pilot books of the 16th century.
- `changed_maas_kings_pilots` (ending 3, the count's road): the king's watch has sworn pilots at a river mouth, named on a list and paid in freedom from toll. A pilot named by the crown is not heard of for centuries. What would follow: the watch of 800 to 811 can cross its own bar; but a named man can be sent for, seized or bought, and in 836, when the fleet comes for Witla, there is a list that says who knows the way.

**Why a merchant could have won these.** The knowledge of two bars: a pilot's craft passes to a pupil, and the spine's Gerlef wants one before his eyes go. Kin in the craft: fostering and sworn kinship were the ordinary way two houses tied themselves together. The lead: a tool, handed on. A leaf of sailing directions: a monastery with boats and a school could have made one any year it thought of it; none did. Saint Martin's man: the church's men stood under its protection and, on Dorestad's banks, free of the king's dues (the privilege of 815; before that by the bishop's word (?)). A trusted woman keeping your goods at Witla: an ale-wife with lodgings and a memory for what each man owes; the pattern is Frideburg of Birka. Toll freedom at a king's post and the count's shipman: such writings exist for abbeys' boats and for Louis's merchants (828); service is how a layman would earn one. A plot on Thiadbod's dam: the dams of Dorestad are parcels held by houses, and a rich man pays in ground when he would rather not pay in coin.

## How long it takes

Two years of game time at the quickest, and as many as the house likes after that. The waits are the sailing year's: first met between Easter and Martinmas; the price named after Michaelmas, when the wine ships stop; the bar sounded after the winter floods; the run north between Whitsun and Lammas; the Vlie in the same summer; the question asked the winter after; the pole handed on at the next spring sounding (the leaf sixty days after the question, the sale thirty). A house that starts in April 794 can be done in the spring of 796. The chain cannot start after 806; the question must be answered before 813, when the old man is dead; the endings stay open until 820.
