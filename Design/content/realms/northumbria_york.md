# Northumbria, Danish York and the northern earldoms, 780-1070: a merchant's view

The data is in `northumbria_york.json` (rulers, wars, raids, laws, coins, church), with the sources named entry by entry. Short names here: **ASC** Anglo-Saxon Chronicle; **HR** Historia regum; **LDE** Symeon's history of the church of Durham; **HSC** Historia de Sancto Cuthberto; **DOD** De obsessione Dunelmi; **RW** Roger of Wendover; **JW** John of Worcester; **DB** Domesday Book. `(?)` marks a doubt or a fact not re-checked. Ports: `eoforwic` (York), `lindisfarena`, `bebbanburg`, `gyrwe` (Jarrow, the Tyne), `streonaeshalch` (Whitby).

**Three realms in one.** To 867 an English kingdom that kills its own kings, with York as its city and its trading strand. From 867 to 954 a Danish kingdom at York, later ruled by the Dublin kings' kin, with English lords holding Bamburgh and Saint Cuthbert's people holding the land between Tyne and Wear. From 954 to 1066 an earldom under the king of the English: Danish in speech and law, and York the second town of England. The way in is always the same: the Humber, then the tide up the Ouse.

## Decade by decade

- **780s.** York has Alcuin's school, a new church with thirty altars (780) and a strand where the Foss meets the Ouse. Frisians had a colony here until one of them killed a nobleman's son, about 773 (Altfrid, *Vita Liudgeri* I.11). The pope's legates lay down equal weights and no usury (786). King Ælfwald is murdered (788).
- **790s.** The bad decade. Famine and signs in the sky (793), Lindisfarne (8 June 793), Jarrow (794), three kings in six weeks (796), a rebel battle (798), a storm that wrecks many ships (799). In 796 King Charles is angry with the whole people. Trade through an English host. Bring wine, quern-stones, grain in the famine; take hides, cloth, lead (?).
- **800s.** Eardwulf fights Mercia (801), is thrown out (806) and is brought home by the emperor's and the pope's men (808). The coast is quiet again (the raid of 800 is doubtful). Cattle plague (810).
- **810s to 830s.** Eanred's thirty quiet years: no raid, no king killed, a bow to Wessex at Dore (829) that costs nothing. The trouble is the money. The York styca sinks from poor silver to brass by the 830s and nobody over the sea will take it: bring your own silver and take goods away, not coin.
- **840s.** Heathen men come back and kill King Rædwulf (844). Brass by the bucket.
- **850s.** Osberht king; the mint stops about 855. The last calm.
- **860s.** Two kings at war with each other. The great army takes York on All Saints' Day 866 and kills both kings inside the walls on 21 March 867. The strand by the Foss is given up (?). Stay away until the army goes south (869).
- **870s.** Revolt and return (872-873, the army wintering at Torksey on the Trent). Halfdan on the Tyne (875): the monasteries end and Saint Cuthbert's body takes the road. In 876 the army shares out the land, "so that they became afterwards their harrowers and plowers" (ASC 876, Ingram 1823). York is a town of farmers' lords. Silver goes by weight.
- **880s.** No king, then Guthred (883): a baptised Dane the church chose, peace sworn between Dane and English, Saint Cuthbert settled at Chester-le-Street with a right of sanctuary. A good decade to come back.
- **890s.** York's ships raid Wessex (893-896), so a Humber ship is suspect in the south. About 895 the mint opens again with good silver pennies.
- **900s.** Pennies of Siefred and Cnut that read "he has done wonders". Æthelwold's adventure (899-902). The town is laid out anew in long plots along Coppergate (from about 900; Hall 1984). King Edward's five weeks of harrying (909).
- **910s.** Tettenhall kills York's kings (910); the pennies name only Saint Peter. Ragnall's fleet is on the Tyne (914, 918) and in York (919). The road over the hills to the Irish Sea and Dublin is now one family's road.
- **920s.** Ragnall and Sihtric bow to the southern king (920, 926). Æthelstan takes York (927) and pulls down the Danes' stronghold. The king's penny and the king's market law come north.
- **930s.** The quiet years of Danish York. The king's fleet passes up the coast (934). Brunanburh (937) is fought far to the west. Æthelstan dies in October 939 and Olaf of Dublin is in York by the year's end.
- **940s.** Kings by the season: Olaf, Olaf, Ragnall, Edmund (944), Eadred, Eric (947), Eadred, Olaf (949). York men harry Lindisfarne (941). Ripon is burned (948). Ask who is king before you come up the Ouse.
- **950s.** Eric again (952), driven out and killed on Stainmore (954). No more kings: an earl, Oswulf of Bamburgh. The archbishop is in the king's prison (952-954).
- **960s and 970s.** Earl Oslac (966-975). King Edgar lets the Danes keep their own law but every sale needs sworn witnesses. New money in 973, and a re-coining every few years after. Peace; York grows fast.
- **980s.** Earl Thored. The raids begin again in the south, not here.
- **990s.** Bamburgh is sacked and both Humber shores harried (993). Saint Cuthbert moves to Durham (995). About 1000 a monk writes that York is full of merchants, Danes above all (Byrhtferth).
- **1000s.** Wulfstan archbishop (1002): sermons and laws. Earl Ælfhelm murdered and the Scots cut down at Durham (1006). Uhtred earl of everything.
- **1010s.** Sweyn's fleet in the Humber (1013). Uhtred murdered, Cnut king, Eric of Hlathir earl (1016). Carham lost to the Scots (1018). Then Cnut's peace.
- **1020s and 1030s.** Cnut's law: witnesses for anything over four pence, no Christians sold out of the land, no Sunday market at York. The only war is the feud between Uhtred's house and Thurbrand's. Siward is earl by 1033.
- **1040s.** Duncan beaten at Durham (1040). Siward kills Earl Eadwulf and holds everything from Humber to Tweed (1041). Strong, safe and Danish.
- **1050s.** Siward beats Macbeth (1054) and dies (1055). Tostig: hard on robbers, heavy with tax.
- **1060s.** Malcolm raids (1061). Tostig's murders (1064) and the rising (3 October 1065). In 1066 Tostig is in the Humber in May and the Norwegian fleet in September: Fulford (20 September), Stamford Bridge (25 September). After the game's end: castles (1068), the city burned (September 1069), the land wasted (winter 1069-1070).

## Who must be paid

- **To 867:** the king's reeve at the strand (no York toll charter survives (?)), and the archbishop, who has his own moneyers and his own people. A stranger trades through a host.
- **867 to 954:** whoever is king at York this season, and the holds along the Ouse. From about 895 the mint changes your silver, and the archbishop has a hand in it. Between Tyne and Wear, Saint Cuthbert's bishop (from 883). North of that, the lord of Bamburgh.
- **954 to 1066:** the king's reeve and the earl's in six shires of the city, the archbishop in his own (DB). The moneyer, at every re-coining (from 973). The army-tax, 1012 to 1051. Under Tostig, more.

## Who protects

- **The saints.** Saint Peter's minster (though a king broke its peace in 791). Saint Cuthbert: thirty-seven days' peace at his body, at Chester-le-Street from 883 and Durham from 995 (HSC 13).
- **The king's hand.** By 1066 his peace given by hand or seal has ninety-six pounds behind it, and the Ouse is one of his four roads (DB).
- **Witnesses.** Under Edgar's and Cnut's law the sworn men who saw you buy are your proof you are no thief.
- **A strong earl.** Uhtred (1006-1016) and Siward (1041-1055) keep the roads; so does Tostig, hated for it.

## What is forbidden

- Unequal weights and lending at interest (786; Cnut again in 1020). Horse-flesh (786).
- Buying goods over twenty pence outside a town or without witnesses (from 927); anything over four pence without four witnesses (from 1020).
- Old or foreign coin in the market after a re-coining (from 973). Striking coin outside a town costs the moneyer his life (about 997).
- Selling, loading or carrying on a Sunday at York: twelve ores (about 1020).
- Selling Christians out of the land, above all to heathens (1020).
- Sacrifice, soothsaying, a fence of peace round a tree, a stone or a well (about 1020).

## When to stay away

- **793 and 794, the coast north of the Tees.** Raiders at Lindisfarne and the Tyne mouth. York itself is hungry, not dangerous: grain sells.
- **Spring 796.** Three kings in six weeks, and a Frankish or Frisian hull is unwelcome while King Charles is angry.
- **Summer 844.** Heathen men kill a king somewhere on this coast.
- **1 November 866 to 869.** York is the army's camp. Again in 872 and 873 (the revolt, the army at Torksey on the Trent).
- **874 to 876, the Tyne.** Halfdan's winter camp. Nothing is left to trade with on the Tyne or the Wear afterwards.
- **893 to 896.** Not York itself, but a Humber ship in a southern port is taken for a raider.
- **909 and 910.** Five weeks of harrying, then Tettenhall.
- **914 to 920, the Tyne and Saint Cuthbert's land.** Ragnall's fleet and two battles at Corbridge.
- **Any year a king changes between 939 and 954:** 939-940, 944, 947-948 (Ripon burned), 952, 954. A safe-conduct dies with the king who gave it.
- **993.** Bamburgh sacked, both shores of the Humber harried.
- **Summer 1013 and spring 1016.** A Danish king's fleet lies in the Humber and the Trent and must be fed.
- **October 1065** at York; **May 1066** in the Humber; **September 1066** on the Ouse, when every hull is burned, taken or pressed.
- After the game: 1069 and 1070, when there is no York to come to.

**The good years**, for the other half of the list: 810 to 840 (with your own silver), 883 to 892, 927 to 939, 954 to 992, 1016 to 1064.

## Ten moments for scenes

1. **Midsummer 793: the news is thirteen days old.** A York priest has Alcuin's letter: he will ask King Charles about the boys the heathens carried off (Alcuin ep. 20). Someone must carry the answer, and perhaps the ransom, over the sea.
2. **794: the wreck at the Tyne mouth.** A storm has broken the raiders' ships below Jarrow. Their dead lie on the sand and the living are being killed at the river mouth (HR 794). Good ship timber, iron and a sail are there for whoever will take them.
3. **Lent 808: a king for cargo.** Eardwulf, driven out, needs a hull from the Humber to Nijmegen (ARF 808). He pays in promises. Next year he is king again; or the other king's men remember who carried him.
4. **About 840: a bucket of brass.** The buyer at York pays in stycas, eight thousand to the bucket (the Hexham hoard). Nobody over the sea will take one. Take goods instead, or the insult.
5. **All Saints' Day 866.** You lie at York for the feast when the army rides in over the Humber. On 21 March the two kings break through the wall and die inside it (ASC 867). Leave, hide, or sell to the new masters.
6. **875 to 882: the cart and the coffin.** Seven bearers, a bishop and a dead saint want a ship to Ireland from the Derwent mouth. A storm turns her back and the gospel book goes overboard; it lies unharmed on the sands at Whithorn at low tide (LDE ii.11-12).
7. **About 883: the slave who is made king.** An abbot has dreamed that the army must buy a Danish boy from a widow at Whittingham and make him king on a hill with a gold ring on his arm (HSC 13). The army does it. A merchant who knows the price of a slave sees the oddest sale of his life, and the peace that follows it.
8. **927: the king's penny.** Æthelstan pulls down the Danes' stronghold and shares out what is in it. Your Saint Peter pennies, with the sword and the hammer, must go to the moneyer Regnald and come back fewer, with EFORWIC on them.
9. **947 to 954: whose town is it?** You come up the Ouse with King Olaf's word and find King Eric's men on the staith; or King Eadred's army on the road with Ripon burning behind it (ASC D 948). In 954 the news is Stainmore, and an earl.
10. **September 1066: Riccall.** Three hundred Norwegian ships lie in the Ouse below York. On the 20th the earls are beaten at Fulford; on the 25th twenty-four ships are enough to take the Norwegians home (ASC 1066). The house's last choice in the north: who gets your hull.

Kept in hand: the washed heads on Durham's wall and a cow for each washer (1006, DOD); the curtain in the hall at Wiheal (1016, DOD); King Cnut walking five miles barefoot to the saint (1031, LDE iii.8); the earl's treasury broken open (3 October 1065, JW).

## Against `chronicle_793.json`

Forty-three entries in the JSON (nineteen rulers, thirteen wars, six raids, three church entries, one law, one coin) match an entry there and carry its id in `chronicle`.

**Wrong, or needing a second look**

- `1066-09-25-stamford-bridge` puts Fulford in the title of the 25th. Fulford was on 20 September and York's surrender came between: it wants its own entry on `1066-09-20`, with `eoforwic`.
- `939-10-27-aethelstan-dies` reads as if Olaf took York and the Danish Midlands at once, "from his brother Edmund" (Edmund was Æthelstan's brother, not Olaf's). York fell late in 939; the Midlands and the peace of Leicester are 940. It has no `end`: 944.
- `796-05-osbald-flees` fires on 15 May, a day after `796-05-14-eardwulf-king`. Twenty-seven days counted from 18 April with the first day end on 14 May: fire it on the 14th, before Eardwulf.
- `876-northumbria-shared` has no `realm` (northumbria), and its effect speaks of trade with Dublin, which belongs to 919 and after.
- `910-08-05-tettenhall` names two kings. Æthelweard has three: Halfdan, Eowils and Ivar (?).
- `937-brunanburh` lists `eoforwic` as a port. Only John of Worcester brings Olaf's fleet into the Humber; mark it (?).
- `918-06-12-aethelflaed-dies` is filed at `legacaestir`; she died at Tamworth. York's offer to her is right (Mercian Register 918).
- `1018-heregeld` is a tribute, not the heregeld. The yearly army-tax runs from 1012 to 1051 and has no entry; nor has its ending (ASC D 1052 (=1051)).
- `954-eric-york` does not say what comes next: Oswulf of Bamburgh as the first earl.
