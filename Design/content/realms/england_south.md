# The English south of the Humber, 780-1070, as a merchant lives it

Mercia, Wessex, Kent, East Anglia, then one kingdom. The data is in `england_south.json` (rulers, who holds each port,
wars, raids, laws, coins, church); this page is the same matter told from the quay. Years are true years. **ASC** is the
Anglo-Saxon Chronicle with its annal year (true year in brackets); **S** a charter by Sawyer number; laws by king and
code (II As = Æthelstan's second code). `(?)` marks what is doubtful or was not re-checked.

**Four things to know before the first landing.** You pay the king's reeve where you land: the wic-reeve in 793,
the port-reeve after 900; by King Edward's time an earl has a third of the town's dues (Domesday, Dover (?)). The king
is a stranger's only kin: he protects you, and takes two-thirds of your man-price if you are killed (Ine 23). You buy
before witnesses, or you are a thief (Hl 16; Ine 25), and you do not leave the road without shouting or blowing a horn
(Ine 20). What is forbidden only grows: buying outside a port (about 905), Sunday markets and horses over sea (about
928), Christian slaves over sea (1008).

## Decade by decade

- **780s. Offa's peace.** Offa of Mercia holds Lundenwic and, from 785, Kent; the West Saxon king is his son-in-law
  (ASC 787). Pay his wic-reeve on the Strand, and the toll at Sarre and Fordwic (S 86, S 29). About 789 three ships
  beach at Portland and kill the reeve who took them for traders (ASC 787; Æthelweard).
- **790s. The ports shut, then open.** About 790 Charles and Offa close their coasts to each other's merchants over a
  marriage that was refused; a Frisian is Charles's man and is turned away (Gesta of Saint-Wandrille 12). In 792 the
  penny is made heavier. In 796 Charles's letter promises merchants protection on both sides (Alcuin ep. 100). Stay out
  of Kent 796-798: it rises, and Cenwulf harries it to the Marsh. Lundenwic burns in 798 (HR).
- **800s. Quiet.** One Mercian king takes the toll at Lundenwic, in Kent and at Gipeswic; Ecgberht comes home from the
  Frankish court to rule Wessex (802). London burns again (801). Nothing is forbidden but what always is.
- **810s. King against archbishop.** Cenwulf and Archbishop Wulfred quarrel over the minsters of Reculver and Thanet,
  which own toll-free ships (816-821). Carry letters for neither. Kentish charters now speak of service against
  heathens and their forts (S 168, S 186 (?)).
- **820s. Kent changes hands.** Ecgberht beats the Mercians at Ellendun (825); Kent, Surrey, Sussex and Essex turn to
  Wessex, and the East Angles kill two Mercian kings and strike their own penny again (ASC 823, 825). From 825 the reeve
  at Sarre, Fordwic and Dofras is a West Saxon's man. Lundenwic stays Mercian, but for 829-830.
- **830s. The first fleets.** Sheppey harried (835); the king beaten at Carhampton (836), winning at Hingston Down
  (838). Protection now means the shire's ealdorman and his levy. Keep off Sheppey and the open Somerset shore.
- **840s. The beach markets burn.** Hamwic beats off thirty-three ships (840); in 842 comes the "great slaughter" at
  London and Rochester (ASC 839, Ingram), and Hamwic is laid waste (Nithard iv.3). Stay off every open strand in 842.
- **850s. They stay the winter.** On Thanet in 850-851, on Sheppey in 855. The king of Kent takes nine ships at
  Sandwich; 350 ships storm Canterbury and London; Æthelwulf wins at Aclea (ASC 851). The penny is worse each year. In
  London weighing is the king's and is paid for (S 208). Avoid the Wantsum whenever a fleet lies there.
- **860s. Paying does not hold them.** Winchester is stormed (860). In 865 the Kentish promise money and are harried
  anyway. The great army winters among the East Angles, who give it horses (865), and kills their king Edmund (869).
- **870s. Two kingdoms end.** Nine battles in Wessex (871); the army winters in London and the Mercians pay (ASC 872;
  S 1278); Burgred leaves for Rome (874); Alfred is surprised at Chippenham and wins at Edington (878). The roads are
  bad, but the coin is mended: about 875 Alfred and Ceolwulf II call in the base pennies.
- **880s. A border and a burh.** Guthrum's men share out East Anglia (880). Alfred takes London (886) and gives it to
  Æthelred of Mercia. The peace sets the border up the Lea and allows trade across it only with hostages given (AGu 5).
  Ships now land inside the wall at Æthelred's hythe (S 346). Keep out of the Stour and the Medway in 885.
- **890s. Four bad years.** Armies at Appledore and Milton (892), Benfleet, Chester, the Lea (893-895). The burhs
  hold. Alfred builds long ships and mans them partly with Frisians (ASC 897). A trader going inland names his crew to
  the reeve (Af 34).
- **900s. Only in a port.** No buying outside a port, and the port-reeve as witness (I Ew 1). The king's cousin raids
  with the East Anglian Danes until he falls at the Holme (902). Chester is rebuilt (907).
- **910s. The east comes back.** Edward takes London and Oxford (911); a burh is built each year; in 917 the army of
  East Anglia swears to him and in 918 Mercia is his. One king now takes the toll from Chester round to Gipeswic. The
  Danes keep their land and law.
- **920s. One coin.** Æthelstan takes York (927). At Grately: one money, struck only in a port; nothing over twenty
  pence bought outside one; no horses over sea; no Sunday market (II As 12-14, 18, 24). London has eight moneyers.
- **930s. The quiet height.** The Londoners' peace-guild rides after thieves and prices lost stock: a horse 120 pence,
  an ox 30, a cow 20, a pig 10, a sheep 5 (VI As 6 (?)). Nothing to fear in the south.
- **940s.** A Dublin king holds the boroughs north of Watling Street (940-942); King Edmund is stabbed at his own
  feast (946). The southern ports feel neither.
- **950s.** The kingdom is split at the Thames between two brothers (957-959) without a blow (ASC B, C 957).
- **960s. Edgar's order.** One measure and weight, as at London and Winchester; wool at 120 pence the wey; thirty-six
  sworn witnesses in every burh (III Eg 8; IV Eg 3-6). Plague, and Saint Paul's burns (962). Monks replace clerks at
  Winchester (964), and the great abbeys become the steady buyers of wine, wax and glass (our reading).
- **970s. The new penny.** About 973 every old penny, and all foreign silver, must be changed at a mint for the new
  one, and again every few years; the king takes his share. Edgar dies (975), famine (976), the boy king is killed at
  Corfe (978).
- **980s. They come back.** Seven ships sack Southampton; Thanet and Cheshire are harried (980); then Padstow,
  Portland, Watchet. London burns (982). No fleet meets them.
- **990s. Tribute.** Ninety-three ships harry Sandwich and Gipeswic and kill Byrhtnoth at Maldon: 10,000 pounds
  (991). London beats off ninety-four ships: 16,000 pounds (994). The treaty gives a merchant ship peace in any estuary
  (II Atr 2). The tribute is raised on every hide, so landholders sell for coin. Avoid the Medway and Wight (997-999).
- **1000s. The worst.** 24,000 pounds, and the killing of the Danes on Saint Brice's day (1002); Exeter, Norwich and
  Thetford burned (1003-1004); famine (1005); a second great tribute (1007); the king's new fleet wrecks itself at
  Sandwich and Thorkell comes (1009). At London the toll goes by the ship: a halfpenny for a small one (IV Atr 2). No
  Christian may be sold abroad (V Atr 2). No Dane should be in an English town in November 1002.
- **1010s. A Danish king.** Gipeswic (1010), Canterbury (1011), the archbishop killed at Greenwich and 48,000 pounds
  (1012); Sweyn king (1013); the sea-flood (1014); Cnut besieging London (1016). Then 72,000 pounds, 10,500 more from
  London (1018), and peace. The yearly army-tax begins (1012). Stay out of the Thames in 1016.
- **1020s. Cnut's peace.** One king on both sides of the sea and a hired fleet on the coast. Four witnesses for
  anything over four pence (II Cn 24). His letter from Rome wins lower tolls on the road south (1027). The safest
  decade of all for a ship between Frisia, London and Denmark.
- **1030s.** Cnut dies (1035); the shipmen of London choose Harold; for two years the kingdom is split at the Thames
  and the queen waits at Bruges. Wheat reaches 55 pence the sester (ASC 1039).
- **1040s.** Harthacnut's ship-tax of 21,099 pounds; Worcester harried for killing two collectors (1041). Wheat at 60
  pence (1044). In 1048 Lothen and Yrling take Sandwich with twenty-five ships and sell the plunder in Flanders.
- **1050s.** The hired ships are paid off and the army-tax ends (1051); the Kentish harbours owe ships instead. A
  brawl at Dover with Count Eustace's men brings down Earl Godwine; he returns with ships from every harbour between
  Pevensey and Sandwich (1052).
- **1060s.** Westminster is hallowed and the king dead within the week. In 1066 Tostig presses shipmen at Sandwich
  (May), the king's fleet waits off Wight until the food is gone (8 September), William lands and Dover burns. London
  keeps its laws by his writ (1067). Danish fleets try the east coast in 1069-1070 and go home paid.

## Ten scenes

1. **Portland, about 789.** Three strange ships on the shingle. The reeve rides down to bring the crews to the king's
   town, as he would bring you, and does not come back. Beached nearby, the player can warn him, watch, or sail.
2. **Lundenwic, spring 796.** The reeve has Charles's letter read out on the Strand: the ports are open and merchants
   stand under both kings. A Frank asks why English cloaks have got so short. The letter can be claimed in a toll dispute.
3. **Lundenwic, 842.** You round the bend to a beach of burnt booths. Survivors offer everything for a passage out;
   goods lie unclaimed. Take, help, or go.
4. **Sandwich, 851.** The king of Kent comes in with nine prizes from the first fight in ships; that winter the
   heathen fleet stays on Thanet. The toll man at Sarre asks whom you mean to sell to.
5. **London, winter 871-872.** The army keeps the winter inside the old wall while a bishop sells land to pay his
   share of its tribute (S 1278). It has silver and slaves and no bread: the best profit and the worst deed of the decade.
6. **The south coast, 896.** The king's new long ships need men who know the sea, and take Frisians. Three do not come
   home: Wulfheard, Æbbe, Æthelhere (ASC 897). A king's man offers you a summer's pay.
7. **Any port, about 973.** The reeve will not take your pennies: they are the old kind. The moneyer changes them and
   keeps his share. Pay, haggle, or find a man across the Lea who buys by weight.
8. **Billingsgate, about 1000.** The toll man measures the ship, not the cargo: a halfpenny from you, four pence from
   the hulk beside you, whose skipper hands over pepper, gloves and vinegar and is treated as a Londoner (IV Atr 2).
9. **Greenwich, Easter week 1012.** The army has its 48,000 pounds and wine from the south; the archbishop will not be
   ransomed and dies under ox bones. Its ending comes in June 1023, when a Danish king has the body rowed over the
   Thames while his guard keeps the Londoners away (ASC 1012, 1023; Osbern, later).
10. **Sandwich to Dover, 1066.** Shipmen pressed in May, a coast eaten bare all summer, the fleet sent home three weeks
    too soon, Dover burned after it surrendered, and at Christmas a new king and the same toll. The last card.

## Against `chronicle_793.json`

**It lacks** (each is in `england_south.json` with its source):

- *Who holds the ports.* Ellendun and the loss of Kent, Surrey, Sussex and Essex to Wessex, with the East Angles'
  revolt (825-827): the largest gap, since sarre, fordwic and dofras change realm here. Ecgberht's year in Mercia and
  London (829-830). The settlement of East Anglia under Guthrum (880) and its submission to Edward (917). Edward taking
  London and Oxford (911). Chester deserted (893) and restored (907, now only in a note). The kingdom split at the Thames (957-959, and
  again 1035-1037). Godwine's exile and return (1051-1052).
- *Kings.* No entry for the deaths of Cenwulf (821), Ecgberht (839), Æthelwulf (858), Edward the Elder (924), Edmund
  (946), Eadred (955), Edgar (975) or Æthelred II (1016), the killing at Corfe and Æthelred's accession (978), Harold
  Harefoot (1037) or Harthacnut's coming (1040).
- *Raids.* Sheppey 835; Carhampton 836 and 843; Hamwic and Portland 840; Romney Marsh 841; London and Rochester 842
  (its 842 entry has Quentovic and Hamwic only); the Sandwich ship-fight 851; Thanet 853 and 865; Sheppey 855;
  Winchester 860; the army in London 871-872; Wareham and the Swanage wreck 876-877; Fulham 879; Rochester and the Stour
  885; Benfleet and the Lea 893-895; the fight of 896 with its three named Frisians (the entry has only the building of
  the ships); Padstow 981, Portland 982, Watchet 988; the fleet at London 992; Rochester 999; Exeter 1003; Norwich
  1004; the great fleet 1006; Gipeswic and Ringmere 1010; Canterbury 1011 (only in a note); the maimed hostages at
  Sandwich 1014; the siege of London 1016; Lothen and Yrling 1048; Dover 1067; the Danish fleet of 1069.
- *Law, toll and coin.* The scales at London (857); Alfred's coin reform (about 875-880); the peace of Alfred and
  Guthrum as an entry of its own; Æthelred's hythe (889, 898-899); buying only in a port (I Edward, about 905); the
  London peace-guild (930s); Edgar's witnesses and wool price (about 962); the merchant-ship peace of 994; the ship
  levy and the ban on selling Christians abroad (1008); the army-tax begun 1012 and ended 1051; Cnut's laws (about
  1021); Harthacnut's ship-tax and Worcester (1040-1041).
- *Hunger, fire, church.* Plague and the burning of Saint Paul's (962); famine 976 and 1005; London burnt 982; wheat
  at 55 and 60 pence (1039, 1044); the quarrel of Cenwulf and Wulfred (816-821); Kingston (838); Æthelwulf at Rome
  (855); the clerks driven from Winchester (964); the fast of 1009; Ælfheah brought home (1023); Stigand (1052);
  Westminster hallowed (1065).

**Wrong or doubtful in it:**

- `991-08-maldon` and `1066-09-28-pevensey` list the port **dofras**, but the fleet of 991 touched Folkestone and
  Sandwich, and William landed at Pevensey; neither is Dover. Sandwich is nearest **sarre**. Dover itself was burned
  later in 1066 (William of Poitiers) and has no entry.
- `991-08-maldon` says "the danegeld begins". The word of the time is *gafol*; Danegeld is a Norman word
  (`peoples/english.md` already rules it out).
- `1018-heregeld`: the id and the effect call the payment of 1018 the heregeld. That was a tribute. The heregeld is the
  yearly army-tax begun in 1012 and ended in 1051, in its thirty-ninth year (ASC D 1052).
- `1007-tribute`: 36,000 pounds is the reading of C and D; E has 30,000. The source line gives all three for 36,000.
- `994-09-08-london-attack`: 16,000 pounds is the Chronicle's sum; the treaty itself names 22,000 (II Atr 7.2).
- `838-hingston-down`, `980-raids-southampton-chester` (and several Frankish entries) say "Viking" in the title; the
  brief wants "heathen men", "Danes" or "ship-army".
- `886-london-alfred` puts the border with Guthrum in its effect; the treaty is undated (878-890), as its note admits,
  and could stand as an entry of its own.
- `918-06-12-aethelflaed-dies` gives Mercia to Edward in June; he took it in December 918, when he removed her
  daughter Ælfwynn (Mercian Register 919).
- `1000-london-tolls`: the title has the men of Rouen "show their goods"; in the text they pay six shillings a large
  ship and a twentieth of the fish, and it is the Flemings, Ponthieu, Normandy and France who show and pay.

## What I could not check

Charter numbers, law clauses beyond those on the Fordham page, Nithard, William of Poitiers, Orderic and the coin
type dates are from memory and carry `(?)` in the JSON where I am least sure: S 168 and S 186 (heathen forts in
Kent), the wool price being a floor (III Eg 8), VI Æthelstan's price list, the Dover and Chester customs in Domesday,
the stops of the Danish fleet of 1069, the place of Cenwulf's death, and Guthrum II. The peace-guild of the 930s uses
the word guild for Londoners a lifetime before the brief's "no guilds by that name before c. 1000"; it is a guild for
keeping the peace, not for trade, but the brief's rule may want a note.
