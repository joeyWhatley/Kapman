# Gods and cult, 793 to 1066: what is known, and what is not

For the writers of cards, places and people. Written 6 October 2026. It goes deeper than section 4 (Faith) of each `peoples/*.md` file and agrees with them; where a people's file says a thing more exactly for one port, that file wins.

**How to read it.** Keys in square brackets: see Sources at the end. **(?)** = written from memory and not checked against the source on the day; check before a quote or a date goes into the game. **late** = written down after 1066: colour at most, never a fact. **far** = true, but of another place or time than the card. Every section ends with **May show** and **Must not**.

**How it was checked.** One web check was made (the Chronicle's wording for 876 and 878). The Fordham pages for Rimbert and Notker carry only part of the text, so no words of Robinson or Grant are quoted here; Rimbert is cited by chapter and told in plain words. Latin and Old Saxon are quoted from the originals (out of copyright); the English beside them is a fresh gloss, not a translation taken from a book.

## 0. Five rules before anything else

1. **Almost everything was written by the other side.** No heathen of these peoples left a book. We have: what churchmen forbade, what missionaries boasted of breaking, what two or three Arab travellers saw, a few lines of verse by court poets, short runic texts, and what is dug up. A writer shows what a Christian saw, or what a spade found, and is honest that the meaning was the heathen's own.
2. **There is no one "Norse religion".** A farm in Uppland, a ship's crew at Hedeby and a Wendish fort did not share a creed. There was no book, no church, no word for "religion": the nearest is *siðr*, "custom". People kept the custom of their fathers and their district. Say "the old custom", "what our fathers did".
3. **The Eddas are not a source for what a man did in 800.** Snorri wrote about 1220 in Christian Iceland; the poems of the Codex Regius were written down about 1270. They hold old matter, but nobody can say which lines. Use them for nothing a card states as fact. The safe native voices are the skalds whose verses can be dated (see 1.14) and the runes.
4. **Doing, not believing.** What is attested is acts: a beast killed and eaten together, a lot cast, a vow made in danger, a thing hung on a tree, a dead man burned with his goods. Nobody is recorded explaining a doctrine. Write acts.
5. **Christian practice is as strange as heathen practice** and far better recorded. A Frankish merchant who fasts forty days, swears on a dead man's bones and carries hot iron to prove he is no thief is not the "modern" one in the scene.

## 1. The sources, one by one

For each: what it is, how near it stands, what it gives, where it misleads.

### 1.1 The Old Saxon baptismal vow [Vow]

A question-and-answer form for baptising Saxons, in a manuscript of about 800 (Vatican, Pal. lat. 577, written at Fulda or Mainz (?)). The only text of the age in which a heathen people's gods are named in their own tongue by people who had to renounce them:

> *Forsachistu diobolae? ec forsacho diabolae. end allum diobolgelde? end ec forsacho allum diobolgeldae. end allum dioboles uuercum? end ec forsacho allum dioboles uuercum and uuordum, Thunaer ende Uuoden ende Saxnote ende allum them unholdum the hira genotas sint.*

Gloss: "Do you forsake the devil? I forsake the devil. And all devil's offering? And I forsake all devil's offering. And all the devil's works? And I forsake all the devil's works and words, Thunaer and Uoden and Saxnote and all those fiends that are their fellows." Then three questions of belief: in God the almighty Father, in Christ God's son, in the Holy Ghost.

- **Gives:** three Saxon gods by name; *diobolgeld* (payment or offering to devils) as the word for sacrifice; the fact that the gods were called real and wicked, not unreal.
- **Misleads if:** read as a list of all the gods, or as their rank. Saxnot is known from nowhere else but the East Saxon kings' pedigree in England (Seaxnet). The dialect is mixed; the form may have been made for more than Saxons (?).

### 1.2 The Indiculus superstitionum et paganiarum [Ind]

In the same manuscript: thirty headings of a lost text, a list of "superstitions and heathen ways", tied to the Frankish church councils of the 740s under Boniface. Only the headings survive, so each is a riddle. The ones a writer can use:

| No. | Heading (short) | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| 1, 2 | sacrilege at the graves of the dead; over the dead, "that is *dadsisas*" | rites and (?) songs or meals at burials |
| 3 | *spurcalia* in February | a feast with pig-killing (?) in late winter |
| 4 | little houses, "that is shrines" (*casulis id est fanis*) | huts as holy places |
| 6 | rites of the woods "which they call *nimidas*" | holy groves |
| 7 | what they do upon stones | offerings on rocks |
| 8, 20 | rites and feast days of "Mercury and Jupiter" | the churchman's names for Woden and Thunor |
| 9 | sacrifice made to one of the saints | baptised people offering to a saint as to a god |
| 10 | amulets and bindings (*filacteriis et ligaturis*) | charms worn or tied on |
| 11 | springs of sacrifice | offerings at wells |
| 13 | omens from birds, horses, the dung of cattle, sneezing | everyday augury |
| 14 | diviners and lot-casters | the lot (see 5) |
| 15 | fire rubbed from wood, "that is *nodfyr*" | the need-fire, against cattle plague |
| 17 | heathen watching of the hearth, or at the start of any business | luck-acts before work, a journey, a deal |
| 18 | uncertain places which they honour as holy | wayside holy spots with no saint |
| 21 | the eclipse of the moon, "which they call *Vince luna*" | shouting "win, moon!" at an eclipse |
| 22 | storms, horns and snail-shells | horn-blowing against bad weather (?) |
| 23 | furrows round farmsteads | a ploughed ring for protection |
| 24 | the heathen course "which they call *yrias*", in torn clothes or shoes | a race or procession (unknown) |
| 25 | that they make saints of any of the dead | honouring the dead as holy |
| 26, 27, 28 | an idol of dough; idols of rags; an idol carried through the fields | cheap household images; a procession round the crops |
| 29 | wooden feet and hands "in the heathen manner" | limb-shaped offerings for healing |
| 30 | that women "command the moon" and can take men's hearts | fear of witch-women |

- **Gives:** the best list there is of what ordinary baptised Franks, Frisians and Saxons still did. This is the cupboard for small card details.
- **Misleads if:** read as a description of a heathen religion. Half of it is Christians doing folk things; several headings are copied from older church lists from Gaul (Caesarius of Arles) and may not be local (?).

### 1.3 The Lex Frisionum [LF]

Frisian law written for Charlemagne about 802, with "additions of the wise men" Wlemar and Saxmund. Two places speak of shrines, one of lots.

- Title V lists people who may be killed without payment: among them "he who has broken into a shrine" (*qui fanum effregit*) and a newborn killed by its mother.
- The addition (Add. XI): *Qui fanum effregerit, et ibi aliquid de sacris tulerit, ducitur ad mare, et in sabulo, quod accessus maris operire solet, finduntur aures eius, et castratur, et immolatur diis, quorum templa violavit.* Gloss: "Whoever breaks into a shrine and takes any of the holy things there is led to the sea, and on the sand which the tide covers his ears are slit, he is gelded, and he is offered to the gods whose temples he violated."
- Title XIV, the lot for a killing in a crowd: two rods cut from a twig, "which they call *tenos*", one marked with a cross, wrapped in clean wool and laid on the altar or on relics; a priest, or failing him an innocent boy, lifts one (told in full in 5).

- **Gives:** the only law of the age that protects heathen shrines; the tide-flat as the place of offering; the plural "gods"; a Christian court still casting lots.
- **Misleads if:** taken as living practice in the Christian west of Frisia in 793. It is old custom written down, most at home east of the Lauwers, and it stood oddly in a Christian king's law even then. Nobody knows that it was ever carried out in these years.

### 1.4 The saints' lives of the Frisian and Saxon mission [VW] [VL] [VVulf] [VBon]

Alcuin's Life of Willibrord (about 796), Willibald's Life of Boniface (about 760), Altfrid's Life of Liudger (about 840), the Life of Wulfram (about 800).

- **Gives:** Fosite's island and its silent spring, its cattle no one might touch; King Radbod casting lots three times a day for three days over Willibrord's men, and one dying [VW 10-11]; an idol on Walcheren with a guardian who struck the bishop with a sword [VW 14]; Boniface felling the oak "of Jupiter" at Geismar in Hesse (723) [VBon 6]; Liudger breaking shrines and finding treasure in them, of which the king took two thirds [VL I.16 (?)]; the newborn who may be drowned only before it has tasted food [VL I.6-7].
- **Misleads if:** the Life of Wulfram is trusted. Its Radbod with one foot in the font, asking where his fathers are, and its boys drowned by the tide for the gods are a monastery's tale written two generations on. They show what Franks told about Frisians.

### 1.5 The Saxon capitulary and the annals [CPS] [ARF] [TSA]

Charlemagne's law for conquered Saxony (782 or 785), the Royal Frankish Annals, and Rudolf of Fulda's account written about 865.

- **Gives:** what was punished and so what was done: burning the dead (c. 7), offering a man "to the devil" (c. 9), vows at springs, trees and groves and eating there "in honour of demons" (c. 21), burial in "the mounds of the heathen" (c. 22), diviners and lot-casters (c. 23), belief that a man or woman is a witch who eats people, and burning them for it (c. 6). The Irminsul destroyed in 772 [ARF]: "a trunk of wood of no small size set upright under the open sky", in their tongue Irminsul, "the universal column, as if holding up all things" [TSA].
- **Misleads if:** Rudolf is used beyond that one sentence; the rest of his account of Saxon belief is copied from Tacitus, seven hundred and fifty years out of date.

### 1.6 Rimbert, Life of Anskar [Rim]

Written about 875 by Anskar's pupil and successor at Hamburg-Bremen. Anskar was at Hedeby and Birka himself (about 829-831, and again about 852). This is the nearest thing to a witness at a northern trading town, and it is about merchants.

- **Gives:** lots cast in the open to ask the gods a yes or no (c. 19, 27, 30); a man who brings a message from the gods to the assembly: they are angry at the new god, and if the Svear want more gods they will take the late king Eric among them (c. 26); sacrifice and vows in danger (c. 19); a diviner asked which god a man has offended (c. 18); the keeper of Birka, Herigar, baptised, with a church on his own land (c. 11, 19); the rich widow Frideburg, who kept a little wine by her for her deathbed, and her daughter Catla, who carried her alms to Dorestad because there were many churches, priests and poor there (c. 20); men who take the sign of the cross to be let into church and put off baptism to the deathbed (c. 24); the church bell at Hedeby, unlawful to the heathen, allowed at last by the younger Horik (c. 32); an old man who tells the assembly that many of them have had this god's help at sea (c. 27).
- **Misleads if:** read as fair. Every lot falls the way the mission needs. Keep the acts, doubt the outcomes.

### 1.7 Thietmar of Merseburg [Thiet]

A Saxon bishop writing 1012-1018, on the Wendish border, with Liutizi warriors serving in his own emperor's army.

- **Gives:** the fullest account in the period of a Wendish temple, Riedegost of the Redarii (VI.23-25): three gates, a wooden shrine standing on the horns of beasts, carved images of gods and goddesses on the outer walls, inside gods "made by hand" in helmets and mail, each with its name cut on it, the first called Zuarasici; banners kept there and carried only to war; priests who alone sit while others stand, who mutter and dig in the earth to cast lots, then lead a holy horse over the points of two crossed spears; a great boar that comes out of the lake when war is near. Also the spring Glomuzi near Lommatzsch, which foretells: wheat, oats and acorns on it for peace and plenty, blood and ash for war (I.3); a shepherd's staff with a hand holding an iron ring, carried from house to house and greeted as "Hennil" (VII.69 (?)); Bishop Reinbern at Kolberg, about 1000, burning shrines and throwing four stones anointed with oil into the sea to cleanse it of demons (VII.72 (?)).
- And of the Danes (I.17): at Lejre every ninth year in January, ninety-nine men and as many horses, dogs and cocks killed. He places it before 934 and had it by hearsay eighty years on.
- **Misleads if:** the Lejre numbers are stated as fact, or the temple's details are moved to another Wendish town.

### 1.8 Adam of Bremen [Adam]

A canon of Bremen writing about 1075, a few years after the game ends. He never went north of Denmark; his chief informant was the Danish king Svein Estridsson.

- **Gives:** Uppsala (IV.26-27 and the notes added to it): a temple with three images, Thor in the middle with a sceptre, Wodan armed, Fricco "with a huge member"; Thor for thunder, weather and crops, Wodan for war, Fricco for peace and marriage; each god has priests; every ninth year a common feast of all the Svear that no one may miss, and Christians buy themselves off; nine males of every living kind offered, the bodies hung in a grove by the temple, every tree of it holy; a spring where a living man is plunged; songs "manifold and unseemly"; a note says a Christian counted seventy-two bodies, and another speaks of a gold chain round the gable. Also Rethra (II.21), the death of Bishop John there in 1066 (III.51), the Prussians' closed groves (IV.18), the Curonians' diviners (IV.16), Heligoland as Fosite's island (IV.3), the Englishman Wulfred who smashed Thor's image at an assembly of the Svear about 1030 and was killed (II.62).
- **Misleads if:** the temple is drawn as he tells it. No such building has been found at Old Uppsala; there is a very large hall and royal mounds, both centuries older than the game. He wrote to show how much work Hamburg-Bremen still had to do. The nine-year feast, the hanging and the buying-off are likely enough; the gold is not.

### 1.9 Ibn Fadlan [IbnF]

Envoy of the caliph to the Volga Bulgars, 921-922. He watched *Rūs* traders on the Volga and wrote what he saw, with an interpreter.

- **Gives:** a trader's prayer: he lays bread, meat, onions, milk and drink before a tall wooden post with a face, small figures round it, tells it how many slave girls and sable skins he has brought, and asks for a buyer with many coins who will not haggle; if trade is good he kills sheep or cattle, gives some meat away, and hangs the heads on the posts. A sick man is put in a tent apart. And the burial of a chief: ten days of preparation, the ship hauled ashore, a slave girl who offers herself, an old woman called "the Angel of Death" who kills her, the nearest kinsman walking backward and naked to light the pyre, a mound raised and a birch post cut with the dead man's name.
- **Misleads if:** carried west unchanged. These men were on the Volga, a thousand miles from Birka, three generations after the game begins, living among Slavs, Finns and Turks. Some of it fits what is dug up in Scandinavia (ship, mound, a companion killed); the rest is theirs.

### 1.10 Ibn Rustah, and al-Tartushi [IbnR] [Tar]

Ibn Rustah, a Persian geographer writing about 903-913 from older reports, never saw the north. Al-Tartushi (Ibrahim ibn Yaqub), a traveller from Muslim Spain, was at Hedeby himself about 965; his words survive in a later geographer.

- **Ibn Rustah gives:** the *Rūs* have healers who order the king to offer women, men and beasts to their maker, and the healer hangs the offering from a pole by the neck; a great man is laid in a grave like a large house with clothes, gold, food and drink, and the wife he loved is shut in with him alive (?).
- **Al-Tartushi gives, of Hedeby:** the people worship Sirius, save a few Christians who have a church; they hold a feast to honour their god and to eat and drink; who kills a beast in offering sets it on poles at the door of his house so that people know; unwanted infants are thrown into the sea; their singing is like the howling of dogs, only worse.
- **Misleads if:** "Sirius" is used (his guess at an unknown god), or if Ibn Rustah's hearsay is told as an eyewitness.

### 1.11 Place names [PN]

Names fixed before or during these years that hold a god's name or a word for a holy place. The words: *vi/vé* (holy enclosure), *hov/hof* (a hall where rites were held), *harg/hörgr* (a heap of stones, an altar), *lund* (grove), *al* (shrine (?)), *helg-* (holy); English *weoh* and *hearg*; Frankish *nimid*.

- **Gives:** which gods were honoured where. Odense is "Odin's *vi*" (written 988). Thor, Frey, Ull, Njord and Odin are on the map in Sweden; Thor, Frey, Ull and Njord in Norway, Odin seldom; Tyr almost only in Denmark (Tissø, "Tyr's lake"). Ull, hardly a name in the stories, is all over the Mälar country and Viken: the map and the Eddas do not match. Loki has no place; Balder next to none.
- **Misleads if:** every "holy" name is taken as proof of a temple, or a name is dated to the game's years without evidence: many are older. Dutch and North German Woden and Donar names are argued over one by one (?); use none as a fact without a people's file behind it.

### 1.12 Amulets and small finds [Am]

- **Thor's hammers.** More than a thousand found, of iron, bronze, silver and amber; the great number are of the tenth century, in Denmark, southern Sweden and the Mälar country; in the ninth century they are few and mostly iron rings with small hammers on them, laid in the urns of the burnt dead around the Mälaren. A small one from Købelev on Lolland has runes: "this is a hammer" (*hmar x is*). They rise just as crosses arrive: the hammer worn at the neck may be an answer to the cross.
- **The Trendgården mould** (north Jutland, about 950-1000): one soapstone block cut to cast a cross and two hammers. The smith sold both.
- **Crosses and crucifixes** in graves from about 900 (Birka).
- **Other pendants:** tiny chairs, staffs, shields, fire-steels, snakes, women with a horn (called "valkyries" now), an armed rider and a woman with a shield (Hårby, Tissø), a seated silver figure with two birds from Lejre (about 900; called Odin, but the dress is a woman's and it is argued over).
- **Charms in runes:** a piece of skull from Ribe (about 725) asking Ulf, Odin and "High-Tyr" for help against pain; later copper and bronze sheets from Sigtuna and Öland against fever and sickness (11th century) (?).
- **Rings for oaths:** the Chronicle for 876 says the Danish army swore to Alfred "on the holy ring" (see 5).
- **Misleads if:** a hammer is hung on every Northman's neck in 793 (too early for most), or a find's modern nickname (valkyrie, Odin) is put in a person's mouth.

### 1.13 Bracteates [Br]

Thin gold discs stamped on one side, copied from Roman medallions, worn as pendants, with a head over a horse, birds, and short rune words (*alu*, *laukaR*, *laþu*). Made about 450-550.

- **Gives:** proof that some of the god-pictures are very old, long before the game.
- **Misleads if:** worn as a current thing. They are 250 years out of date in 793. One could turn up as an heirloom, a find from a ploughed mound, or scrap gold on a scale. Nobody of the game's time could say what the picture meant, and neither can we.

### 1.14 Picture stones, runestones and the skalds [Pic] [Run] [Sk]

- **Gotland picture stones** of the eighth to tenth centuries (Tängelgårda, Ardre, Lärbro, Stora Hammars): a ship under sail with the crew holding the sheets, a rider met by a woman with a horn, a horse with eight legs, a man laid on a small platform under a knot of three triangles while another is hung from a bent tree, armed men behind. The names we give the scenes come from Icelandic books four hundred years younger.
- **The Oseberg hanging** (buried 834): a procession of wagons and walking people, a tree with bodies hanging in it.
- **Thor's fishing,** the god in a boat hauling up a monster with an ox-head for bait, his foot through the planks: carved at Altuna in Uppland, Hørdum in Thy, Gosforth in Cumbria and on Gotland, all tenth or eleventh century. This one story is safely of the period.
- **Runestones:** "Thor hallow these runes" on a few Danish and Swedish stones of the tenth century (Glavendrup, Sønder Kirkeby, Virring, Velanda); a curse on whoever moves the stone (Glavendrup, Tryggevælde); the title *goði* on three stones on Funen. From about 1000, by the thousand in Uppland: a cross, and "God help his soul"; men who "died in white clothes", that is in the week after baptism; bridges and causeways built "for the soul".
- **The skalds:** poets at kings' courts whose verses, kept in later books, can be dated by the kings they name. Haraldskvæði (about 900): the king will "drink Yule" at sea. Eiríksmál (about 954) and Hákonarmál (about 961): a dead king welcomed by Odin in his hall. Sonatorrek (about 960): a father whose son has drowned says the sea-god and Rán have robbed him and that he would fight them if he could. Sigvat's verses of about 1019: turned from four farm doors in one evening in Götaland, because the house was holding a sacrifice to the elves, and the woman feared Odin's anger. Verses to Thor of about 990-1000 listing the giants he killed.
- **Misleads if:** court poetry about kings is taken for what a farmer or a rower hoped for.

### 1.15 What the spade has found [Arch]

- **Halls with a holy yard:** Tissø on Zealand (a great man's hall with a fenced plot and a small building inside it; weapons and tools thrown in the lake); Lejre; Uppåkra in Scania, where a small tall house stood on one spot for centuries, with a glass bowl, a beaker and gold-foil figures under its floor (pulled down in the tenth century (?)).
- **Borg in Östergötland:** a small house with a stone platform, a paved yard, about a hundred iron amulet rings and heaps of bones, skulls and jaws above all; buried under gravel about 1000.
- **Frösö church in Jämtland:** under the altar, the stump of a birch with the bones of bears, elk, deer, pigs and cattle round it, tenth and eleventh century.
- **Trelleborg on Zealand:** wells older than the fort, with the bones of children and beasts in them (?).
- **Old Uppsala:** a huge hall on a terrace and the royal mounds (sixth and seventh century); no temple.
- **Wendish:** a timber hall at Groß Raden (ninth and tenth century) with plank walls ending in carved heads, long called a temple, now doubted; a small wooden figure with four faces from Wolin (ninth century (?)); a two-headed oak post from the Fischerinsel near Neubrandenburg (eleventh or twelfth century).
- **Gives:** the surest ground there is: beasts killed and eaten in number, skulls kept, things given to water, rites in the lord's own hall and yard.
- **Misleads if:** a find is given a liturgy. Bones do not say what words were spoken.

### 1.16 The English, Irish and Rus records [ASC] [Laws] [AU] [PVL]

- The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle: the heathen army's oath on the ring (876), the banner "which they called the Raven" taken in Devon (878).
- English church law against the settlers' ways: Wulfstan of York for King Cnut (about 1020) forbids honouring "heathen gods, and the sun or the moon, fire or flood, wells or stones or any kind of forest trees", and witchcraft and lots [Laws II Cnut 5]. Ælfric's sermon on false gods (about 1000) says the Danes call Jove Thor and Mercury Othon.
- Irish annals: the "ring of Tomar" taken from the foreigners of Dublin in 994 or 995, and "Tomar's wood" by Dublin cut and burned in 1000 (?). Tomar is Thor.
- The Rus Primary Chronicle (put together about 1113, late but near): the treaties of 907, 945 and 971 sworn by Perun and by Volos "god of cattle", with the Christians among the Rus swearing in St Elias's church; a Christian Northman and his son killed in Kiev in 983 when the lot for an offering fell on the boy.

## 2. Who keeps the old custom, and until when

A card must first ask: in this year, in this port, is anyone still heathen in the open?

| People | In 793 | The turn | In 1066 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Franks | Christian for three hundred years | none | Christian |
| English | Christian for four or five generations | Danish settlers from 865 bring the old gods; baptised within two generations | Christian; law still forbids "heathenship" |
| Frisians west of the Lauwers | Christian for one to three generations, thinly | none | Christian |
| Frisians east of the Lauwers | baptised under arms in the 780s; rose in 784 and again about 792 and burned churches | Liudger's work, to 809; Heligoland baptised about 791 [VL] | Christian |
| Saxons | at war with Charles until 804; baptism or death in law [CPS] | relics brought in from 836; the Stellinga rising of 841-843 "for the custom of the heathens" [Nith] | Christian, and proud of it |
| Danes | heathen | Harald Klak baptised 826; churches at Hedeby (about 850) and Ribe (about 860); bishops named 948; King Harald baptised about 965 | Christian in law; the old ways in corners |
| Northmen of Norway | heathen | kings from 995; church law about 1024; St Olaf from 1031 | Christian in law |
| Icelanders, Faroese | (settled from about 870) | baptism by law 999 or 1000 | Christian |
| Svear and Götar | heathen | Birka's mission 829-about 865 fades; a king baptised about 1008 (?); crosses on stones from about 1020 | mixed: the Uppsala feasts still held [Adam] |
| Gotlanders | heathen | merchants bring priests home in the eleventh century [GS, late] | mostly Christian (?) |
| Wends (Obodrites, Wilzi/Liutizi, Rani, Pomeranians) | heathen | princes baptised from the 930s; the great rising of 983 throws the church out; again in 1018 and 1066 | heathen and at war with the church |
| Prussians, Curonians, Lithuanians, Estonians, Finns | heathen | none | heathen |
| Rus | heathen | Christians among them by 945; the prince baptised 988 | Christian in the towns, Greek rite |

- **May show:** baptism going port by port and class by class, merchants first, because a Christian market is easier for a Christian; a rising that brings the old rites back in the open for a year (East Frisia 793, Saxony 841, the Wends 983 and 1066); grandparents who remember.
- **Must not:** a heathen Frank; an openly heathen Englishman before 865; a "last pagan" speech; conversion as one day's event; anyone calling himself "a pagan" or "a heathen" (those are the Christians' words; he says "we keep the old custom").

## 3. The gods, people by people

One rule covers all of it: **a god may be named in a card only for a people and a time in which a source of the period names him.** Otherwise say "the gods", "the god of this place", "the one they will not name to strangers".

### 3.1 Frisians

- **Known:** "gods" in the plural and their shrines [LF]; **Fosite**, honoured on an island between the Frisians and the Danes, with a spring drawn only in silence and cattle no one touched [VW 10; VL I.22]; an idol on Walcheren [VW 14]. The Old Frisian day names (Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday after Tiw, Woden, Thunor, Frija) show the names were known when the week was learned (?).
- **Not known:** what Fosite was a god of; any Frisian myth; any Frisian calling on Woden.
- **May show:** an old woman who will not draw water noisily; a fisherman from the east who leaves the first fish on a stone; shrines as small huts, broken open by missionaries for the silver in them; Landric the island priest (in the spine) who knows the old rite from inside.
- **Must not:** Fosite as "god of justice" or the same as the Icelandic Forseti (a modern guess); Stavo, Meda, Fosta, Medea, Freda (sixteenth-century scholars' inventions (?)); the Oera Linda Book (a nineteenth-century fake); Nehalennia (a goddess of the Roman age, six hundred years gone); Radbod's foot in the font as an event.

### 3.2 Saxons

- **Known:** **Thunaer, Uoden, Saxnote** [Vow]; the Irminsul [ARF; TSA]; groves, springs, trees, stones [CPS; Ind]; in a charm written down in the tenth century at Merseburg, **Uuodan, Friia, Volla, Sunna, Sinthgunt, Phol and Balder** healing a horse's sprained leg, and women called *idisi* who bind and loose prisoners [Mers].
- **Not known:** what Saxnot was; whether Irmin was a god at all (the word may only mean "great").
- **May show:** a baptised Saxon who can say the vow by heart because he was made to; a horse-leech who mutters the bone-to-bone charm (in a monk's hearing, with a Paternoster after it); the place where the column stood, with nothing there.
- **Must not:** "Wotan" (Wagner's spelling); Ostara as a Saxon goddess (Grimm's guess from Bede's English month-name); Krodo; the Externsteine as a temple; the Irminsul as a world-tree; Widukind's white horse.

### 3.3 Danes

- **Known:** **Thor** called on in runes to hallow a stone; hammers; **Odin** in Odense's name and on the Ribe skull; **Tyr** in place names; a banner called the Raven [ASC 878]; an oath-ring [ASC 876]; "Tomar's ring" at Dublin [AU]; offerings on poles at the door, a town feast [Tar]; anger at a new god and his bell [Rim 31-32]; Widukind's Danes at a feast about 965, who grant that Christ is a god but say theirs are greater [Wid III.65].
- **May show:** a Dane who says Thor's name the way a Frisian says Martin's, for weather and luck, and says little else; a hammer under a shirt after about 900; a king who lets a church be built and stays unbaptised.
- **Must not:** the full Icelandic household of gods recited; Saxo's stories; Ragnar Lodbrok and his sons' vengeance; a Dane swearing "by Odin" in every line (no source has a common man do it).

### 3.4 Northmen of Norway and the western sea

- **Known:** Thor, Frey, Njord, Ull in place names; Odin as the kings' god in court verse; Rán and the sea-god in Sonatorrek; elves given an autumn offering at the farm; *dísir* (women spirits or foremothers) with a feast of their own (name only (?)); Thor verses about 1000.
- **May show:** a shut door in autumn and a woman who says "go away, we are holy here tonight"; a helmsman who names Rán when a man is lost and nothing more; pride in a king's poet.
- **Must not:** Valhalla as every sailor's hope; valkyries carrying the dead; Ragnarök as a known belief of 800; Loki as a devil; "All-father".

### 3.5 Svear and Götar

- **Known:** the gods asked by lot, and answering through a man at the assembly [Rim 26-27]; a dead king made a god [Rim 26; Adam IV.26]; **Thor, Wodan and Fricco** at Uppsala [Adam]; "Thor hallow" on stones; place names for Ull, Frey, Thor, Odin, Njord (as Njärd-); the women's staffs in graves at Birka and on Öland, which may be a seeress's (see 7).
- **May show:** the Birka assembly arguing about a foreign god exactly as it argues about a toll; a trader who has vowed something to Thor against a bad harvest and something to Christ against the sea, and counts both paid.
- **Must not:** the golden temple with its chain; the Yngling kings as history; "Frey" for Adam's Fricco in a person's mouth before you have a reason (Adam wrote Fricco; the people's own form is not recorded there).

### 3.6 Wends

- **Known:** before about 1000, no god's name at all. Then **Zuarasici** (Svarožic) at Riedegost [Thiet VI.23; also a letter of Bruno of Querfurt, 1008]; **Redigast** as the god's name in Adam, who seems to have taken the place's name for the god's; goddesses on banners [Thiet VII.64 (?)]; the staff Hennil; holy springs and lakes.
- **Known a lifetime after the game (late, but the nearest we have):** Triglav at Szczecin (1124-28), Svantevit at Arkona and his white horse, horn and yearly harvest feast (told at its fall in 1168), Prove in an oak grove near Oldenburg, Siwa, Radigast [Ebo; Herb; Helm; Saxo].
- **May show:** a fort town where each district keeps its own temple and banner, and the priests rank above the prince in matters of war; a holy horse no one rides; an oath sealed in the Wendish way (see 5); a merchant asked to give a share to the god of the place before he may sell.
- **Must not:** Svantevit, Triglav or Prove by name before 1066 unless the card is marked as rumour; Belbog and Chernobog as a pair; Lada, Lel, Jarilo, Kupala as gods; the Prillwitz bronzes; the Book of Veles; Perun and Mokosh on the Baltic coast (they are Kiev's).

### 3.7 Prussians, Curonians and their neighbours

- **Known:** holy groves and springs that Christians may not come near, lest they be fouled [Adam IV.18]; diviners in every house among the Curonians, sought out from as far as Spain and Greece, he says [Adam IV.16]; Estonians who buy a man without blemish from the merchants to offer to a "dragon" [Adam IV.17]; a priest, Sicco, among those who kill Adalbert in 997 [Can]; a long wake and a burning (see 8) [Wulf]. No god is named for any Balt before the thirteenth century.
- **May show:** a wood the pilot will not let you cut a spar in; a buyer who looks a slave over for marks and will not say why; a people with no temple at all.
- **Must not:** Perkūnas by name (first written 1261 (?)); Romuva, the high priest Criwe, the three gods Patollo, Potrimpo and Perkuno (Peter of Dusburg 1326 and Simon Grunau about 1520); the Kalevala; holy fire tended by virgins.

### 3.8 Rus

- **Known:** wooden posts with faces [IbnF]; oaths by **Perun** and **Volos** [PVL 907, 945, 971]; Vladimir's row of idols on the hill at Kiev in 980 and their throwing-down in 988 [PVL].
- **Must not:** bring any of it west of Ladoga.

## 4. Sacrifice

**What is known.**

- **The usual offering is a beast, killed and eaten together.** Bones at Borg, Frösö, Tissø and Hofstaðir are those of meals. The Old Norse word is *blót*; the Saxon vow calls it *diobolgeld*; Gotland's later law calls sharers in it *suþnautar*, "boiling-fellows" [GS, late]. Horses were eaten at it: the church made horseflesh the mark of a heathen (a pope's letter to Boniface in 732; Iceland's exemption in 1000) [Bon; Ari].
- **Food and drink are set before a post or image** [IbnF]; heads and hides are hung up afterwards on poles or trees [IbnF; Tar; Adam].
- **Things are given to water and earth:** weapons and tools in the lake at Tissø; silver hidden in the ground and never fetched (though most hoards are savings).
- **There are great feasts at long intervals,** every ninth year at Uppsala and (hearsay) Lejre [Adam; Thiet].
- **People were sometimes killed.** The evidence is of five kinds and agrees: a law [LF Add. XI; CPS 9, which forbids it]; outside witnesses [Thiet; Adam; IbnF; IbnR; PVL 983]; pictures (Stora Hammars, Oseberg); graves where a second person lies bound or beheaded beside the first (Lejre, Stengade, Ballateare on Man) [Arch (?)]; wells with children's bones (Trelleborg) (?). It was rare, for great occasions and great men; the victims were slaves, captives and law-breakers. In 1066 the Wends killed Bishop John at Rethra and offered his head on a spear to their god [Adam III.51].
- **Vows:** in danger or sickness a man promised a gift or a fast to a god if he came through [Rim 19, 30]. Christians did the same to saints (see 10.2).
- **The need-fire:** every hearth put out and new fire rubbed from wood, to drive cattle through against plague [Ind 15]; forbidden, and done.

**May show.** A feast: a hall, a killed ox or horse, the meat boiled in a pit or cauldron, ale, a share set aside. A trader laying bread, an onion and a cup before a post on a river bank and telling it his cargo. A ram's head on a pole by a house door at Hedeby, and the neighbours knowing what it means. A Christian crewman who will not eat at a host's table because the meat was offered, and the trouble that makes (St Paul's question, and a real one: popes wrote to missionaries about it). A slave bought "without blemish" and the seller not asking. The ninth-year feast as a fact of the calendar that empties the roads and fills the market.

**Must not.** Blood sprinkled on walls and people from a bowl with a twig, the *hlautteinn* (Snorri, about 1230: late, and argued over); a stone altar with a victim stretched on it; priests in robes with knives; the blood eagle (a thirteenth-century reading of one hard verse); "sacrifice to Odin for wind" as a routine; any count of victims given as fact; a sacrifice shown as horror for its own sake. The card states what is seen from the edge of the crowd, and the player's man is a stranger there.

## 5. Lots, omens and oaths

**Lots: the best-attested act of the old custom, among every people on the chart.**

- **Svear and Danes:** to ask whether a god is willing, whether to attack a town, whether a mission may stay, which god will help [Rim 18, 19, 26, 27, 30]. The lot answers yes or no to a question put; it is cast in the open, before the assembly, by men of standing.
- **Frisians:** King Radbod casts lots three times a day for three days to learn who should die for profaning Fosite's spring [VW 11]. And in Christian law: when a man is killed in a crowd and seven are accused, each swears; then two rods are cut, one marked with a cross, wrapped in clean wool and laid on the altar or the relics, and a priest or an innocent boy takes one up. If it is the crossed one, the seven are clear. If not, each man marks a rod with his own sign, they are wrapped and drawn one by one, and the last man's rod names the killer [LF XIV].
- **Saxons and Franks:** lot-casters forbidden [Ind 14; CPS 23], which means used.
- **Wends:** the priests of Riedegost dig lots from the earth, cover them with turf, and check the answer by the horse and the spears; both must agree [Thiet VI.24].
- **Rus:** the lot picks the victim [PVL 983].
- **What the lots looked like:** marked slips of wood. The Frisian law calls them *teni* (twigs). That is all that is known. Tacitus's account (fruit-tree twigs thrown on a white cloth) is seven hundred years too early to quote as practice.

**Omens.** From birds, from horses and from cattle dung, from sneezing, from what is met first on setting out [Ind 13, 17]. From a holy horse's step [Thiet]. From a spring's surface [Thiet I.3]. From the moon's eclipse, at which people shout to help her [Ind 21]. From the sky: the Chronicle's own 793 has whirlwinds, lightning and "fiery dragons" before Lindisfarne, and Christians read those signs as readily as anyone. Dreams matter to both sides: Anskar's life is steered by them [Rim].

**Oaths.**

- The Danish army in 876 swore to King Alfred "on the holy ring", which the Chronicle says they would not do before to any nation [ASC 876; the Everyman text has "bracelet" in one version and "ring" in the other].
- Rus swear on their weapons, laid down, and by Perun, with gold rings taken off (?) [PVL 945].
- Liutizi seal a peace by cutting hair from the crown of the head, with a handful of grass, and giving right hands [Thiet VI.25].
- Christians swear on relics or the altar, with oath-helpers (see 10).

**May show.** The `ask_omen` act as a lot: a question that can be answered yes or no, three slips, a cloth, an old man or woman of the place who lifts one, and a fee or gift. The answer as a word, never a prophecy. A skipper who will not sail after a bad first meeting on the quay. A Frisian court where the lot lies on the altar wrapped in wool, and a boy is fetched to draw. A mixed crew in which the heathen wants the lot and the Christian says a psalm, and both then feel better. A Dane asked to swear on a ring and treating it as the gravest thing he owns.

**Must not.** Runes cast and read one by one for their "meanings" (a twentieth-century practice; no source of the period has it); rune-stones in a bag; any spoken prophecy that comes true in detail; the *völva* of the sagas chanting on a high seat with the future laid out (see 7); tarot-like wording; a lot that gives a number or a date; "the Norns".

## 6. Holy places

**What is known.**

- **Open-air first:** a grove (*nimidas*, *lund*), a single tree, a spring, a stone, a hill, a lake, an island [Ind 6, 7, 11; CPS 21; PN; Adam IV.18; Thiet I.3]. A fence or rope marks the holy ground (*vi*).
- **The lord's own hall and yard second:** rites were held where the feasts were held, in the great man's house, with a small fenced building beside it at some places (Tissø, Uppåkra, Borg). The word *hof* in names seems to mean such a farm.
- **Huts and "shrines":** small buildings, *casulae* and *fana*, among Franks, Saxons and Frisians, worth robbing for what was kept in them [Ind 4; LF; VL].
- **Real temples with images and priests** are attested for the Wends [Thiet] and claimed for Uppsala [Adam].
- **Rules of the place:** silence at Fosite's spring and its cattle untouchable [VW 10]; no stranger in a Prussian grove [Adam IV.18]; no weapons, or peace-bands on them, at holy places and assemblies (law texts, late (?)); nothing cut or taken.
- **What happens to them:** the church is built on the spot or beside it (Frösö; Old Uppsala; Pope Gregory's advice to the English in 601 to keep the building and change the use, known to every missionary through Bede).

**May show.** A site of kind `grove`, `shrine`, `well` or `mound` as a plain place with one rule that binds the player (be silent, cut nothing, leave a share, do not come armed, do not come at all). An `offer` act there: bread, ale, a coin, a bit of the cargo, and the townsfolk's regard goes up while a Christian crewman's goes down. A hall where the feast and the rite are the same evening. A church standing where everyone knows what stood before, with the old well still used. A Wendish temple seen from outside the third gate, which is as far as a stranger goes.

**Must not.** Stone temples; stone circles or dolmens in use (they were already old beyond memory); a Scandinavian "temple" as a building apart with a congregation; stave churches before the eleventh century; idols of gold; sacred oaks everywhere (the oak is Geismar's, in Hesse, and Prove's, late); Yggdrasil as a thing people point to.

## 7. Who leads the rites

- **Among Scandinavians, the man of the house, the chief and the king.** There was no priesthood. *Goði* on three Funen stones is a title of a local leader with a duty to a holy place or a people; one of them is also called *þulr*, a speaker or reciter [Run]. Adam's "priests assigned to each god" at Uppsala are his guess or his informant's.
- **Women who see.** Graves of rich women with an iron staff, odd amulets and, at Fyrkat about 980, henbane seeds and a box of owl pellets (?) [Arch]. Skalds of the tenth century know the word *seiðr* for a kind of sorcery. That such women went from farm to farm and were paid for telling the season to come is saga (late) but fits. English and Frankish law fears witches and "women who command the moon" [Ind 30; Laws].
- **Among the Wends, real priests,** who keep the temple, cast the lots, guard the banners and can overrule a prince [Thiet; and Helmold, late].
- **Among the Balts,** diviners in the household [Adam]; one named "priest of idols", Sicco [Can].
- **Among Frisians and Saxons,** nobody is named. The guardian of the Walcheren idol is called only its keeper [VW 14].
- **May show:** a householder cutting the beast's throat himself; a chief whose standing rests on giving the feast; an old woman with a staff who is fed at the best seat and feared; a Wendish priest as a man of power with a say in whether you trade.
- **Must not:** "druids"; a robed priest of Odin; "shaman" as a word; a priestess with a temple; the seeress as a comic fortune-teller.

## 8. The dead

**What is known, by people.**

- **Christians everywhere:** washed, wrapped, laid on the back with the head to the west, without goods, in consecrated ground, with Masses and alms for the soul on the third, seventh and thirtieth day and at the year (?). Burning the body is a capital crime in Saxony [CPS 7], and so is burial in the old mounds [CPS 22]. In practice the first Christian generations still put a knife, a coin or a brooch in the grave.
- **Frisians and Saxons before baptism:** burned or buried, both, often side by side in one field, with horse graves among the Saxons (?); feasts and songs over the dead that the church called *dadsisas* [Ind 2].
- **Danes:** mostly buried unburnt in the ninth and tenth centuries, simply; the great in a timber chamber with weapons, a horse, a wagon-body for a woman's coffin; a ship at Ladby (about 925) and Hedeby (about 850). Stones set in the outline of a ship. At Mammen (winter 970-971) a great man lies in a chamber with an axe inlaid with silver and a large wax candle.
- **Northmen of Norway:** burned or unburnt, in boats large and small under mounds: Oseberg (834), two women, with a wagon, sledges, beds, kitchen gear, fifteen horses; Gokstad (about 900).
- **Svear:** burned on a pyre with dogs, horses and goods, the ashes under a low mound with a stone on it; at Birka also rich unburnt chamber graves, some with folding scales and weights: the merchant buried with his trade.
- **Wends:** burned, the ashes left on or in a low mound; unburnt graves spread from the tenth century (?).
- **Prussians:** the dead man lies unburnt in his house a month or two, a king half a year, while kin and friends drink and play; they know a way to keep the body cold. On the day of burning, what is left of his goods is laid in five or six heaps, the largest a mile from the town, the smallest nearest, and men race for them on the fastest horses; then he is burned with weapons and clothes, and every bone must be burned or the people pay a great price [Wulf].
- **Rus on the Volga:** the chief's ship-burning [IbnF].

**Where the dead go.** Honestly unknown. Court poets send kings to Odin's hall [Sk]. A father says the sea has his son [Sk]. A mound is treated as a house: goods, food, sometimes a door. Churchmen forbid making "saints" of the dead and eating at graves [Ind 1, 2, 25]. Runestones before the conversion say who raised the stone and who inherits, and nothing about a next world.

**May show.** The `bury` and `remember` acts in each custom: a Mass bought and alms given at a church; a share of the dead man's goods burned or laid with him among heathens, and the crew quarrelling over which is right for a man who wore both hammer and cross. A Prussian horse-race for a dead man's goods that a stranger with a fast horse may or may not enter. A pyre seen across the water at night. A mound by the harbour mouth that pilots steer by, with a name. A baptised widow who wants her heathen husband moved to the churchyard.

**Must not.** A burning ship pushed out to sea with a flaming arrow (no evidence at all; ships were burned or buried on land); coins on the eyes; "Hel-shoes" (one saga, late); walking dead as an event the card vouches for (a crewman may fear them); Valhalla promised to a drowning trader; a Christian heaven-and-hell speech from a heathen; widows burned as a rule (two Arab reports of the east, and a few double graves).

## 9. Sea customs

**The first thing to say is how little there is.** Sailors' beliefs were not written down. Most "old sea lore" is of the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries. What can be stood on:

**Heathen.**

- **The sea takes men, and has a name for it:** Rán, and her husband the sea-god, in a poem of about 960 by a man whose son drowned off Iceland [Sk]. The net she catches men in is from later books.
- **Thor is the god in the boat:** the fishing of the world-serpent is carved in three countries in the tenth and eleventh centuries [Pic]. And a late but often-told line has a settler who was Christian at home and called on Thor at sea (Helgi the Lean in the Book of Settlements, late).
- **Lots and vows before sailing and in danger** [Rim 19, 27, 30]; the old man at Birka says the Christian god has helped many of them in peril at sea [Rim 27]. Gods were chosen for results.
- **A trader's offering for a good market** on landing [IbnF].
- **Beast-heads on the stems** (Oseberg's serpent prow, 834; ships with heads on coins of Hedeby about 825 and in the Bayeux embroidery about 1075). That heathen law told men to take them down in sight of land, so as not to frighten the land-spirits, stands in one Icelandic book of the thirteenth century (late).
- **The ship as the vessel of the dead:** boat graves, stone ships, the sailing ship on Gotland's stones.
- **The shore as a place between:** the Frisian shrine-robber is offered on the sand the tide covers [LF]; al-Tartushi's unwanted infants go into the sea [Tar].
- **A polemic to treat with care:** Dudo of Saint-Quentin, about 1015, says the Northmen of old chose a man by lot before sailing and killed him to Thor for a fair wind [Dudo (?)]. He is writing the Normans' dark past for their Christian duke.

**Christian.**

- **Prayer and vow in a storm:** promise a candle, alms, a fast, a pilgrimage, a gift to a named saint's house if the ship comes home. Miracle books of every abbey on the coast hold such stories (St Cuthbert, St Philibert, St Bertin, St Willibrord) (?).
- **Relics and holy water aboard,** when a churchman travels; the sign of the cross over the bow; a Mass heard and alms given before leaving (`hear_mass`, `alms`).
- **Saints for the sea in these years:** Peter the fisherman; Martin (Utrecht's patron, and the day, 11 November, that ends the season); Clement, drowned with an anchor, whose churches stand at Danish and Norwegian harbours from about 1000-1050 (Aarhus, Roskilde, Oslo, Trondheim; St Clement Danes in London); Mary as "star of the sea" (the hymn *Ave maris stella* is in a ninth-century book); Cuthbert on the Northumbrian coast; Brendan as a story (the *Voyage* was written by about 900 (?)); Olaf from 1031.
- **The bishop who cleanses the sea:** Reinbern at Kolberg throws four anointed stones into it and pours holy water, about 1000 [Thiet].
- **Fast days hold at sea:** no meat on Fridays and in Lent; fish, bread, ale. A mixed crew eats at two rules.
- **Weather-makers:** about 815 Bishop Agobard of Lyon writes against the common belief that certain people (*tempestarii*) can raise hail and storm for pay, and that ships come through the clouds from a land called Magonia to carry off the beaten-down crops [Agob]. Churchmen forbid horn-blowing against storms [Ind 22]. Burchard still asks about weather-makers in 1010 [Bur].

**May show.** A skipper who does the same four things before every crossing and cannot say why. A vow made aloud in bad weather as a card choice with a cost due on landing (`vow`), and a second card if it is not paid. Ravn naming nobody and pouring a little ale over the side. A Christian crewman signing the bow. A man lost overboard and each of the crew saying what took him. A wax candle the length of the boat's keel promised to St Martin (candles "of a man's measure" are a miracle-book habit (?)). A stem-head unshipped before a strange coast, done as a custom, with the reason given only as "it is done".

**Must not.** St Nicholas as the sailor's saint before about 1090 (his cult spreads through the north after his bones came to Bari in 1087; a few German and English churches knew him earlier); St Elmo's fire by that name; whistling for wind; Friday as an unlucky sailing day; women or priests as bad luck aboard; a coin under the mast as a known custom; Finns or witches selling wind in knotted cords (thirteenth century and after); mermaids, the kraken, Davy Jones; "crossing the bar"; a ship launched over bound captives (*hlunnroð*, a word in late sagas); christening a ship with drink.

## 10. Christian practice as lived

The Christian half of the chart is where the player starts, and the one writers get wrong by making it modern. Seven things.

### 10.1 Relics

- **What they are:** the body of a saint, or a bone, a tooth, hair, cloth that touched the tomb, dust from it, oil from its lamp. The saint is held to be present in them and to own property through them: land is given "to St Martin", and he punishes those who take it.
- **Every altar has them.** A church cannot be consecrated without relics (required at the council of Nicaea in 787 and by Frankish councils after (?)).
- **They move.** Saxony had no saints, so bodies were brought in with processions: Vitus to Corvey and Liborius to Paderborn in 836, Alexander to Wildeshausen in 851 [TSA; Wid I.34]. Einhard had Marcellinus and Peter stolen from Rome in 827 and carried down the Rhine, and wrote the story himself [Einh]. A stolen saint who works miracles in his new home has shown that he wished to go: theft is a proof, if it succeeds.
- **They flee.** The monks of Noirmoutier carried St Philibert inland by stages from 836 to 875; the community of St Cuthbert left Lindisfarne with his coffin in 875, came to Chester-le-Street in 883 and Durham in 995 [HSC (?)].
- **Enemies become devotees.** Danes killed King Edmund of East Anglia in 869; within thirty years Danish-ruled East Anglia struck pennies in his name as a saint [Coin]. Danes killed Archbishop Ælfheah in 1012; King Cnut carried his body to Canterbury in 1023 [ASC]. King Olaf fell in 1030 and was a saint in 1031.
- **Oaths are sworn on them** (see 10.5), and they are carried to assemblies and round the fields.
- **Merchants deal in them** whether they should or not: Italian relic-sellers are named in the ninth century (the deacon Deusdona, Einhard's supplier) [Einh].
- **May show:** a relic as cargo that changes how every Christian port treats the boat, and that must not get wet, be sold, or be shown to the wrong bishop; a small bag of dust from Willibrord's tomb round a crewman's neck; a shrine with crutches, wax limbs and broken fetters hung by it (freed captives' chains are St Faith's and St Leonard's custom in the south; wax and wooden limbs are in the Indiculus as a heathen habit and in every later shrine as a Christian one); a saint's feast as the day of the fair.
- **Must not:** the Three Kings at Cologne (1164); the Holy Grail; a Turin-style shroud; papal canonisation (the first is 993, and local bishops made saints through the whole period); a sceptical character who says relics are fakes in a modern voice (the period's doubt is "is this really *his* bone?", and it is settled by fire or by miracle); splinters of the Cross as common trade goods; St James's scallop shell badge (twelfth century).

### 10.2 Vows

- **A promise to God or a saint, with a price:** a pilgrimage, a fast, a gift of wax or silver or land, a child given to a monastery (an oblate, like Beornwine in the spine), a church or a bridge built. Made in sickness, storm, battle, barrenness. Binding: a broken vow is a sin needing penance, and the saint is believed to collect.
- **Where pilgrims went:** to the nearest saint first. To Rome all through the period, from England above all: kings Alfred as a boy (853), Cnut for the emperor's crowning (1027), after which he wrote home that he had got the tolls on English and Danish merchants and pilgrims lowered [Cnut 1027]. To Compostela from the tenth century (the tomb was found about 820-830; the first pilgrim from beyond the Pyrenees who is named is a bishop of Le Puy in 950). To Jerusalem rarely before 1000, more often after the land road through Hungary opened: Earl Swein Godwinson barefoot in 1052, Bishop Ealdred of Worcester in 1058 [ASC], a great German company in 1064-65.
- **Heathens vow too** [Rim 19, 30], and the Svear before the Curonian fort vowed a fast to Christ and alms to the poor, as merchants among them advised [Rim 30].
- **May show:** `vow` as a real contract with a due date; a pilgrim as a paying passenger with a staff and a letter from his bishop, who must not be robbed and brings luck or trouble; a merchant paying for a share in a church bell (a `work`); a vowed fast aboard.
- **Must not:** crusade vows or the taking of the cross (1095); indulgences sold; palmers with shell badges; a vow dissolved by paying a fee to "the Church" as a routine.

### 10.3 Penance

- **How it worked:** a sinner confessed to a priest, who looked up the sin in a handbook (a *penitential*) and set a term of fasting: so many days, Lents or years on bread, water and salt, with no meat, no wine, no bed with one's wife. The books of the age: Theodore's and Egbert's in England, Halitgar's (about 830) and Regino's (about 906) in Francia, Burchard of Worms's "Corrector" (about 1008-1012).
- **The scale** (it varies book to book): killing in anger, several years; killing in a public war at a lord's order, forty days; perjury, years; theft, restitution and a term; work on Sunday, days; eating meat that was offered to idols, a term; consulting a diviner, a year or more (?).
- **Buying it off (commutation):** a year's fast could be turned into psalms sung, blows taken, alms given, a sum of silver paid, or other men hired to fast for you. Churchmen complained and it went on. For a rich merchant, penance is a price list.
- **Public penance** for public scandal: put out of church on Ash Wednesday in sackcloth, taken back on Maundy Thursday; in the meantime no arms, no lawsuits, and (in the stricter books) no trading (?).
- **What priests asked about.** Burchard's questions are a second Indiculus, for the Rhineland about 1010: have you kept the first of January in the heathen way; tied knots or said spells over bread, herbs or bindings; believed that women ride at night with a crowd of spirits; that there are three sisters who fix a child's fate, and laid a table for them; that a man can turn wolf; that there are weather-makers; gathered herbs with any words but the Creed and the Lord's Prayer [Bur].
- **Fasts everyone kept:** Lent (forty days before Easter: one meal a day after the ninth hour or at evening, no meat, and in the stricter rule no eggs or cheese); the Ember days four times a year; Fridays; the eves of great feasts. Fish, eels, bread, pulses, ale. In Saxony eating meat in Lent out of contempt was a capital crime in 785 [CPS 4].
- **May show:** a priest with a small book who names a term and then, lower, a price; a crew whose provisions change in Lent (`provision`); a man doing seven years' fast for a killing, who trades thin and cannot bear arms; a hired faster; a merchant whose penance for Sunday trading is alms at the next port.
- **Must not:** a confessional box (sixteenth century); the rosary; a duty to confess once a year (1215; in these years it is urged before Lent and often not done); absolution first and penance after as the fixed order (the older way is penance first); "Hail Marys" set as a penance; a kindly modern talk in place of a tariff.

### 10.4 Sanctuary

- **The rule:** a man who reaches a church, or its door, or its yard, may not be dragged out or killed. Charlemagne's Saxon law, harsh in everything else, opens with it: he keeps life and limb and answers for the deed by payment [CPS 2]. English kings set terms: under Ine his life is spared and he pays as the law directs; under Alfred he has a set number of nights (three at a small church, seven at a greater (?)) in which his kin may arrange the payment, and whoever harms him there pays the king and the church; under Æthelred (1014) churches are ranked, and breaking the peace of a head minster costs most [Laws]. Some northern churches had a stone seat at the altar that was the heart of the peace (Hexham, Beverley; the wide peace claimed for them is in later charters (?)).
- **What it is for:** time. It stops the first blow of a feud and lets silver do the work. It does not wipe out a debt, a theft or a blood-price.
- **Heathens did not respect it,** and that is half of why 793 shocked: Alcuin's letters turn on a holy place that could not protect itself.
- **Holy peace on the other side:** a market or an assembly is a peace counted in days; holy ground forbids bloodshed (Fosite's island; the Prussian groves).
- **May show:** the `sanctuary` act: the player's man, or a debtor of his, sits in a church while the count's men wait outside and someone goes for the kin and the silver; the days running; food handed in or not. A slave who has run to the altar and the priest bargaining for him. A heathen crewman who cannot see why the armed men stop at a door.
- **Must not:** forty days and then "abjuring the realm" barefoot to a port (English practice of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries); a sanctuary knocker as the thing to grasp (Durham's is twelfth century); sanctuary as a way out of debt; a church that shelters a heathen raider.

### 10.5 The oath and the ordeal

- **First the oath.** A free man of good name answers a charge by swearing it false, with a fixed number of oath-helpers of his own rank who swear that his oath is clean. They swear on relics or the altar; in Frisian law a small matter may be sworn on one's own cloak or coin instead (?) [LF]. A false oath is a sin and a crime, and loses a man his "oath-worthiness" for good.
- **Then the ordeal,** for those whose oath is not enough: the man caught before, the man of ill fame, the slave, the man without kin, and **the stranger, who has no oath-helpers in this town.** A foreign merchant is exactly the person who ends up at the iron.
- **The forms:**
  - **Hot iron:** carry a glowing bar a set distance (nine feet in English law). The hand is bound and sealed, and opened on the third day: clean or festering [Laws II Æthelstan 23, about 930].
  - **Hot water:** take a stone or ring from a boiling cauldron, to the wrist or the elbow; bound and judged the same way.
  - **Cold water:** bound and let down on a rope into blessed water; the water takes the innocent and refuses the guilty, who floats. New in the ninth century; an emperor banned it in 829 and Archbishop Hincmar defended it in 860; it went on (?).
  - **The cross:** both parties stand with arms out before a cross; the first to drop them loses. Banned by Louis the Pious about 818.
  - **The blessed morsel** (English *corsnæd*): a piece of barley bread and cheese with a curse said over it; the guilty man chokes. Chiefly for clergy [Laws].
  - **Battle:** the two parties, or hired champions, fight. Frankish, Frisian (the law prices a hired champion), Saxon, Lombard, Norman. **Not English before 1066.**
- **How it is done:** in or by the church, run by the priest, who is paid. Three days' fast on bread, water, salt and herbs; Mass; the accused takes communion and swears; the iron or the water is blessed; the witnesses of both sides stand in two rows, fasting, and no one speaks.
- **The ordeal that changed a kingdom:** about 965 the priest Poppo carried a great weight of hot iron before King Harald of the Danes, showed his hand unhurt, and the king ordered Christ alone to be honoured [Wid III.65].
- **Heathen proofs:** the oath on the ring; duels are likely but only later law and saga describe them.
- **May show:** a `lawsuit` in a foreign port where the player has no kin: find oath-helpers (men who know him, a guild-brother after 1000, a host), or pay, or go to the iron. The three days of fasting as days lost. The crowd. The priest's fee. The result given as a fact with no word on whether God did it. A Frisian who hires a champion. A heathen offered the iron as the price of trading in a Christian port (after Poppo, a Dane would know the story).
- **Must not:** ducking witches in a pond (early modern); trial by jury; lawyers; torture to get a confession (not Frankish or English practice for free men in these years); *holmgang* on an islet with a marked cloak, or passing under a strip of raised turf (saga, late), as things the card vouches for; an ordeal treated as a joke by the people in it.

### 10.6 Baptism, Mass and the ordinary week

- **Baptism:** by rights at the vigils of Easter and Pentecost, in a font or a tub, with the renouncing (the Saxon vow is the form), oil, and a white garment and headcloth worn for a week. Godparents become kin: a king who stands sponsor to a beaten enemy makes him a son (Alfred and Guthrum, 878, with the "loosing of the chrism" eight days later [ASC]). Infants within thirty days in Wessex, within the year in Saxony [Laws Ine 2; CPS 19].
- **The sign of the cross without baptism** (*prima signatio*): a heathen is marked as a learner, may enter church and eat and trade with Christians, and puts off the font until he is dying, since baptism washes all [Rim 24]. This is the commonest state of a northern merchant in a Christian port.
- **Baptism for the gifts:** Notker of St Gall, about 885, tells of Northmen who came each Easter to the emperor Louis to be baptised for the white robes and the weapons given them, until one year the robes ran out and were pieced from old cloth, and an old man said he had been washed here twenty times and always given good clothes, and this sack was fit for a swineherd [Notk II.19; the wording to be taken from Grant 1907 before it is quoted]. It is a monk's joke, and the period's own.
- **Mass:** in Latin, the priest's back to the people, short. Lay people take communion seldom (three times a year is urged). Sunday: no ploughing, no court, no market in law [capitularies; Laws Edward and Guthrum 7]; markets were held anyway and moved to Saturday by and by.
- **What a layman knows:** the Lord's Prayer and the Creed in his own tongue (required of godparents by Charlemagne), the sign of the cross, a few saints and their days, when to fast, what he owes. Not the Bible.
- **What he owes:** the tithe (a tenth of the year's increase; in law in Francia from 779, in Saxony from 785, in England by the tenth century); burial fee; candles; in England a penny a hearth to Rome.
- **Churches:** of wood nearly everywhere north of the Rhine mouth; stone at the great minsters and old Roman towns. A bell, an altar with relics, a font, one or two books, a lamp. Standing room; no seats.
- **Must not:** pews, pulpits, stained-glass stories, the elevated host and a bell at it (thirteenth century), church weddings as a requirement (marriage is made by the families; a blessing is extra), the word "parish" for the north before the eleventh century, married priests as a scandal (they are ordinary until the reforms after 1050), friars (1200s), the Devil with horns and a pitchfork.

### 10.7 Mixed belief

This is the normal state of most people on the chart for most of the game, and the richest ground for cards.

- **Both signs at once:** the cross-and-hammer mould; graves with both; hammers cut on stones that also bear crosses (?).
- **Christ as one god more:** the Danes of about 965 [Wid III.65]; the Birka assembly weighing him by results [Rim 27]; the Svear's lot in Courland [Rim 30]; Helgi the Lean (late).
- **A king buried both ways:** Hákon the Good of Norway, raised a Christian in England, laid in a mound in 961 and sent to Odin by his poet [Sk]. Rollo of Normandy, of whom a monk wrote about 1030 that on his deathbed he had Christian captives killed for his gods and gave gold to the churches (Adémar, hostile (?)).
- **Old charms with new names:** the English field-blessing that calls on "Erce, mother of earth" and has four Masses sung over four sods; the Nine Herbs Charm in which Woden strikes the adder with nine twigs, in a book of prayers; the Merseburg charms on a blank leaf of a Mass-book [Charms; Mers]. A priest wrote each of these down.
- **Christians at the old places:** offerings at wells and trees forbidden in every law from 743 to 1020, which means done for three hundred years [Ind; CPS 21; Bur; Laws II Cnut 5].
- **Offerings to saints as to gods** [Ind 9], and wooden limbs hung up for healing [Ind 29].
- **The law that made room:** Iceland in 1000: all to be baptised, but sacrifice in secret, the eating of horse and the exposing of infants allowed for a time [Ari].
- **The gospel in the old key:** the Saxon *Heliand* (about 830) makes Christ a lord, the apostles his sworn men, and the towns hill-forts [Hel].
- **Stones in a Christian yard with the old stories:** the Gosforth cross (tenth century) has a bound figure with a woman holding a bowl over him, a god with a horn, and a crucifixion, on one shaft.
- **May show:** nearly anything, if it is small and done without comment. The brief's own picture is right: a hammer under a shirt next to a cross. A baptised Frisian who still will not draw Fosite's water noisily. A priest who blesses the plough with words his bishop would not like. A Dane with the cross signed on him who goes to church for the market's sake and to the feast for his mother's. A merchant who gives to both because both have been right before.
- **Must not:** anyone explaining it as "hedging my bets" with a wink; a secret pagan resistance; a wise heathen and a foolish priest, or the reverse; tolerance praised in a modern voice. People did it and did not have a theory about it.

## 11. The year, in one table

Feasts a person may date by. Heathen feasts are thin: say the season, not a named festival, unless it is in this table.

| Season | Christian | Old custom, attested | Not to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Midwinter | Christmas, twelve days; Candlemas 2 Feb | *Yule*, drunk as a feast, with ale [Sk; Gulathing law, late]; the great offerings at Lejre in January [Thiet, hearsay] | Yule log, tree, mistletoe, "Mother's Night" outside England |
| Late winter | Lent begins; in Francia the fast itself | *Spurcalia* in February [Ind 3]; the Svear's assembly and market at Uppsala (*Disting*, name late) | Imbolc and other Irish feasts moved north |
| Spring | Easter (the year's hinge; debts and oaths fall due); Rogation days, with processions round the fields | Uppsala's nine-year feast "about the spring equinox" [Adam, note]; images carried round the fields [Ind 28] | Ostara, eggs and hares |
| Summer | Pentecost; John's Mass 24 June; Peter and Paul 29 June | assemblies and fairs | Midsummer bonfires as a pagan rite (they are St John's fires, and Christian in the sources (?)) |
| Harvest | Mary's Mass 15 Aug; Michaelmas 29 Sept | the Wends' harvest feast (late: Arkona) | "Mabon", "Lammas rites" |
| Autumn | Martinmas 11 Nov: slaughter, dues paid, ships laid up | the elves' offering at the farm [Sk, 1019]; "winter nights" (name late) | Samhain or Hallowe'en |

## 12. The short list of things that are later invention

One place to look before a card goes in.

- **Books too late to use as fact:** Snorri's Edda and Heimskringla (about 1220-30); the eddic poems as written (about 1270); every saga; Saxo (about 1200); Helmold (about 1170) for anything before 1066; Peter of Dusburg (1326); Simon Grunau (about 1520); Olaus Magnus (1555).
- **Scholars' inventions:** Ostara, Krodo, Stavo, Fosta, Belbog, Lada, Romuva and Criwe, a Frisian or Frankish "pantheon", the Irminsul as a world-tree, the Oera Linda Book, the Prillwitz idols, the Book of Veles.
- **Modern images:** horned and winged helmets; the blood eagle; skull cups; the flaming funeral ship at sea; rune "meanings" and rune-casting; the valknut as a named, explained sign (the name is modern); "Viking" as a people; "Asatru"; "shaman", "pagan pride", "the Old Religion"; Thor as a red-bearded comic strongman; Odin with one eye in a traveller's hat, as a thing a ninth-century Dane describes.
- **Christian anachronisms:** confession boxes, rosaries, pews, crusades, indulgences for sale, friars, the Inquisition, witch-burnings by the church (in 785 the law kills the man who burns a witch [CPS 6]), papal canonisation as the rule, St Nicholas for sailors, the Three Kings at Cologne, clerical celibacy as settled fact, the horned Devil.

Cards the material offers at once (ideas only; the card writers own the cards):

1. The lot at Birka: the town will not let a Christian-owned boat unload until the lot is asked. Options: wait for it, give to the feast, sail on. (829-865.) [Rim 26-27]
2. Catla's alms: a Birka woman wants passage to Dorestad with her dead mother's silver for the poor. (About 845-860.) [Rim 20]
3. The shut door: an autumn night in Götaland and no farm will take the crew in. (Any year to about 1030.) [Sk]
4. The mould: a smith at Hedeby or in north Jutland offers a cross or a hammer, same price, and asks which shore the buyer is bound for. (About 930-1000.) [Am]
5. The iron: accused of short weight in a port where no one will swear for the player. (Any Christian port.) [Laws]
6. The silent spring: a watering stop on Heligoland with a rule, and a thirsty, talkative crewman. (To about 800; after that as memory.) [VW 10]
7. The bell: Hedeby's Christians have a bell and may not ring it; a Frisian merchant is asked to carry a gift to the king about it. (About 850-860.) [Rim 32]
8. The twentieth baptism: an old Dane at a Frankish court at Easter who wants a better robe. (About 820-840.) [Notk II.19]
9. The saint in the hold: monks need a coffin carried by night. (Noirmoutier 836; Lindisfarne 875; Saxony 836, 851.) [Einh; TSA]
10. The horse-race: a rich Prussian has lain dead two months and tomorrow his goods are raced for. (Truso, any year.) [Wulf]
11. The unblemished slave: a buyer from the east looks a man over too carefully. (Baltic, any year.) [Adam IV.17]
12. The bishop's head: news on the quays in the winter of 1066. [Adam III.51]
