# Lore: books and texts a merchant could carry, 793 to 1066

Written 6 October 2026 for the chart game. Two parts: what was written, read, carried and sung in the North Sea
world (sections 1 to 8), and forty candidates for the game's library that are not yet in
`Assets/Kapman/Game/Data/texts_793.json` (section 9; the same forty as data in `texts_more.json`).

How to read the marks. A fact with a source in brackets was checked against that source this session. A fact marked
**(?)** is from memory or from one secondary page and was not checked. The forty candidates were each opened in the
printed translation named (a scan's text or a Project Gutenberg transcript) and the first words copied from it, except
where the entry says otherwise.

What is already in the chest, so nothing here repeats it (310 entries): Beowulf in 43 fitts (Hall 1892), the
Seafarer, the Wanderer, Deor, Maldon, 27 stanzas of Hávamál (1 to 81), Vafthruthnir's and Alvis's lays, Gestumblindi's
riddles, 31 Exeter riddles, Latin riddles of Symphosius, Aldhelm, Tatwine and Eusebius, twelve skaldic verses, Ohthere
and Wulfstan, Dicuil, Adam of Bremen's sailing times (Latin only), Landnámabók's sailing directions, Ælfric's
merchant, the Saxon baptismal vow, Alcuin to King Æthelred (793), the Chronicle for 793, and the Irish quatrain
"Bitter is the wind tonight".

---

## 1. The short version, for a writer in a hurry

- **A book is treasure.** One large Bible took the skins of about 515 calves. A single good book was once traded for
  eight hides of land. The fittings on a king's gift-book were worth 1,500 pennies, three times the player's debt.
- **Books belong to churches, not to people.** A merchant does not own books. He **carries** them: on loan between
  abbeys, as a gift from a king, as a dead man's bequest, as loot bought back from raiders.
- **Almost nobody reads.** Clergy read Latin. A few great laymen read; fewer write. Charlemagne never learned to form
  his letters. A merchant counts on a tally stick, weighs silver, and has his letters read to him.
- **Everybody listens.** Laws are read out at the moot. Songs go round the table with the harp. News comes with the
  ship. A text in the game is more often **heard** than held.
- **Runes are the layman's letters.** In Frisia, Denmark, Sweden and Norway a man can cut his name, a label or a
  message on a stick of yew or a rib of whalebone. That is a letter a merchant can read himself.
- **From 793 the libraries are in the raiders' path.** Lindisfarne 793, Jarrow 794, Iona 795 to 806, York 866,
  Corbie 881, St Gall 926. A book moved in time is a book saved, and that is work for a ship.

## 2. The libraries

Each one with what a skipper would find, the years it matters, and the nearest port in `ports_793.json`.

### York (`eoforwic`)

- The best library north of the Alps in the 770s. Archbishop Ælberht (767 to 778) travelled abroad more than once
  to buy books and left the collection to his pupil Alcuin (Alcuin's poem on the bishops, kings and saints of York,
  lines c. 1530 to 1560, names some forty authors: Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, Bede, Aldhelm, Virgil, Lucan, Pliny,
  Cicero, Aristotle, Boethius and others) (?).
- In 796 or 797 Alcuin, now abbot of Tours, asked Charlemagne for leave "to send some of our young men to pick out
  what I need, and bring to France the flowers of Britain; that not in York only there may be a garden enclosed, but
  in Tours also" (Browne 1908, ch. on the school of Tours; candidate `alcuin_flowers_of_britain`). So in the game's
  first years books cross from the Humber to the Loire by ship.
- Liudger, the Frisian who became first bishop of Münster, studied at York under Alcuin and had to go home in a hurry
  about 773 when a Frisian merchant killed the son of a local earl and every Frisian had to leave the city
  (Altfrid, Life of Liudger I.11 to 12) (?). A Frisian colony of traders at York twenty years before the game
  starts is as good a tie between Dorestad and York as the sources give.
- In 852 Abbot Lupus of Ferrières wrote to Abbot Altsig at York asking for the loan of Quintilian, Jerome and Bede
  to copy (Lupus, letter 62 in some numberings) (?). So the library was still worth writing to in 852.
- The Danes took York in November 866 and held it in 867 (Chronicle). Nothing is heard of the library after that.
  In the game: full until 866, a few rescued books after.

### Wearmouth and Jarrow (`gyrwe`)

- Twin houses founded 674 and 681 or 682 by Benedict Biscop, who went to Rome five or six times and each time
  "brought back a large number of books" (Bede, Lives of the Abbots, tr. Giles; candidate `bede_abbots_books`).
  He ordered on his death-bed that the library "be kept entire, and neither by neglect to be injured or dispersed".
- Abbot Ceolfrith doubled it and had three complete Bibles (pandects) written. One, sent to Rome in 716, survives:
  the Codex Amiatinus, 1,030 or 1,040 leaves, 49 by 34 cm, 18 cm thick, over 34 kg; "the 1030 leaves of the Codex
  Amiatinus would have utilized the skins of 515 cattle" (Christopher de Hamel, quoted in Wikipedia, "Codex
  Amiatinus"). Three of them is over 1,500 calves, which needs the grant of land the house in fact got.
- Bede (died 735) wrote here, with perhaps 200 to 250 works to hand (?). After his death the house could not copy
  his books fast enough for the demand from the Continent: in 764 Abbot Cuthbert wrote to Bishop Lul of Mainz that
  the hard winter had held back the scribes' hands, sent Bede's lives of St Cuthbert, and asked for a glass-maker and
  a harper (?).
- The Chronicle (MS D, E) says the heathen plundered "Ecgferth's minster at the mouth of the Don" in 794, usually
  taken to be Jarrow (?). In the game: rich until 794, poor and nervous after, gone as a house of learning by about
  860 (?).

### Lindisfarne (`lindisfarena`)

- The Lindisfarne Gospels were written about 715 to 720 by Eadfrith, bishop 698 to 721, bound by Æthelwald and
  given a jewelled cover by the anchorite Billfrith; so says the note added about 950 to 970 by the priest Aldred,
  who wrote an English gloss between the lines at Chester-le-Street (Wikipedia, "Lindisfarne Gospels") (?). It has 258
  or 259 leaves of calf-skin.
- Raided 8 June 793. The Gospels and St Cuthbert's body survived. In 875 the community left the island with both and
  wandered seven years; settled at Chester-le-Street in 883 and at Durham in 995 (?).
- Alcuin's letter to Bishop Higbald and the monks (793) is candidate `alcuin_lindisfarne_793`. In 797 he told the same
  bishop to "read very often ... the book of the blessed Gregory ... on the Pastoral Care" (Browne 1908, Ep. 81): a
  book request the player could fill.
- A letter of Alcuin of 797 asks what Ingeld has to do with Christ, complaining that a harper sings heathen songs at
  a bishop's table. It used to be thought the bishop was Higbald; that is now doubted (?). Either way: heroic song
  was heard in English minsters.

### Iona (`hi`)

- Columba's house, founded 563. Adamnan's Life (c. 700) has the saint die as a scribe, stopping at the foot of a
  page of the Psalter (candidate `adamnan_last_page`). The Book of Kells was probably begun here about 800 (?).
- Raided 795, 802 and 806 (68 of the community killed). From 807 the abbot built a new house at Kells and most of
  the treasures went there; some monks stayed (Annals of Ulster) (?). A voyage from Iona to the Irish coast with
  books and relics in 806 or 807 is historical ground for a chain.

### Utrecht (`traiectum`)

- St Martin's, Willibrord's church (from 695). Under Abbot Gregory (died about 775) it had a school that drew
  Franks, English, Frisians, Saxons, Bavarians and Swabians (Liudger, Life of Gregory) (?). Liudger learned there and
  brought books back from York (?).
- Boniface's own letter-book and some of his books went to Fulda and Mainz, not Utrecht (?).
- **The Utrecht Psalter was not at Utrecht in the game's years.** It was made at Hautvillers near Reims about 820
  to 835, was in England about 1000, and reached Utrecht in 1716 (?). Do not put it in the town.
- Bishop Hunger fled the Northmen in 857, first to Sint-Odiliënberg, and the bishops sat at Deventer until Bishop
  Balderic came back about 920 (?). In the game: a working school in 793, an empty close from 857, books again
  after 920.

### Fulda (nearest ports `mogontia`, `colonia`)

- Founded 744 by Sturm for Boniface, whose body lies there (killed at Dokkum, `doccinga`, 754). Hrabanus Maurus
  taught there from about 804 and was abbot 822 to 842; the library may have reached 2,000 volumes, the largest in
  Germany (?). Einhard was schooled there.
- The Lay of Hildebrand was written about 830 on the blank outer leaves of a Fulda book by two hands (candidate
  `hildebrand`). Fulda also made an Old High German Gospel harmony (Tatian) about 830, and is one of the places
  proposed for the Heliand, the Gospel as a Saxon lord's saga (?).
- Hrabanus's short tract on the invention of letters gives a rune row, "the letters of the Marcomanni, whom we call
  Northmen" (?): monks on the Rhine took an interest in the Northmen's letters.
- Charlemagne's letter on the study of letters was addressed to Fulda's abbot Baugulf (candidate
  `charlemagne_letters_fulda`).
- Fulda is far up the Fulda river from the Main. A merchant reaches its books and its orders at Mainz.

### Corbie (nearest ports `quentovic`, `rotomagus`)

- Founded 657 to 661 by Queen Balthild on the Somme. Under Abbot Maurdramnus (772 to 781) its writing room made a
  Bible in one of the first clear Caroline minuscules, the plain round hand the game's Latin books should be drawn in
  (Wikipedia, "Corbie Abbey") (?).
- In 822 Corbie founded Corvey on the Weser in Saxony. Anskar, the missionary to Hedeby and Birka, was a monk of
  Corbie and then of Corvey; the "nearly forty books" the pirates took from him in 829 (candidate
  `rimbert_forty_books`) were gathered for the Birka mission, some surely from these houses.
- Paschasius Radbertus and Ratramnus wrote there in the 840s. The Northmen sacked Corbie in 881 (?).

### Saint-Denis (`parisius`)

- The royal abbey north of Paris, burial place of the Frankish kings. Abbot Fardulf (793 to 806) is abbot when the
  game starts (?). In 827 envoys of the emperor Michael II brought Louis the Pious a Greek book of Dionysius the
  Areopagite, which was laid in Saint-Denis; Abbot Hilduin had it turned into Latin (?).
- **The fair of Saint-Denis** (9 October and the weeks after) is the oldest named fair in the Frankish north. A
  judgment of King Pippin of 753 names Saxons and Frisians among the merchants who came to it by river (?). A
  Frisian skipper has every reason to lie below Saint-Denis in October: wine, honey and madder at the fair, and an
  abbey that buys parchment, wax and gold leaf.
- The abbey paid a huge ransom for its abbot Louis, taken by Northmen in 858 (686 pounds of gold and 3,250 of
  silver, Annals of St Bertin) (?).

### St Gall (no port; beyond Lake Constance)

- Grew from the cell of the Irishman Gall (about 612); an abbey from 719. The Plan of St Gall (about 820 to 830)
  draws the ideal abbey with the writing room below and the library above, next to the choir (?).
- Its ninth-century catalogue lists about 400 volumes (?). Notker the Stammerer (died 912), who wrote the Deeds of
  Charles (candidates `notker_*`), was its librarian for a time. Irish scholars passed through and stayed; a book of
  Latin grammar written in Ireland about 845 and brought here carries in its margins the quatrain "Bitter is the wind
  tonight" already in the chest.
- A St Gall school-book of the ninth century holds the oldest rune poem, the Abecedarium Nordmannicum: the sixteen
  Danish runes with their names, in a mix of Saxon and Norse (?).
- The Hungarians came in 926; the books had been sent to Reichenau for safety. "During a fire in 937, the Abbey was
  destroyed, but the library remained intact" (Wikipedia, "Abbey library of Saint Gall").
- For the game St Gall is a name at the far end of the Rhine: books and Irish scholars go up from Mainz, stories
  come down.

## 3. What a book cost and how it was made

**The skin.** Parchment is calf, sheep or goat skin, limed, scraped, stretched and dried. A large book takes one
skin for every two leaves (one folded sheet); a small one gets two or four sheets from a skin (?). The Codex
Amiatinus: about 515 calves (above). The Lindisfarne Gospels: perhaps 130 to 150 (?). Parchment could be scraped and
used again. **No paper north of the Alps** (BRIEF, rule 2).

**The ink and the colours.** Iron-gall ink from oak galls, copperas and gum; or lamp-black. Red lead and vermilion
for headings. For a great book: orpiment (yellow), verdigris (green), woad or indigo (blue), lichen purple, and
rarely lapis lazuli and gold leaf (?). Boniface wanted the Epistles of Peter written in gold so that the heathen
would honour them by sight (candidate `boniface_gold_letters`). **These are things a merchant carries**: galls,
gum, orpiment, gold leaf, good calf-skins, wax.

**The hand.** A goose or swan quill, cut with a knife; lines ruled with a hard point. Alcuin's rule for the writing
room at Tours (candidate `alcuin_scribes`): copy from a correct book, put the points in their places, add nothing of
your own. Scribes complained in the margins: "My hand is weary with writing" (Meyer 1911, "Colum Cille the Scribe").
A whole Bible was years of work for several hands; a psalter or a gospel-book a season or two for one (?).

**The binding.** Sewn on cords, oak boards, leather over; a treasure-binding of gold, silver and gems for an altar
book. The binding was often worth more than the writing and was what raiders wanted: gospel-books were ransomed back
without their covers. (The Codex Aureus now in Stockholm has a note in English of about 850 that Ealdorman Ælfred and
his wife Werburg bought it back "from the heathen army with pure gold" and gave it to Christ Church, Canterbury (?).
That is a ransom card with a real name on it.)

**The price.** Three figures a writer can lean on:

| What | Price | Source |
|---|---|---|
| One fine book of the Geographers, bought at Rome | Eight hides of land by the river Fresca, given by King Aldfrith (c. 700) | Bede, Lives of the Abbots 15 (tr. Giles) |
| The æstel on each copy of Alfred's Pastoral Care | "worth fifty mancus": 1,500 pennies at 30 d the mancus | Alfred's preface (tr. Sweet 1871); the rate (?) |
| A book of homilies, bought by a countess of Anjou c. 1050 | 200 sheep, three measures of grain and some marten skins | often quoted; not checked (?) |

For the game: an ordinary service book is worth more than the Gislinge boat's whole cargo; a great gospel-book is
worth more than a ship. A book should never be a market good with a price word. It is a **commission**: carried for
a fee, with a reputation at each end.

**Copying.** A book is borrowed to be copied and sent back; the lender asks a pledge. Lupus of Ferrières (840s to
850s) would not send a big book by a single messenger on foot for fear of robbers, and asked for books to be sent
with trustworthy carriers (?). This is `act:copy_text`: it should cost days, not silver, and need a clerk.

## 4. Who could read

- **Clergy and monks**: Latin, by rule. The Rule of Benedict sets hours of reading every day and a book to each monk
  in Lent (ch. 48) (?). Quality varied: Charlemagne complained that letters from monasteries had "correct thoughts
  and uncouth expressions" (candidate `charlemagne_letters_fulda`), and Alfred said that when he came to the throne
  in 871 he could not think of one man south of the Thames who could turn a Latin letter into English (candidate
  `alfred_preface_pastoral_care`).
- **Nuns**: among the best scribes. Abbess Eadburg's women at Minster-in-Thanet wrote in gold for Boniface. A
  Frankish capitulary of 789 forbids abbesses to write or send *winileodas*, songs for a friend (?): so they did.
- **Kings and great laymen**: some read; few wrote. Charlemagne "tried also to learn to write ... but he made little
  advance in this strange task, which was begun too late in life" (Einhard 25, tr. Grant). Alfred learned to read
  English at twelve or later and Latin at about thirty-nine (Asser 22, 87) (?). A Frankish count's will of the 860s
  lists some fifty books (Eberhard of Friuli) (?), and a noble mother, Dhuoda, wrote a handbook for her son in 841 to
  843 (?). In England after Alfred, reading English became more common among thanes and reeves (?).
- **Merchants**: no North Sea merchant's writing survives from these years, and no source says one could read
  Latin. They reckoned by tally stick and by weight. But they stood next to writing all the time: toll men had
  written lists, kings wrote about them (candidate `charlemagne_offa_796`), charters freed named houses from toll,
  and in the north they cut runes. Ælfric's merchant (already in the chest) speaks in a school-book; that is as near
  to a pen as the sources put him.
- **Listening is reading.** Alfred won his mother's book by having it read to him and saying it back (candidate
  `asser_book_of_poems`). Laws were read out at the moot. The game's rule that a crewman reads part of a text aloud is
  the period's own way.

What this means for play: a text in the chest is locked or open by **who is aboard**. A Latin text needs a clerk as
passenger or a schooled crewman; an English or Saxon one needs a man of that tongue; runes need a rune-wise man. The
engine's `tongue` and `learn` verbs already carry this.

## 5. Letters and their carriers

- **What a letter is.** A single sheet of parchment, folded, tied and sealed with wax; or a pair of wax tablets
  for a short message (Aldhelm's riddle on the tablets is in the chest). The words are Latin and formal; **the real
  message is often in the bearer's mouth** ("the bearer will tell you the rest") (?). That is a gift to a card
  game: the letter is cargo, the spoken half is knowledge.
- **Who carries.** There is no post. A letter goes with a pupil, a priest, a pilgrim, a king's envoy, or a merchant
  going that way. Boniface: "I have chosen the priest Eoba to write this request" and carry it (Kylie 1911). About
  150 letters to and from Boniface and Lul survive, and some 270 to 300 of Alcuin's (?), nearly all of which crossed
  the Channel or the North Sea in someone's ship.
- **What goes with a letter.** Always a gift: a cloak, a towel, pepper and incense, a silver cup, a book
  (Boniface's letters name them all; Kylie 1911). A letter with nothing in the bearer's hand was rude.
- **Letters about trade.** Charlemagne to Offa, 796 (candidate `charlemagne_offa_796`): merchants to have the old
  protection on both sides; traders who hide among pilgrims must pay toll; the black stones Offa asked for will be
  sent if he says what size; and the cloaks sent from England should be as long as they used to be. About 790
  Charlemagne had shut the ports of Gaul to English ships in a quarrel over a marriage (Deeds of the Abbots of
  Fontenelle) (?).
- **Letters of safe passage.** A king's or bishop's letter asked for lodging and freedom from toll on the road
  (the Frankish *tractoria*) (?). Cnut's letter of 1027 (candidate `cnut_letter_1027`) is the same thing won for a
  whole people: his traders and pilgrims to pass the Alpine barriers without toll.
- **How long.** A letter from Tours to York and its answer could take a season. Alcuin heard of Lindisfarne
  (8 June 793) in Francia in time to write several letters about it that year (?). The game's rule that news is as
  old as the ship that brought it fits exactly.
- **Rune letters.** In the north a message is cut on a stick. See the next section.

## 6. Runes and rune sticks

- **Two rows in use in 793.** In England and Frisia the *futhorc* of 28 to 33 runes, an old row with added signs. In
  Denmark, Sweden and Norway the *younger futhark* of only 16 runes, which replaced the 24-rune row during the
  eighth century (?). A Frisian and a Dane can each cut runes and not read the other's easily: a small, true
  obstacle.
- **Frisian runes.** About twenty inscriptions, fifth to ninth century, many on wood, bone and whalebone from the
  terps: the yew stick from Westeremden (perhaps about 750 to 800), the yew stick from Britsum, the little yew sword
  from Arum cut *edæboda*, perhaps "return-messenger", and a whalebone staff from Bernsterburen (?). The player's
  own people cut runes on yew in the game's first years. A rune-cut yew token is the right shape for a Frisian
  merchant's pledge or message.
- **What was cut on sticks.** Names and owner's marks, short messages, charms, alphabets, jokes. Hedeby
  (`sliesthorp`) has given rune sticks of the ninth and tenth centuries, and Staraya Ladoga (`aldeigjuborg`) one of
  the early ninth with a verse on it (?). The great find of trade sticks is later: about 670 inscriptions from the
  wharf at Bergen, mostly twelfth to fourteenth century, with tags reading "So-and-so owns" tied to bales, and
  business messages (?). That is after the game; use it only as the likely habit of an older trade whose sticks have
  rotted.
- **In the stories.** Saxo has Amleth sent to Britain with "a letter graven on wood--a kind of writing material
  frequent in old times", which he shaves clean and cuts again (candidate `saxo_letter_on_wood`). Egil finds a
  botched love-charm on whalebone under a sick girl's bed: "Runes none should grave ever Who knows not to read them"
  (candidate `egil_runes_whalebone`). Hávamál 139 to 146 tells how Othin won the runes and asks, "Knowest how one
  shall write, knowest how one shall rede?" (candidate `havamal_runes`).
- **Stones.** The Rök stone in Östergötland (about 800) is the longest inscription, some 760 runes, full of
  half-told old stories (?). Harald Bluetooth's stone at Jelling (about 965) says he "won all Denmark and Norway and
  made the Danes Christian" (?). The fashion for stones "in memory of" a named man peaks in Sweden after 1000, and
  many name men who died east or west on voyages (?). No free English translation of a stone was found that passes
  the rule; the game needs its own drafts for these.
- **Monks and runes.** The Exeter Book riddles spell answers in runes (two are in the chest). A St Gall
  school-book has the Danish rune names; Hrabanus at Fulda copied a rune row. An English rune poem of 29 stanzas was
  in a tenth-century book (burnt 1731; printed by Hickes in 1705) (?). A free English of it by J. M. Kemble (1840,
  died 1857) exists but was not checked this session (?).
- **Ogham.** In Ireland and the Irish Sea the older stroke-alphabet survives on standing stones and as a learned
  game (?). Not a merchant's tool.

For the game: the chest's `rune_stick` form is right for anything Norse or Frisian that is short. A rune stick
should be a thing you can be **given, carry, read if someone aboard knows runes, and alter at a risk**.

## 7. What went by mouth

- **The harp at the table.** Bede's story of Caedmon (candidate `bede_caedmon`): at a feast "it was agreed to make
  merry by singing in turn", and the harp came round. Every free man was expected to have a song.
- **The singer by trade.** Old English *scop*, Norse *skáld*. Widsith (candidate `widsith`) is the trade's own boast:
  a list of every people and king the singer has served and what each gave him. Deor (in the chest) is the other
  side: a singer who lost his place.
- **Bernlef the Frisian.** Altfrid's Life of Liudger (II.1) (?) tells of a blind man at Helwerd in the Frisian
  north, about 785 to 800, whom his neighbours loved "because he knew well how to sing the deeds of the ancients and
  the wars of kings" to the harp. Liudger cured his sight and taught him psalms instead. He is real, he is Frisian,
  and he is alive when the game starts. The matter of his songs would be that of Finnsburg (Finn the Frisian king:
  Beowulf fitts XVII and XVIII, in the chest), Widsith and Hildebrand.
- **What kings did with the songs.** Charlemagne "wrote out the barbarous and ancient songs, in which the acts of
  the kings and their wars were sung" (candidate `einhard_songs_winds`); the book is lost. His son Louis is said to
  have despised the heathen songs he learned as a boy (Thegan 19) (?).
- **The Church's unease.** The council of Clofesho (747) told priests not to chant in church like worldly poets (?);
  Alcuin (797) asked what Ingeld had to do with Christ. So the songs were everywhere, cloisters included.
- **New songs for new events.** The Chronicle turns to verse for Brunanburh in 937 (candidate `brunanburh`) and
  Maldon was sung within years of 991 (in the chest). The Franks had the Ludwigslied for the beating of the Northmen
  at Saucourt in 881 (no free English found). Skalds made praise-poems on raids and battles as they happened (twelve
  verses are in the chest), and these are the oldest Norse sources of all: Bragi the Old, about 850 (?).
- **Wisdom by heart.** Proverbs (Hávamál; the Exeter gnomic verses, candidate `maxims_frisian_wife`), riddles
  (the chest has many), charms for a journey or a sick ox, lists of kings, and the law itself: in the north the law
  was spoken from memory at the thing until the twelfth century (?).
- **Sailing knowledge.** Courses, sea-marks, tides and distances in days went by mouth (Ohthere, Wulfstan and
  Landnámabók in the chest are such talk written down). There were no sea charts.
- **Tall tales.** The whale taken for an island (candidate `exeter_whale`), Irish voyages to islands of sheep and
  birds (Brendan, Bran, Máel Dúin), and the news from the edge of the world.

For the game: `act:song`, `act:riddles` and `act:hear_law` are where these live. A heard text should be able to go
into the chest only if someone aboard can hold it: a singer, or a clerk who writes it down (which is how
Hildebrand and the Chronicle's poems reached us).

## 9. Forty candidates for the game's library

None of these is in `texts_793.json` (ids checked by script). Each has a translation that passes the rule
(translator dead before 1956, printed before 1931) unless the entry carries a (?). "First words" are the first
words of the passage in that translation. The same forty, with reasons, are in `texts_more.json`.

**A warning on where to take the text from.** The Fordham sourcebook pages are handy for reading but say their
"electronic form ... is copyright" and not for commercial use. Take the shipped words from the printed scan
(archive.org) or from Project Gutenberg. The translation itself is free either way.

### 9.6 Notes on the forty

- **Told later.** Nos. 39 and 40 were written after 1066 about earlier times; in the chest's terms they are
  `toldLater: true`, like the saga verses already there. No. 33 was written c. 1005 about older custom. Nos. 16 to
  18 were written c. 885 about c. 780 to 810: before 885 they are stories told, not books.
- **Dorestad as "Dordrecht".** Robinson (no. 19) and "Dorstadt" in C. H. Robinson (nos. 22 to 24) are the
  translators' forms. Keep their words and add [Dorestad] in square brackets, as the chest does elsewhere.
- **"Scots"** in nos. 16 and 26 means Irishmen, as it did then. **"Norman"** in no. 17 is Grant's word for Northman.
- **Dates on the laws.** Ine c. 688 to 694; Alfred's code c. 890; the peace with Guthrum between 878 and 890.
- **The Frideburg passage** continues: "sell all that has not been given away and go with the money to Dorstadt.
  There are there many churches, priests, and clergy, and a multitude of poor people" (checked). It is the only
  sentence in any source of the time that describes Dorestad from the far end of the Baltic.
- **Annal 897** goes on to name "Wulfheard, a Frieslander; Ebb, a Frieslander, and Ethelere, a Frieslander" among
  the dead in Alfred's ships (checked).

## 10. Needs (for the spine's owner; nothing here was added to a shared table)

- `texts_793.json` has the forms `book`, `parchment`, `rune_stick`, `wax_tablet`. Texts that are **heard** (a law
  read at the moot, a song in a hall, a tale on the quay) have no form. A form such as `heard` would let nos. 13,
  16 to 18, 21, 25, 30 to 33, 37 and 40 enter the chest only when someone aboard can keep them.
- The file's `people` values are `english`, `irish`, `latin-church`, `norse`, `saxon`. The forty also use
  `frankish`, `frisian` and `danish`; these are the ids of files in `Design/content/peoples/`, but the texts loader
  may not know them.
- A year from which a text exists in the world (`from`), and a year it is lost at a place (York 866, Jarrow 794),
  are not in the texts format. `where_found` in `texts_more.json` says them in words.
- A book as cargo is a `commission`, not a `goods` entry. No new verb is needed: `commission`, `give_text`,
  `passenger` and `flag` cover every job in section 8.
