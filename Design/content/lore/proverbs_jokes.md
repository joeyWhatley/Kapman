# Proverbs, jokes and insults, 793 to 1066

Lore for Kapman: what the people of the North Sea world really found wise or funny, 150 items, each with its source and translator, grouped by who would say it. The same items as data are in `proverbs_jokes.json` (`text`, `people`, `when`, `source`, and with them `id`, `group`, `kind`, and where needed `answer` and `note`). This page is made from that file; change the file, not the page.

## How to use it

- **A proverb is said to someone, about something.** Give it to a person on a card as an answer or a parting word, not as a caption. The `note` often says when it fits.
- **Who says it matters.** `people` uses the names of the files in `Design/content/peoples/` (`norse`, `danish`, `english`, `frankish`, `frisian`, `saxon`, `irish_gaelic`), with `monks`, `merchants` and `anyone schooled` for sayings that go with a calling and not a people. A Northman does not quote Cato; a monk does not quote the High One, unless he was a Northman first.
- **Two voices.** Items marked as translated by Bellows, Grant, Meyer or Gummere are quoted word for word from a public-domain book and keep that book's wording, 'thou' and all. They are safe to show as texts the player reads or hears recited. Items marked *own rendering* are my own plain English from the Old English, Latin, Old Irish or Old High German, made for this game: they can be spoken on a card as they stand, and reworded freely.
- **The period's kinds of joke**, in the order they turn up here: understatement (the Northmen); the insult in turn (flyting); the byname; 'said the man who...' (the Durham Proverbs); the riddle with a clean answer and a dirty one (the Exeter Book); the trick with a price (Notker); the trick question among sums (Alcuin's school); the scribe's complaint; the Irish three.
- **What is not here**, because it is too late for 1066 or cannot be dated before it: the runes of Bergen and Maeshowe (12th century and later), the Norwegian and Icelandic rune poems, Mac Conglinne's vision, most saga wit. The saga bynames are in, marked as written down later.
- **(?)** marks what I could not check against a printed text: a line number, a spelling, a wording given from memory, or a date that is argued. The list of these is at the foot of the page.

## 1. Northmen and Danes

Three kinds. The sayings of the High One (Havamal): short, dry advice for a guest and a traveller, where the joke is usually how little is claimed. The flyting (senna): insults traded in verse in front of the hall, of which two whole poems survive. And bynames, given by others and kept for life. Havamal, Lokasenna and Harbarthsljoth are quoted word for word from Bellows (1923), who spells them Hovamol and Harbarthsljoth and keeps 'thou'; where a card wants the game's plain voice, say the sense in plain words and keep Bellows for a text the player reads. All three poems stand in one manuscript of c. 1270; how old the verses are is argued, and no stanza can be proved to have been said in 793 (?).

### 1. pj001 (proverb)

> Wits must he have who wanders wide,  
> But all is easy at home;  
> At the witless man the wise shall wink  
> When among such men he sits.  

- **Who says it:** norse, danish
- **When:** sayable 793-1066; verse of the 9th-10th c. (?), written down c. 1270
- **Source:** Havamal 5, tr. Henry Adams Bellows, The Poetic Edda (1923), where the poem is called Hovamol

- **When:** sayable 793-1066; verse of the 9th-10th c. (?), written down c. 1270

### 24. pj024 (insult)

> If in thou goest to Aegir's hall,  
> And fain the feast wouldst see,  
> And with slander and spite wouldst sprinkle the gods,  
> Think well lest they wipe it on thee.  

- **Who says it:** norse, danish
- **When:** sayable 793-1066; poem of the 10th c. (?), written down c. 1270
- **Source:** Lokasenna 4, tr. Bellows 1923
- **Note:** The doorkeeper Eldir to Loki. A flyting (senna) is a trade of insults in turn, in verse, before the whole hall: the one who runs out of words or loses his temper has lost.

### 25. pj025 (insult)

- **Who says it:** norse, danish
- **When:** sayable 793-1066; poem of the 10th c. (?), written down c. 1270

### 26. pj026 (insult)

> Small ill does it work though a woman may have  
> A lord or a lover or both;  
> But a wonder it is that this womanish god  
> Comes hither, though babes he has borne.  

- **Who says it:** norse, danish
- **When:** sayable 793-1066; poem of the 10th c. (?), written down c. 1270

### 33. pj033 (byname)

> Ketil Flatnose (Ketill flatnefr).  

- **Who says it:** norse
- **When:** the man lived in the mid 9th c.; the byname is written down in the 12th-13th c.
- **Source:** Landnamabok and Laxdaela saga (12th-13th c.); own rendering of the byname
- **Note:** A chieftain of Norway and the Hebrides, father of Aud the Deep-minded. Northmen named each other for the plainest thing to be seen: the nose, the hair, the legs, the belly. A byname is given by others, kept for life, and no one asks leave.

### 34. pj034 (byname)

- **When:** the man is placed in the 8th c.; the byname is written down c. 1130

### 35. pj035 (byname)

> Thorolf Butter (Thorolfr smjor): he came home from the new land in the west and said butter dripped from every blade of grass there.

## 2. The English

The English wrote their own proverbs down: the Durham Proverbs (46 of them, each in Latin and English, c. 1050), the Maxims and the Precepts in the Exeter Book (c. 970), the Rune Poem. Their best joke is the riddle with two answers, one of them not fit for the bishop whose book it is in. Their flyting is Unferth's in Beowulf and Byrhtnoth's at Maldon. None of the proverb collections has a public-domain English translation of its own that is good enough to quote, so every item here from Old English is my own plain rendering, with the Old English given where it is short, in plain letters (th for thorn and eth, ae for ash).

### 55. pj055 (proverb)

### 56. pj056 (proverb)

> The thief must go in dark weather. The giant must live in the fen, alone in the land.  

- **Who says it:** english

- **Note:** The poem is a list of where each thing belongs: the king in the hall, the bear on the heath, the fish in the water. Said with a look at a man who is somewhere he should not be.

### 57. pj057 (joke)

> Truth is the trickiest thing.

### 58. pj058 (proverb)

### 59. pj059 (joke)

- **Who says it:** english

- **Source:** The Old English Rune Poem, the stanza of the rune rad; own rendering from the Old English (text in G. Hickes, Thesaurus, 1705)
- **Note:** A dry one: every journey is easy by the fire. It works as well for a sea crossing.

### 60. pj060 (proverb)

- **Who says it:** english

### 61. pj061 (riddle)

> I am a wonderful thing, a joy to women, of use to the neighbours. I hurt no one in the town but the one who kills me. I stand up high in a bed; underneath I am shaggy. Sometimes a farmer's pretty daughter is bold enough to take hold of me. She rubs my red skin, takes my head, puts me in a tight place. She soon feels it, the girl with the braided hair who shut me in: her eye is wet.  

- **Answer:** An onion.
- **Who says it:** english

- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 25 (Krapp and Dobbie's number; 26 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English

### 62. pj062 (riddle)

> A strange thing hangs by a man's thigh, under its master's cloak. It has a hole in front. It is stiff and hard and stands firm. - **Who says it:** english
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 44 (Krapp and Dobbie; 45 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English

### 63. pj063 (riddle)

> I have heard of something growing in a corner, swelling and standing up, lifting its cover. A proud bride took hold of that boneless thing with her hands; a lord's daughter covered the swelling thing with a cloth.  

- **Answer:** Bread dough.
- **Who says it:** english
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 45 (Krapp and Dobbie; 46 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English

### 64. pj064 (riddle)

> A young man came to where he knew she stood in a corner. He stepped up to her, a strong lad, lifted his own shirt with his hands and pushed something stiff under her belt. Under her belt there began to grow what good men love in their hearts and buy with money.  

- **Answer:** A churn, and butter.

- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 54 (Krapp and Dobbie; 55 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English

### 65. pj065 (riddle)

> A moth ate words. It seemed to me a strange thing, when I heard of that wonder, that a worm should swallow a man's song, a thief in the dark eat a great saying and the strong ground it stood on. The thieving guest was not one bit the wiser for having swallowed the words.  

- **Answer:** A bookworm (said too of a pupil who learns by rote).
- **Who says it:** english, monks
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 47 (Krapp and Dobbie; 48 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English

### 66. pj066 (riddle)

> A man sat at wine with his two wives, and his two sons, and his two daughters, dear sisters, and their two sons, fine first-born boys. The father of each of those boys was in there with them, uncle and nephew. In all there were five men and women sitting inside.  

- **Answer:** Lot, his two daughters and their two sons (Genesis 19).
- **Who says it:** english, monks
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 46 (Krapp and Dobbie; 47 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English
- **Note:** A counting joke for the cloister: the wicked answer is in Scripture.

### 67. pj067 (joke)

> 'Would you catch a whale?' 'No.' 'Why not?' 'Because it is a risky thing to catch a whale. It is safer for me to go on the river with my boat than to go with many boats hunting whales.' 'Why so?' 'Because I would rather catch a fish I can kill than one that can sink and kill me and my friends with one blow.' 'And yet many catch whales and come to no harm, and make a great price by it.' 'You speak the truth. But I dare not, for the slowness of my spirit.'  

- **Who says it:** english
- **When:** c. 998
- **Source:** Aelfric of Eynsham, Colloquy (a Latin schoolroom dialogue with an Old English gloss), the fisherman; own rendering from the Latin and Old English (text in B. Thorpe, Analecta Anglo-Saxonica, 1834)
- **Note:** The honest small man, and close to the player's own choice between the river and the open sea.

### 68. pj068 (joke)

> 'We boys ask you, master, to teach us to speak Latin rightly, for we are unlearned and speak badly.' 'Are you willing to be beaten while you learn?' 'We would rather be beaten for learning than not know. But we know you are a kind man and will not lay strokes on us unless we force you to.'  

- **Who says it:** english, monks
- **When:** c. 998
- **Source:** Aelfric of Eynsham, Colloquy, the opening; own rendering from the Latin (text in Thorpe, Analecta Anglo-Saxonica, 1834)

### 69. pj069 (insult)

> You goat dung. You sheep dung. You horse dung. You cow dung. You pig dung. You dog dung. You fox dung. You cat dung. You hen dung. You ass dung.  

- **Who says it:** english, monks
- **When:** c. 1000
- **Source:** Aelfric Bata, Colloquies, colloquy 25 (?), two pupils trading abuse in Latin as a lesson in words; own rendering from memory of the Latin list, so the animals and their order are not certain (?)
- **Note:** Schoolboys were taught Latin by being made to quarrel in it. The joke is the list: the master wanted them to learn every animal.

### 70. pj070 (insult)

> Art thou that Beowulf, Breca's rival,  
> who emulous swam on the open sea,  
> when for pride the pair of you proved the floods,  
> and wantonly dared in waters deep  
> to risk your lives?  

- **Who says it:** english
- **When:** poem of the 8th-10th c. (?); the manuscript is c. 1000
- **Source:** Beowulf, lines 506-510, tr. Francis B. Gummere, The Oldest English Epic (1909)
- **Note:** Unferth's taunt at the feast: the English flyting. Beowulf's answer begins 'What a deal hast uttered, dear my Unferth, drunken with beer', and ends by naming Unferth 'the bane of thy brethren dear' (Gummere). The stranger who answers a taunt well has won his seat.

### 71. pj071 (insult)

> Do you hear, seaman, what this people says? They will give you spears for tribute, the deadly point and the old sword: a payment that will do you no good in the fight. It seems to me too shameful that you should go to your ships with our silver and no battle, now that you have come so far into our land.  

- **Who says it:** english
- **When:** the battle was on 10 or 11 August 991; the poem is of soon after
- **Source:** The Battle of Maldon, lines 45-48 and 55-58; own rendering from the Old English (the manuscript burned in 1731; text printed by T. Hearne, 1726)
- **Note:** Ealdorman Byrhtnoth's answer to the raiders' demand for silver. He lost the battle. In the same year the first tribute to the Danes was paid (the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, 991): the brave words are remembered because they did not work.

### 72. pj072 (byname)

> Aethelred Unraed: 'Noble-counsel No-counsel'. Edmund Ironside. Harold Harefoot. Eadric Streona, 'the Grasper'.  

- **Who says it:** english
- **When:** the men lived c. 968-1040; Ironside is in the Chronicle under 1057, Streona in a Worcester book of c. 1095, Unraed first c. 1180 (?), Harefoot in the 12th c. (?)
- **Source:** Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (D) 1057 for Ironside, 'called Ironside for his valour'; Hemming's Cartulary for Streona; own rendering of the bynames
- **Note:** Unraed is a pun on the king's own name (aethel-raed, noble counsel). Do not have anyone say it before the 12th century unless as a whisper marked invented (?). 'The Unready' is a much later mistake for it.

## 3. The Franks: the court and the schoolroom

What made the Franks laugh is in Notker's stories of Charles the Great, written at St Gall in 883-884 from an old soldier's and an old clerk's talk: a great man made small by a sharp answer, and above all a trick with goods and prices. Everyone schooled in Latin, Frank or English, clerk or count's son, had 'Cato' by heart, and the court played Alcuin's question game. The two German phrase lists show what was said on the road. Notker is quoted word for word from Grant (1905); the Latin is my own rendering.

### 73. pj073 (joke)

> One of you has given a Jew all this silver for a painted mouse.  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker the Stammerer (the Monk of St Gall), Gesta Karoli I.16, tr. A. J. Grant, Early Lives of Charlemagne (1905)
- **Note:** A vain bishop is tricked by a merchant, on the king's order, into buying a common house mouse stuffed with spices as a rare beast from Judea. The price climbs from three pounds of silver to a full measure. Charles then lays the silver before all the bishops and says this. It is the period's own trader's tale: the long-distance merchant here is a Jew, as many were in Francia.

### 74. pj074 (joke)

> Why do you do that, lord emperor? You are throwing away the very best part.  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli I.15, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** On a fast day a bishop has only cheese to give the king. Charles cuts off the rind; the bishop says this. Charles eats the rind, agrees, and orders two cartloads of just such cheeses sent to Aachen every year. The bishop is in despair: he cannot tell which cheeses are good without cutting each one open. Charles tells him to cut them all, skewer the good halves together and send those.

### 75. pj075 (insult)

> You nobles, you sons of my chiefs, you superfine dandies, you have trusted to your birth and your possessions and have set at naught my orders to your own advancement.  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli I.3, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** Charles in the palace school, to the idle sons of great men, after praising the poor boys who had worked. He ends: 'you will never get any favour from Charles.'

### 76. pj076 (joke)

> Most foolish of mortal men! which of these dresses is the most valuable and the most useful, this one of mine which was bought for a piece of silver, or those of yours which you bought for pounds, nay for many talents?  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli II.17, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** The courtiers had come from Pavia in silk and peacock feathers bought from the Venetians. Charles took them hunting in the rain as they stood, he in a sheepskin. Their finery shrank and cracked by the fire; his sheepskin rubbed up white. A joke about imported goods, told a lifetime after.

### 77. pj077 (saying)

> These ships are not filled with merchandise, but crowded with our fiercest enemies.  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli II.14, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** Charles at a harbour town sees sails. Some take them for Jewish, African or British traders. He knows them 'by the build of the ships and their speed' for Northmen. Then he stands at the window and weeps for what his grandsons will suffer. For the game: on this coast every strange sail is first asked, merchant or raider?

### 78. pj078 (joke)

> I have gone through this washing business here twenty times already, and I have been dressed in excellent clothes of perfect whiteness; but a sack like this is more fit for clodhoppers than for soldiers. If I were not afraid of my nakedness, for you have taken away my own clothes and have given me no new ones, I would soon leave your wrap and your Christ as well.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, danish, norse
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of the court of Louis the Pious (814-840)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli II.19, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** Northmen came each Easter to be baptised for the white robe and the gifts. One year there were fifty and the robes ran out, so shirts were cut up and stitched. An old Northman says this to the emperor. The Franks told it against the Northmen; a Dane would tell it against the Franks.

### 79. pj079 (proverb)

> The first of virtues, I think, is to hold your tongue. He is nearest to God who knows when to be silent.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; the first Latin reader of every school 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.3; own rendering from the Latin 'Virtutem primam esse puto compescere linguam: proximus ille deo est, qui scit ratione tacere' (Latin text and another English version in W. J. Chase, The Distichs of Cato, 1922)
- **Note:** Every clerk, monk and schooled nobleman in the West learned Latin from 'Cato'. A lettered person quotes it as a man now quotes a nursery rhyme.

### 80. pj080 (proverb)

> Do not fight with words against a man of many words. Speech is given to all; sense to few.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.10; own rendering from the Latin 'Contra verbosos noli contendere verbis: sermo datur cunctis, animi sapientia paucis'

### 81. pj081 (proverb)

> Keep clear of rumours, and do not try to be the one who first tells a thing. No one was ever hurt by having kept quiet; many by having spoken.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.12; own rendering from the Latin 'Rumores fuge neu studeas novus auctor haberi; nam nulli tacuisse nocet, nocet esse locutum'
- **Note:** In a game where news is the treasure this is the miser's rule, and a clerk would say it to a talkative skipper.

### 82. pj082 (proverb)

> When someone praises you, be your own judge. Do not believe others about yourself more than you believe yourself.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.14; own rendering from the Latin 'Cum te aliquis laudat, iudex tuus esse memento; plus aliis de te quam tu tibi credere noli'

### 83. pj083 (proverb)

> When a poor friend gives you a small gift, take it kindly and remember to praise it fully.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.20; own rendering from the Latin 'Exiguum munus cum dat tibi pauper amicus, accipito placide, plene laudare memento'

### 84. pj084 (joke)

> Do not be afraid of the words of an angry wife. It is when a woman weeps that she is laying a trap.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis III.20; own rendering from the Latin 'Coniugis iratae noli tu verba timere; nam lacrimis struit insidias, cum femina plorat'
- **Note:** Schoolboys of seven learned this by heart, most of them meant for the cloister.

### 85. pj085 (proverb)

> Keep out of the law court. Be clean. Greet people gladly. Lend. See to whom you give. Love your wife. Go easy on the wine. Believe nothing rashly. Shun the whore. Read books. Shun dice. Say little at a feast.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis, the short sentences at the head of the book; own rendering from the Latin 'Foro parce. Mundus esto. Saluta libenter. Mutuum da. Cui des, videto. Coniugem ama. Vino tempera. Nihil temere credideris. Meretricem fuge. Libros lege. Aleam fuge. Pauca in convivio loquere.'
- **Note:** Each is two or three words of Latin: the first things a child read. 'Foro parce' can also be read 'keep away from the market place'. Good as single lines on a wax tablet a boy is copying.

### 86. pj086 (riddle)

> What is man? The slave of death, a traveller passing by, a guest in the place.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks
- **When:** c. 790-800
- **Source:** Alcuin of York, Disputatio Pippini cum Albino (a dialogue written for Pippin, son of Charles the Great); own rendering from the Latin 'Quid est homo? Mancipium mortis, transiens viator, loci hospes'
- **Note:** The court's question game: a boy asks 'What is...?' and the master answers in three or four names. Half lesson, half riddle.

### 87. pj087 (riddle)

> What is a letter? The keeper of history. What is a word? The betrayer of the mind. What is sleep? The picture of death.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks
- **When:** c. 790-800
- **Source:** Alcuin, Disputatio Pippini cum Albino; own rendering from the Latin 'Quid est littera? Custos historiae. Quid est verbum? Proditor animi. Quid est somnus? Mortis imago.'

### 88. pj088 (riddle)

> I saw the dead beget the living, and by the breath of the living the dead were eaten up.  

- **Answer:** Fire, born from rubbing two dry sticks, which burns the sticks.
- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks
- **When:** c. 790-800
- **Source:** Alcuin, Disputatio Pippini cum Albino, near the end; own rendering from the Latin (?)
- **Note:** The exact Latin wording could not be checked: 'breath' may be 'anger' (?).

### 89. pj089 (riddle)

> A man had to take a wolf, a goat and a bundle of cabbages over a river. The only boat he could find would carry two of them at a time. He had been told to bring all three across unharmed. Let him say, who can, how he did it.  

- **Answer:** Take the goat over. Go back and take the wolf over, and bring the goat back. Leave the goat, take the cabbages over. Go back and fetch the goat.
- **Who says it:** frankish, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** oldest manuscript of the late 9th c.; put under Alcuin's name (died 804) (?)
- **Source:** Propositiones ad acuendos iuvenes ('Problems to sharpen the young'), no. 18 (?); own rendering from the Latin
- **Note:** The oldest known telling of this puzzle anywhere. Made for a ferry: a card at any river crossing can set it.

### 90. pj090 (joke)

> An ox ploughs all day. How many footprints does he leave in the last furrow?  

- **Answer:** None. The ox walks in front of the plough and the plough covers his prints.
- **Who says it:** frankish, monks, anyone schooled
- **When:** oldest manuscript of the late 9th c.; put under Alcuin's name (?)
- **Source:** Propositiones ad acuendos iuvenes, no. 14 (?); own rendering from the Latin
- **Note:** A trick question set among sums, so the clever boy starts counting.

### 91. pj091 (insult)

> The Romance-speakers are stupid, the Bavarians are clever. There is little sense in the Romance-speakers; they have more stupidity than sense.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, saxon (any German-speaker of a speaker of Romance)
- **When:** c. 810
- **Source:** The Kassel Glosses (Kassel, 4o Ms. theol. 24), a Latin and Old High German word list from Bavaria; own rendering of 'Stulti sunt Romani, sapienti sunt Paioari; modica est sapientia in Romana, plus habent stultitia quam sapientia' / 'Tole sint Uualha, spahe sint Peigira; luzic ist spahi in Uualhum, mera hapent tolaheiti denne spahi' (?)
- **Note:** Written into a word list between the names of body parts and tools, as a practice sentence. 'Walha' is the German speaker's word for anyone who speaks Romance: a Frank from the west as much as an Italian. The spelling is from memory (?).

### 92. pj092 (insult)

> A dog's arse in your nose.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, saxon, frisian (any speaker of a German tongue)
- **When:** c. 900
- **Source:** The Paris Conversations (Altdeutsche Gespraeche; Paris, BnF lat. 7641, written in the margins), a phrase book of spoken Old High German for a speaker of Romance; own rendering of 'Undes ars in tine naso', glossed in Latin 'canis culum in tuo naso'
- **Note:** A real traveller's phrase book of the period, and it has the curses in. This is what a carter or a boatman on the Rhine really said.

### 93. pj093 (saying)

> Give me my horse. Mend my shoe. Sir, I want a drink. What land do you come from? Where did you lodge last night, friend?  

- **Who says it:** frankish, saxon, frisian (any speaker of a German tongue)
- **When:** c. 900
- **Source:** The Paris Conversations (Paris, BnF lat. 7641); own rendering of phrases such as 'Gimer min ros' (da mihi meum equum), 'Buozze mine sco', 'Erro, e guille trenchen', 'Gueliche lande cumen ger?' (?)
- **Note:** The useful half of the phrase book. Good as the first words the player learns of a tongue. The Old High German spellings are from memory and need checking against the edition (?).

### 94. pj094 (joke)

> Carmina clarisonae calvis cantate Camenae. (Sing, clear-voiced Muses, songs for the bald.)  

- **Who says it:** frankish, monks
- **When:** c. 900 (?)
- **Source:** Hucbald of Saint-Amand (died 930), Ecloga de calvis, line 1; own rendering
- **Note:** A poem of 146 lines in praise of bald men in which every single word begins with the letter c. Monks' wit: hard to do, pointless, and very proud of itself. Bald men, it says, make the best kings, bishops and soldiers.

### 95. pj095 (byname)

> At court the king is David, Alcuin is Flaccus, Angilbert is Homer, and little Einhard is Nardulus, who runs about like an ant.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, monks
- **When:** c. 794-800
- **Source:** Alcuin's letters and poems for the names David, Flaccus and Homer; Theodulf of Orleans, poem 25 to the king (c. 796) for Nardulus and the ant (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Poetae I); own plain retelling
- **Note:** The learned kind of byname: the court scholars gave each other names from the Bible and the old poets, and teased the small man for his size.

### 96. pj096 (byname)

> Charles the Bald. Louis the Stammerer. Charles the Fat. Charles the Simple.  

- **Who says it:** frankish
- **When:** the kings reigned 840-929; 'Stammerer' (Balbus) is in Regino of Pruem, c. 908; 'Bald' and 'Simple' are in use by the 10th c. (?); 'Fat' (Crassus) is first found in the 12th c.
- **Source:** Regino of Pruem, Chronicon; own rendering of the bynames
- **Note:** Kings got the same kind of byname as anyone. Have no one say 'Charles the Fat' in the game's years: in his own day he was only the emperor Charles, or Charles the Third.

### 97. pj097 (joke)

> Ho, everyone that desires wisdom, let him draw near and take it at our hands; for it is wisdom that we have for sale.  

- **Who says it:** frankish (of the Irish), irish_gaelic
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of the first years of Charles the Great
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli I.1, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** Two Irish scholars land in Gaul with British traders and stand in the market with nothing to sell, shouting this, because, they said, people care only for what is sold and not for what is given free. Charles hears of it and sends for them.

## 4. Monks and scribes

The monk's joke is the complaint: the cold, the ink, the hand, the beer. It is written where the abbot will not look, in the margin or after the last line. Irish scribes wrote theirs in Irish beside the Latin they were copying. The verse is Kuno Meyer's (1911), word for word.

### 98. pj098 (marginal note)

> A blessing on the soul of Fergus. Amen. I am very cold.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** A scribe's note in Old Irish in the margin of the St Gall Priscian (St Gallen, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. Sang. 904); printed and translated by W. Stokes and J. Strachan, Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus II (1903); the English here is from memory of theirs (?)
- **Note:** A Latin grammar copied by Irish monks, who wrote what they felt in the margins in their own tongue where the master would not read it. The driest voice of the whole age.

### 99. pj099 (marginal note)

> Oh, my hand!  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** Margin of the St Gall Priscian (Cod. Sang. 904), Old Irish 'uit mo chrob'; Stokes and Strachan, Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus II (1903) (?)

### 100. pj100 (marginal note)

> New parchment, bad ink. I say nothing more.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** Margin of the St Gall Priscian (Cod. Sang. 904); Stokes and Strachan, Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus II (1903); English from memory of theirs (?)
- **Note:** A complaint about the goods supplied: a trader who sells parchment or oak-gall ink can hear it said to his face.

### 101. pj101 (marginal note)

> Ale-killed.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** Margin of the St Gall Priscian (Cod. Sang. 904), the one word 'latheirt', written in ogham strokes; Stokes and Strachan, Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus II (1903) (?)
- **Note:** A hangover, written in the old secret letters at the top of the page so that it would not be read.

### 102. pj102 (colophon)

> Three fingers write, and the whole body labours.  

- **Who says it:** monks
- **When:** found from the 8th c. on
- **Source:** A scribe's closing formula, Latin 'Tres digiti scribunt, totum corpus laborat'; own rendering (many examples gathered in W. Wattenbach, Das Schriftwesen im Mittelalter, 3rd ed. 1896)
- **Note:** Written at the end of a book, where the scribe may speak for himself.

### 103. pj103 (colophon)

> As the harbour is sweet to the sailor, so is the last line to the scribe.  

- **Who says it:** monks, merchants
- **When:** found from the 8th c. on
- **Source:** A scribe's closing formula, Latin 'Sicut navigantibus dulcis est portus, ita scriptori novissimus versus'; own rendering (examples in Wattenbach, Das Schriftwesen im Mittelalter, 1896)
- **Note:** The one saying the monk and the boatman share. A monk who sees the player's boat come in can turn it round.

### 104. pj104 (colophon)

> He who cannot write thinks it no work. It dims the eyes, bends the back, breaks the ribs and the belly, pains the kidneys and wearies the whole body. So, reader, turn the leaves slowly and keep your fingers off the letters.  

- **Who says it:** monks
- **When:** 945
- **Source:** Colophon of the scribe Florentius of Valeranica to a copy of Gregory's Moralia (Madrid, Biblioteca Nacional, MS 80); own rendering from the Latin, shortened (?)
- **Note:** From Castile, outside the game's seas, but the same formula runs through scribes' closing notes all over the West. Exact wording not checked (?).

### 105. pj105 (verse)

> A hedge of trees surrounds me,  
> A blackbird's lay sings to me;  
> Above my lined booklet  
> The trilling birds chant to me.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** Old Irish verse in the margin of the St Gall Priscian, tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'The Scribe'
- **Note:** The same book as the cold and the bad ink. On a good day he wrote this.

### 106. pj106 (verse)

> My hand is weary with writing,  
> My sharp quill is not steady,  
> My slender-beaked pen juts forth  
> A black draught of shining dark-blue ink.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** 11th c. (?); put in the mouth of Colum Cille (died 597)
- **Source:** Old Irish poem, tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'Colum Cille the Scribe'

### 107. pj107 (verse)

> I and my white Pangur  
> Have each his special art:  
> His mind is set on hunting mice,  
> Mine is upon my special craft.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic
- **When:** early 9th c.
- **Source:** Old Irish poem written by an Irish monk on the Continent in the Reichenau Primer (St Paul im Lavanttal, Stiftsbibliothek), tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'The Monk and his Pet Cat'
- **Note:** The poem ends: 'He is a master of the work / Which every day he does: / While I am at my own work / To bring difficulty to clearness.' A monk likening himself to his cat is as near to a smile as the cloister writes.

### 108. pj108 (joke)

> I read or I write, I teach or I search out wisdom. I eat and I drink gladly; I call on the Muses in rhyme. I sleep, snoring; awake, I pray to God.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic, frankish
- **When:** c. 848-858
- **Source:** Sedulius Scottus, an Irish scholar at Liege on the Meuse, poem II.74 (?) (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Poetae III); own rendering from the Latin, shortened
- **Note:** A scholar's honest list of his day, for his bishop. Liege is on the river road from Dorestad.

### 109. pj109 (joke)

> Thirst and hunger trouble us, a two-headed beast. Our house is black with dark that never lifts. Good bishop, send the learned men something fit to drink.  

- **Who says it:** monks, irish_gaelic, frankish
- **When:** c. 848-858
- **Source:** Sedulius Scottus, begging poems to Bishop Hartgar of Liege (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Poetae III); own free rendering of the sense of several lines (?), not a quotation
- **Note:** Sedulius and his Irish friends had come in out of the snow and lived on the bishop's bounty. He complains in good verse about the bad beer and asks for wine. A model for a learned beggar at the player's door.

### 110. pj110 (joke)

> I myself am lazy and slower than a tortoise, and so never got into Frankland.  

- **Who says it:** monks
- **When:** 883-884
- **Source:** Notker the Stammerer, Gesta Karoli I.34, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** The author on himself. He was called Balbulus, the Stammerer, and in his own book he calls himself stammering and toothless (II.17) (?). A man who gives himself the byname first takes the sting out of it.

### 111. pj111 (saying)

> Let God's words be read when the priests sit at table. It is right to hear a reader there, not a harper; the sermons of the fathers, not the songs of the heathen. What has Ingeld to do with Christ? The house is narrow; it cannot hold both.  

- **Who says it:** monks, english
- **When:** 797
- **Source:** Alcuin of York, letter 124 (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Epistolae IV) to a bishop he calls Speratus; own rendering from the Latin
- **Note:** Ingeld is a hero of the old songs (he is in Beowulf). The letter is the proof that English churchmen had a harper in at dinner and liked it. Use it both ways: a stern abbot says it; a cheerful one has just sent for the harper.

### 112. pj112 (joke)

> Alas, alas, there is death in the pot, man of God. The wine is gone from our stores, and bitter beer rages in our bellies.  

- **Who says it:** monks, english, frankish
- **When:** 790
- **Source:** Alcuin, letter from Northumbria to his pupil Joseph, letter 8 (?) (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Epistolae IV); own rendering from the Latin
- **Note:** Alcuin, home in England after years at the Frankish court, cannot get wine and asks his friend to drink his share. 'Death in the pot' is a joke from Scripture (2 Kings 4.40). A wine merchant on the Rhine could live on men like this.

### 113. pj113 (insult)

> Look at your hair, how you have wished to copy the heathen in your beards and your hair. Are you not afraid of the men whose hair you wanted to wear?  

- **Who says it:** monks, english
- **When:** 793, after 8 June
- **Source:** Alcuin, letter 16 to King Aethelred of Northumbria on the sack of Lindisfarne (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Epistolae IV); own rendering from the Latin
- **Note:** Written in the very weeks the game begins. The young men of Northumbria had been cutting their hair in the Northmen's fashion before the raid: the raiders were known, traded with and copied.

## 5. The Irish

The Irish kept wisdom in threes (the Triads, 9th century) and in the question-and-answer of King Cormac's teaching, and their scholars were the wandering wits of the Frankish world, laughed at and learned from. Triads and Cormac are Kuno Meyer's words (1906, 1909, 1911). The player meets the Irish at Dublin after 841, as monks and scholars on the Rhine and Meuse, and as slaves in any Northman's market.

### 114. pj114 (triad)

> Three slender things that best support the world: the slender stream of milk from the cow's dug into the pail; the slender blade of green corn upon the ground; the slender thread over the hand of a skilled woman.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 75, tr. Kuno Meyer (Royal Irish Academy, Todd Lecture Series XIII, 1906); wording as in Meyer's Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911)
- **Note:** The Irish put wisdom in threes. A triad is said as a challenge: the speaker gives the head, 'three slender things', and the listener must fill it.

### 115. pj115 (triad)

> The three worst welcomes: a handicraft in the same house with the inmates; scalding water upon your feet; salt food without a drink.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 70, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911

### 116. pj116 (triad)

> Three rude ones of the world: a youngster mocking an old man; a robust person mocking an invalid; a wise man mocking a fool.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 82, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911

### 117. pj117 (triad)

> Three fewnesses that are better than plenty: a fewness of fine words; a fewness of cows in grass; a fewness of friends around good ale.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 93, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911

### 118. pj118 (triad)

> Three laughing-stocks of the world: an angry man, a jealous man, a niggard.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 95, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906

### 119. pj119 (triad)

> Three signs of a fop: the track of his comb in his hair; the track of his teeth in his food; the track of his stick behind him.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 103, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911

### 120. pj120 (triad)

> Three signs of ill-breeding: a long visit, staring, constant questioning.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911 (number not checked (?))
- **Note:** All three are what a merchant does in a strange port.

### 121. pj121 (triad)

> Three idiots of a bad guest-house: an old hag with a chronic cough; a brainless tartar of a girl; a hobgoblin of a gillie.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911 (number not checked (?))
- **Note:** Meyer's 'tartar' is his own word of 1906, not the period's; in the game's own voice say 'a brainless shrew of a girl'.

### 122. pj122 (triad)

> Three false sisters: 'perhaps,' 'may be,' 'I dare say.' Three timid brothers: 'hush!' 'stop!' 'listen!'  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland, two triads, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906; wording as in Meyer 1911 (numbers not checked (?))
- **Note:** The three false sisters are every buyer who has not made up his mind.

### 123. pj123 (triad)

> Three that are most difficult to talk to: a king about his booty, a viking in his hauberk, a boor who is under patronage.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 232, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906
- **Note:** The Irish word is Gall, a foreigner, which by then meant a Northman: 'viking' is Meyer's English. The mail shirt is the point: he is rich, armed, and not listening.

### 124. pj124 (triad)

> Three worst smiles: the smile of a wave, the smile of a lewd woman, the grin of a dog ready to leap.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** The Triads of Ireland 238, tr. Kuno Meyer 1906
- **Note:** The smile of a wave is the white on its top. A sea saying, and the best single line here for a storm card.

### 125. pj125 (saying)

> I was a listener in woods,  
> I was a gazer at stars,  
> I was blind where secrets were concerned,  
> I was silent in a wilderness,  
> I was talkative among many,  
> I was mild in the mead-hall,  
> I was stern in battle.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.; put in the mouth of the legendary king Cormac mac Airt
- **Source:** Tecosca Cormaic (The Instructions of King Cormac), tr. Kuno Meyer (Royal Irish Academy, Todd Lecture Series XV, 1909); wording as in Meyer's Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911)
- **Note:** The answer to 'What were your habits when you were a lad?' Every answer in the book opens 'Not hard to tell.'

### 126. pj126 (joke)

> 'What is the sweetest thing you have heard?' 'Not hard to tell. The shout of triumph after victory,  
> Praise after wages,  
> A lady's invitation to her pillow.'  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** Tecosca Cormaic, tr. Kuno Meyer 1909; wording as in Meyer 1911
- **Note:** 'Praise after wages': thanks are sweet once the silver is in the hand. A hired man's proverb.

### 127. pj127 (verse)

> Bitter is the wind to-night,  
> It tosses the ocean's white hair:  
> To-night I fear not the fierce warriors of Norway  
> Coursing on the Irish Sea.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic, monks
- **When:** c. 850
- **Source:** Old Irish verse in the margin of the St Gall Priscian, tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'The Viking Terror'
- **Note:** The dry humour of the coast: a storm is good news, because no raider can sail. Any monk or farmer by the sea can say the same in plain words.

### 128. pj128 (verse)

> To go to Rome  
> Is much of trouble, little of profit:  
> The King whom thou seekest here,  
> Unless thou bring Him with thee, thou wilt not find.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic, monks
- **When:** 9th c.
- **Source:** Old Irish verse, tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'The Pilgrim at Rome'
- **Note:** Said to a pilgrim paying for a passage south, by someone who has been.

### 129. pj129 (verse)

> Sweet little bell  
> That is struck in the windy night,  
> I liefer go to a tryst with thee  
> Than to a tryst with a foolish woman.  

- **Who says it:** irish_gaelic, monks
- **When:** 9th c. (?)
- **Source:** Old Irish verse, tr. Kuno Meyer, Selections from Ancient Irish Poetry (1911), 'The Church Bell in the Night'
- **Note:** Irish bells had no tongue and were struck with a hammer (Meyer's note).

### 130. pj130 (joke)

> Three Irishmen came to King Alfred in a boat with no oars. They had stolen away from Ireland because they wished to be on pilgrimage for the love of God, and they did not care where. The boat was made of two and a half hides, and they took food for seven nights. On the seventh night they came to land in Cornwall, and went straight to the king.  

- **Who says it:** english (of the Irish), irish_gaelic
- **When:** 891
- **Source:** The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle under 891; own plain retelling (the entry in English in J. A. Giles, 1847). Their names were Dubslane, Macbeth and Maelinmun
- **Note:** The Chronicle tells it flat, between a battle and a death, and so should the game. The joke for a seaman is the boat: no oars, no steering, and they arrived.

### 131. pj131 (insult)

> Take the second letter out of Scottus (an Irishman) and you have what he is: sottus (a fool).  

- **Who says it:** frankish (of the Irish), monks
- **When:** c. 796
- **Source:** Theodulf of Orleans, poem 25 to King Charles, on an Irish scholar at court (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Poetae I); own plain retelling of the pun, not a quotation (?)
- **Note:** A Goth from Spain mocking an Irishman at a Frankish court, in Latin. A later tale gives the Irishman's side: Charles the Bald, across the table, asks John the Irishman what separates a sot from a Scot, and John says, 'Only the table.' That tale is first written by William of Malmesbury, c. 1125, so it is not safe as fact (?).

## 6. Merchants, Frisians and seafarers

What was said about traders, by traders, and about ships. The Frisians left no writing of their own from these years: what there is was said of them by Franks and English, and it is here. The rest is what any man in a boat could say, whatever his tongue.

### 132. pj132 (joke)

> What is the good of those little napkins? I cannot cover myself with them in bed and when I am on horseback I cannot shield myself with them against wind and rain.  

- **Who says it:** frankish (of the Frisians), frisian
- **When:** story told in 883-884 of Charles the Great (died 814)
- **Source:** Notker, Gesta Karoli I.34, tr. Grant 1905
- **Note:** The one period joke that is about the player's own people. The Franks took to short striped cloaks in the Gaulish fashion; the Frisians sold the short ones at the price of the old long ones; the king found out and ordered that only the old cloaks, 'broad, wide and long', be bought at that price. In Dorestad it is told as a good stroke of business, in Aachen as a swindle.

### 133. pj133 (insult)

> Hoist your sails and be gone; leave Dorestad behind you. Black Hrotberct will hardly make ready a roof for a guest there, and the greedy merchant, look, has no love for your song.  

- **Who says it:** monks, frankish, english (of a Dorestad merchant)
- **When:** c. 780-790
- **Source:** Alcuin of York, poem 4, a letter in verse sent down the Rhine to his friends (Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Poetae I); own rendering from the Latin 'Hinc tua vela leva, fugiens Dorstada relinque: non tibi forte niger Hrotberct parat hospita tecta, nec amat ecce tuum carmen mercator avarus'
- **Note:** A real merchant of Dorestad, named, a few years before the game begins, and what a scholar thought of him. Hrotberct is a historical person: he may be an old man in 793 (?). Earlier in the same poem the prior at Utrecht gives honey, porridge and butter, 'for Frisia grows neither oil nor wine' (?).

### 134. pj134 (saying)

> Welcome is her dear one to the Frisian wife when the ship is at rest. His boat has come and her man is home, her own provider. She calls him in, washes his clothes, stained by the sea, and gives him fresh ones; she gives him on land what his love asks.  

- **Who says it:** frisian, english (of the Frisians)
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book); older in the mouth
- **Source:** Maxims I, lines 94-99; own rendering from the Old English (text in Thorpe, Codex Exoniensis, 1842)
- **Note:** The English proverb poem's picture of a sailor's home is a Frisian one: to the English, a Frisian was a man with a ship. The warmest thing said of the player's people in any source.

### 135. pj135 (joke)

> A wife must keep faith with her man. Many are steadfast; many are curious and love strange men when the other is far away. A sailor is long on his voyage. Still, a man must always wait for his dear one.  

- **Who says it:** frisian, english, any seafarer
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book); older in the mouth
- **Source:** Maxims I, lines 100-104, the lines that follow the Frisian wife; own rendering from the Old English, shortened
- **Note:** The crew's joke on any long voyage, and the poem says it straight after the kind picture.

### 136. pj136 (proverb)

> Weary is the man who rows against the wind.  

- **Who says it:** english, frisian, any seafarer
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book); older in the mouth
- **Source:** Maxims I, line 185 (?); own rendering from the Old English 'Werig sceal se with winde roweth'
- **Note:** The poem goes on: he is often blamed for a coward, losing heart while his oar dries on the boat.

### 137. pj137 (saying)

> 'I go on board my ship with my goods and row over the sea, and sell my things, and buy costly things that are not made in this land, and bring them here to you with great danger over the sea. Sometimes I am wrecked and lose everything, and barely get away alive.'  

- **Who says it:** english, frisian, any merchant
- **When:** c. 998
- **Source:** Aelfric of Eynsham, Colloquy, the merchant; own rendering from the Latin and Old English (text in Thorpe, Analecta Anglo-Saxonica, 1834)
- **Note:** A merchant of the period speaking for himself, in a schoolbook. He lists what he brings: purple cloth and silk, gems and gold, strange clothes and spices, wine and oil, ivory and brass, copper and tin, sulphur and glass.

### 138. pj138 (joke)

> 'Will you sell your things here for what you paid for them there?' 'I will not. What good would my work do me then? I mean to sell dearer here than I bought there, so that I get some gain to feed myself and my wife and my son.'  

- **Who says it:** english, frisian, any merchant
- **When:** c. 998
- **Source:** Aelfric of Eynsham, Colloquy, the merchant; own rendering from the Latin and Old English (text in Thorpe, Analecta Anglo-Saxonica, 1834)
- **Note:** The whole of trade, said to a monk who asked as if profit were a new idea. The game's own answer to anyone who calls the player greedy.

### 139. pj139 (proverb)

> Shun what is too much; remember to be glad of little. The ship is safer that sails on a small water.  

- **Who says it:** anyone schooled, monks, frankish, english
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis II.6; own rendering from the Latin 'Quod nimium est fugito, parvo gaudere memento: tuta mage est puppis, modico quae flumine fertur'
- **Note:** A clerk's warning to a man who wants a bigger ship.

### 140. pj140 (proverb)

> Try what you can do. It is far better to work along the shore with oars than to spread sail on the deep.  

- **Who says it:** anyone schooled, monks, frankish, english
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis IV.33; own rendering from the Latin 'Quod potes, id tempta; nam litus carpere remis utilius multo est quam velum tendere in altum'
- **Note:** The landsman's rule, and the exact choice the chart gives the player: the coast or the crossing.

### 141. pj141 (proverb)

> Hold cheap what is dear and dear what is cheap. Then no one will take you for greedy or for mean.  

- **Who says it:** anyone schooled, monks, frankish, english
- **When:** written in the 3rd-4th c.; a schoolbook 793-1066
- **Source:** Disticha Catonis I.29; own rendering from the Latin 'Quod vile est, carum, quod carum, vile putato: sic tu nec cupidus nec avarus nosceris ulli'
- **Note:** Meant as a moral. A merchant hears it as a way to haggle.

### 142. pj142 (proverb)

> Better loaded, often, than overloaded.  

- **Who says it:** english, any merchant
- **When:** written down c. 1050; older in the mouth
- **Source:** Durham Proverbs; own rendering from the Old English as remembered, 'Betere byth oft fethre thonne oferfethre' (?)
- **Note:** For a boat that carries 660 kilograms and not one more. The Old English could not be checked against the edition (?).

### 143. pj143 (proverb)

- **Who says it:** english, any merchant
- **When:** written down c. 1050; older in the mouth
- **Source:** Durham Proverbs; own rendering from the Old English as remembered, 'Hwon gelpeth se the wide sithath' (?)

### 144. pj144 (proverb)

- **Who says it:** norse, danish, any merchant

### 145. pj145 (proverb)

- **When:** sayable 793-1066; verse of the 9th-10th c. (?), written down c. 1270

- **Note:** A sailor's scrap, jumbled in the manuscript (Bellows's note). Said late in the season, when the question is one more voyage or none.

### 148. pj148 (riddle)

> I saw a thing made with skill, moving over the ground, crying out as it went. The strange thing had no sight and no hands, no shoulders and no arms. It must go on one foot, far over the fields. It had many ribs, and its mouth was in its middle. It is of use to men: it brings food in plenty to the people, and every year it pays a tribute that rich and poor both live by. Tell me, wise man, what the thing is.  

- **Answer:** A merchant ship.
- **Who says it:** english, any seafarer
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 32 (Krapp and Dobbie; 33 in Tupper 1910); own rendering from the Old English, shortened
- **Note:** The one foot is the keel, the ribs are the frames, the mouth is the open hold.

### 149. pj149 (riddle)

> A creature came walking to where many men sat at their meeting, wise in mind. It had one eye and two ears and two feet, twelve hundred heads, a back and a belly and two hands, arms and shoulders, one neck and two sides. Say what I am called.  

- **Answer:** A one-eyed man selling garlic.
- **Who says it:** english, any market
- **When:** written down c. 970 (the Exeter Book)
- **Source:** Exeter Book Riddle 86 (Krapp and Dobbie); own rendering from the Old English
- **Note:** A market joke: the monster is only the pedlar. The answer is known from the Latin riddle of Symphosius it copies (no. 94 (?)).

### 150. pj150 (riddle)

> What is a ship? A wandering house, an inn wherever you like, a traveller who leaves no footprints, a neighbour of the sand. What is the sea? The road of the bold, the edge of the land, the divider of countries, the inn of the rivers, the well of the rain.  

- **Who says it:** frankish, english, monks
- **When:** c. 790-800
- **Source:** Alcuin of York, Disputatio Pippini cum Albino; own rendering from the Latin 'Quid est navis? Domus erratica, ubilibet hospitium, viator sine vestigiis, vicinus harenae. Quid est mare? Audaciae via, limes terrae, divisor regionum, hospitium fluviorum, fons imbrium' (?)
- **Note:** A schoolmaster's ship, seen from the shore. 'A neighbour of the sand' is right for a boat that is beached every night.
