# Sea lore and seamanship, 793 to 1066

Lore file for the writers of cards, people and ports. Written 6 Oct 2026. One file, one writer.

What this is: how a skipper of about 800 found his way and read the weather with no compass and no chart; what was
said and believed at sea; the words for a ship and her handling in three tongues; the law of wreck; Ohthere's and
Wulfstan's voyages with their days and distances; and 60 sayings of weather lore.

How to read it:

- A key in square brackets, like [Bede-DTR], points to the list in section 18.
- `(?)` marks a thing I could not check against the text itself tonight. Section 17 lists them all in one place.
- `(D)` marks a thing I derived (a distance, a speed, a rule for the game). It is mine, not a source's.
- `LATER` marks a thing first written down after 1066, or true only of the later part of the game. A card set in 800
  must not use it as a known thing; a card set in 1050 often may.
- Words quoted from old texts are **our own rendering** unless a translator is named. The chest already holds
  Bosworth's Ohthere and Wulfstan (`ohthere`, `wulfstan`), the Seafarer, Dicuil, Adam, and Hávamál 81 (Bellows).

The one thing to hold on to: **in 800 nobody sails by an instrument.** A skipper sails by what he has seen before, by
what an older man told him, by the lead, the sun, the stars, the birds, the colour of the water, and by waiting. The
waiting is most of it.

## 2. Coasting and seamarks

**Sailing "by the land".** Ohthere says of the whole way from Hålogaland to Sciringes heal that a man "must sail by
the land all the while", and that he camped ashore at night [Oros-Oht]. That is the ordinary way: day sails from one
known landing to the next, the boat on the beach or at anchor by dark. Wulfstan's run to Truso, seven days and nights
under sail without stopping, is told because it was worth telling [Oros-Wulf].

**How a coast is told.** Both men give their course the same way: so many days, with this land to starboard and that
to port. Nothing else. No bearings, no distances in miles along the coast [Oros-Oht], [Oros-Wulf]. A skipper's
knowledge is a string of such lines, learned by ear. In the game this is what a "route" a person teaches should
sound like: *"Three days with Denmark on your left hand and the open sea on your right, then two days among the
islands."*

**Marks made by nature.** Headlands, the mouths of rivers, the colour of a cliff, a hill with a flat top. A study of
the Old English place-name word *ōra* found it used for hills with a level top and a rounded shoulder that show well
from the sea or the river, as at Bognor and Windsor: the name was a seaman's mark before it was a village's [Cole]
(?). On the Frisian and Flemish coast there are no cliffs: the marks are the line of the dunes, a gap in them (a
*gat*), a terp with its trees, and the church or hall on it (D).

**Marks made by men.**

**Places a seaman would not touch.** Heligoland. Alcuin, writing about 796 of Willibrord's landing there about a
century earlier, calls it Fositesland after its god: nobody dared touch the beasts that grazed there, or draw water
from its spring except in silence. Willibrord baptized three men in the spring and had cattle killed for food; King
Radbod cast lots three times a day for three days to find who should die for it, and one man did [Alc-VW]. Adam of
Bremen, about 1075, says the same island (he calls it Farria, and Heiligland) was held in awe by all sailors and
most of all by pirates, who gave the hermits there a tenth of their plunder, because no man who took the least thing
from it came home unpunished [Adam IV.3]. A landing with a rule of silence at the well is a true thing to put on the
chart.

**The pilot.** Old English *lādmann* (a guide), Old Norse *leiðsǫgumaðr* (a way-teller). Where banks shift each
winter, as in the Maas mouth, the Vlie and the Thames estuary, a stranger hires one. The spine has him already:
Gerlef of Witla, with a sounding pole taller than himself and a lead with tallow in its foot [Spine-Gerlef].

## 3. The lead, and the look and smell of the bottom

**The tool.** A weight on a line, let down until it touches. A hollow in the foot of the lead is filled with tallow,
and the tallow brings up a sample of the bottom [Lead]. In shoal water a pole does the same work faster. Old English
has words for both: *sundgyrd* (a sounding rod), *sundlīne* and *sundrāp* (a sounding line, a sounding rope), in the
glossaries of the 10th and 11th centuries [BT] (?). The Bayeux Tapestry (about 1075) shows men sounding from the bow
as the ships come in to Pevensey [Bayeux] (?).

**It is old, and every priest knew it.** Acts 27:28, Paul's ship off Malta at midnight: they "sounded, and found it
twenty fathoms: and when they had gone a little further, they sounded again, and found it fifteen fathoms" (King
James wording; in 800 it was heard in Latin). Then they dropped four anchors from the stern "and wished for the day"
[Acts]. Herodotus, a thousand years before that: a day's sail off Egypt the lead brings up mud in eleven fathoms,
which shows how far the river's silt reaches [Hdt II.5]. A monk passenger who knows his Acts can tell the skipper he
is doing what Saint Paul's shipmaster did.

**What the bottom says.** A skipper knows a stretch of sea by its depth and by what sticks to the tallow: coarse
sand, fine grey sand, shell, mud, small black stones, nothing at all (rock). The oldest northern sailing directions
that survive in writing, the Low German *Seebuch* and the English rutter of the 15th century, are full of it: so many
fathoms, and "the ground is ooze", or sand with shells, or stinks [Seebuch] LATER (?). They only wrote down what had
been taught by mouth for centuries (D). For the game: a lead cast gives two facts, a depth and a bottom, and a
skipper who knows the stretch can turn them into a place.

**The look of the water.** Things a careful man sees before the lead tells him (D, all ordinary seamanship; no
single old text lists them for the North Sea):

- Brown or milky water off a river mouth: the silt of the Rhine, Maas, Scheldt and Thames reaches miles to sea.
- A line of broken or steep water with no wind to make it: a bank, or the tide running over one.
- Green water going pale: it shoals. Pale going dark: a channel.
- Weed, eelgrass or reeds drifting: land, or a river, to windward.
- A smell of mud, smoke or cut hay on an offshore wind at night.
- The sound of surf, or of seals and birds on a bank, in fog.

**Two invented sayings in the period's manner** (not in any source): *"The lead never lies; the man who reads it
does."* and *"Sand in the wave, bank under the keel."*

## 4. Out of sight of land: the sun, the star, and latitude sailing

**How often it is needed.** A Frisian of 800 can go from Dorestad to Ribe, Lundenwic or Quentovic and hardly lose the
land. The open crossings are short: the Maas mouth to the Thames, about 110 sea miles (D); Wissant to Dover, 20. The
long open passages (Norway to Shetland, the Faroes, Iceland from about 870, Greenland from about 985) are the
Northmen's, and later.

**The sun.** At noon it stands in the south, and that is the one sure direction in a day. Its height at noon tells
how far north a man is, if he remembers how high it stood at home at the same time of year. The Old English Rune
Poem: "The sun is ever a hope to seamen, when they fare over the fish's bath, until the sea-horse brings them to
land" (our rendering) [OE-Rune]. In Iceland in the 12th century a farm labourer, Oddi Helgason, called Star-Oddi,
set out in a short text how the sun's noon height rises and falls week by week through the year, in units of the
sun's own width, and where on the horizon dawn shows [Oddi] (?) LATER. It shows what kind of knowing this was: kept
in the head, counted in hand-breadths and sun-widths.

**The star.** Old English *scipsteorra*, "ship-star", is the pole star [BT] (?); Old Norse *leiðarstjarna*,
"way-star" (the same word as English "lodestar"). The Rune Poem's verse for the rune Tir: "it keeps faith well with
princes; it is always on its course over the mists of night; it never fails" (our rendering) [OE-Rune]. In the
northern summer the sky is too light to show it for weeks; that is one more reason the long runs need luck.

**Latitude sailing.** The one written northern sailing direction for open sea is in the Hauksbók copy of the Book of
Settlements (written 1306 to 1308, of older matter): "From Hernar in Norway one is to sail due west to Hvarf in
Greenland; and then one sails north of Shetland so that it is only seen if the sea-view is very clear, and south of
the Faroes so that the sea is half-way up the slopes, and south of Iceland so that they have birds and whales from
it" (our rendering) [Landn-H] (?) LATER. That is the whole method: hold one height of the sun, run along it, and
check off the islands by how much of them shows. It belongs to the game's years after about 985.

**Days' sailing.** The same book gives the open-sea runs in *dœgr sigling*, a "day's sail": seven from Stad in Norway
west to Horn in east Iceland; four from Snæfellsnes west to Greenland; five from Reykjanes south to Ireland (one
manuscript says three); four from Langanes north to Svalbard; one from Kolbeinsey north to the wastes of Greenland
[Landn] (?). A *dœgr* is 12 hours, or loosely a day and a night; scholars do not agree, so do not turn these into
speeds. Along the coast the Norse unit is the *vika sjávar*, the "sea week", the stretch rowed between changes at
the oar: later reckoned at about 4 to 5 sea miles [Vika] (?).

**The airts.** Old Norse names the eight directions from the west coast of Norway, where the sea is west and the
land east: *norðr, austr, suðr, vestr*, and between them *landnorðr* (north-east, "land-north"), *landsuðr*
(south-east), *útsuðr* (south-west, "out-south") and *útnorðr* (north-west). Icelanders kept the names though their
land lies the other way [CV]. Old English names winds by where they come from: *norðanwind, ēastanwind,
sūðanwind, westanwind*, and Ohthere waits for "a west wind and somewhat north" [Oros-Oht]. Charlemagne gave the
twelve winds Frankish names in place of the Latin ones, Einhard says: *ostroniwint, ostsundroni, sundostroni,
sundroni, sundwestroni, westsundroni, westroni, westnordroni, nordwestroni, nordroni, nordostroni, ostnordroni*
[Einh c. 29] (?). A Frankish toll man might use them; a Frisian skipper says east, south, west and north.

**Being lost.** *Hafvilla*, "sea-bewilderment". The Greenlanders' saga tells it of Bjarni Herjólfsson, about 986:
the fair wind dropped, north winds and fog came down, "and they did not know where they were going, and that went
on many days. After that they saw the sun and could tell the airts" (our rendering) [Graen] (?). He found a land
nobody had seen, did not go ashore, and was thought incurious for it. For the game: fog and a week of overcast are
the two things that take a ship's place from her; when the sun shows she gets her north back but not her place.

**The "sunstone" and the "sun compass": do not use them as fact.**
- *Sólarsteinn* is named in a tale about Saint Olaf (*Rauðúlfs þáttr*, 13th or 14th century), where the king holds
  a stone up under a snowing sky and sees where the sun is, and in Icelandic church inventories of the 14th and 15th
  centuries. No text shows one used to steer a ship, and a trial at sea in 2014 did not make it work [Sunstone].
- The Uunartoq disc, half a small wooden disc with scratched lines, found in 1948 in a Norse ruin in Greenland
  (about 1000 (?)), has been read as a sun compass and as nothing of the kind [Sunstone] (?).
- For the game: a "sunstone" may be a rare and doubtful thing a Northman shows after 1000, with odds of "doubtful".
  It must never be in a Frisian's boat in 800.

## 5. Birds, whales and swell

**Birds find land.** Flóki Vilgerðarson, sailing from the Faroes to look for Iceland in the 860s, took three ravens.
The first flew back astern, the second went up and came back to the ship, the third flew on ahead and they followed
it to land. He is "Raven-Flóki" for it [Landn], [WP-Floki]. Whether or not it happened, it is how men of the 12th
century thought their grandfathers had sailed, and Genesis 8 (Noah's raven and dove) taught every Christian the same
trick.

**Birds mark land that is out of sight.** The Hauksbók course passes Iceland far enough off that the land is under
the horizon, and knows it by "birds and whales" [Landn-H]. In plain terms (D):

- Gulls and terns work within a day's flight of a shore and go home at dusk. The line they fly at evening points to
  land; at dawn, away from it.
- Auks and puffins flying low and straight with fish in the bill, in summer, are going to their cliff.
- Gannets and fulmars range far: they tell less.
- Land birds blown out to sea come aboard to rest, and tell only that a wind off the land has been blowing.

**The birds an English seaman knew by name** are in the Seafarer (in the chest): the swan's song, the gannet's cry
(*ganetes hlēoþor*), the curlew's (*huilpan swēg*), the gull (*mǣw*) singing in place of mead-drinking, the tern
(*stearn*) icy-feathered, the eagle wet-winged. Old English poetry calls the sea *ganotes bæð*, the gannet's bath,
*hronrād*, the whale's road, and *swanrād*, the swan's road [Seaf], [Beow].

**Whales.** Feeding grounds are fixed places: banks and the edges of deep water. The men who sailed the same line
each year knew where they would see them. Ohthere knows how far north the whale hunters go (three days), the size of
the whales off his own coast (48 ells, the biggest 50) and the walrus, hunted for its teeth and its hide [Oros-Oht].
The King's Mirror (about 1250) lists the whales of the Iceland sea by kind, with which are good to eat and which
sink ships [KM] LATER.

**Swell.** A long, slow, even swell with no wind comes from a storm that is somewhere else, and often comes ahead of
it. A swell keeps its line when the wind shifts, so in fog a man who noted its line before the fog can steer by the
way the boat lifts to it. Over a bank the swell stands up short and steep. No northern text of these years says
this; it is ordinary seamanship, and the King's Mirror's *hafgerðingar*, the "sea-hedges" of the Greenland sea, three
great waves that close in on a ship, are the one swell the old writers thought worth a name [KM] LATER (D).

**Cloud and light.** A still heap of cloud low on the horizon on a clear day often stands over land. Northmen bound
for Greenland knew the white glare thrown up into the sky by ice (D).

**The great whirlpool.** Paul the Deacon, a Lombard at Charlemagne's court, writing about 790, puts "the navel of
the sea" off the western shore of the far north: it sucks the waves in and spews them out twice a day, and pulls
ships in "as swiftly as arrows"; he says there is another between Britain and the Gaulish coast [Paul I.6] (?).
Adam of Bremen tells of Frisian nobles who sailed north from the Weser in the 1040s, past Orkney and Iceland, into
darkness and a current that ran back into "a deep chasm", lost ships in it, and came home to tell Archbishop
Alebrand at Bremen [Adam IV.40-41] (?). So: in 793 an educated Frank believes in the whirlpool, and a Frisian in an
ale-house can be told of it as fact.

## 6. Tides, and how they were reckoned

**What every coast man knew without books.** The sea comes and goes twice in a day and a night. It is a little
later every day. It runs high and hard about new and full moon and slack about the half moons. A gale from the sea
piles it up; a wind off the land holds it down.

**Bede wrote it down.** *The Reckoning of Time* (725), chapter 29, "On the harmony of the sea and the moon", is the
best account of the tides written before the 17th century. A copy stood in every good Carolingian library by 800.
What it says [Bede-DTR] (the numbers checked from memory against the Latin, not tonight against the page (?)):

1. **The moon does it.** The sea follows the moon "as if drawn on by certain breathings of the moon against its
   will" (our rendering).
2. **Later each day.** The moon rises and sets later each day by four *puncti*, four fifths of an hour, and the tide
   keeps step: each tide comes later than the one before by "three quarters of an hour and a half-ounce", about
   47 and a half minutes by our clock.
3. **A count.** In a lunar month the sea rises and falls 57 times; in a lunar year 684 times.
4. **Springs and neaps.** He has names for them, *malinae* (the growing tides) and *ledones* (the lesser), taking
   turns about every seven or eight days, the *malinae* about new and full moon. They are older words than Bede,
   from Gaul; nobody knows their root.
5. **Wind.** A wind can hold a tide back or bring it on before its hour.
6. **Not the same hour everywhere.** This is his own, and nobody had written it before: "we who live along the
   much-divided shore of the British sea know that where the tide begins to rise in one place, it begins to fall in
   another" (our rendering). Each harbour has its own lag behind the moon. Later seamen called this the
   "establishment of the port".
7. **Nineteen years.** After nineteen years the moon and the tides come round to the same days again.

**Bede on a real place.** In the Church History (IV.16) he says that in the strait between Wight and the mainland
"two tides of the ocean, which break out of the boundless northern sea round Britain, meet daily in conflict" beyond
the mouth of the Hamble, and after their struggle flow back into the ocean they came from (our rendering)
[Bede-HE]. The double high water of the Solent, seen and explained in 731.

**How a skipper used it** (D, from the above). He does not count minutes. He knows, for each harbour he uses, where
the moon stands when the water is highest there, and he knows the moon's age by looking at her. So:

- *"At Dorestad nobody cares: the river runs one way."*
- *"At Witla you cross the bar when a full moon is in the south-east."* (The bearing is invented; the kind of rule
  is real, and is first written down in the 14th and 15th centuries as "moon south, high water at..." [Seebuch]
  LATER (?).)
- Without the moon in sight he counts: new moon was six nights ago, so the morning high water is some four and a
  half hours later than it was then.

**Tides that decided things** (for cards):

- 896: King Alfred's new long ships caught six Danish ships in a river mouth on the south coast. The English ships
  went aground on the ebb, three on one side of the channel and the rest on the other; the Danes walked across the
  sand to fight at low water, and when the flood came it reached the Danish ships first and they rowed out
  [ASC 897 in Giles's dating].
- About 666: Bishop Wilfrid's ship was thrown on the South Saxon shore and the sea went out and left her dry. The
  heathen came for the wreck. Their priest stood on a high mound to curse the ship's company and bind their hands
  by his craft; one of Wilfrid's men slung a stone and killed him. They fought three times, and the tide came back
  "before its usual hour", floated her, and they reached Sandwich [VW c. 13] (?).
- The old Frisian laws (written down from the 13th century, older in matter) say a Frisian need follow the king's
  call no further than the Weser to the east and the Fli to the west, "out with the ebb and up with the flood",
  because he must guard his shore every day against the salt sea and the wild Northman [OFris-17] (?). The tide as
  the measure of a man's duty.

**An invented saying in the period's manner:** *"The tide waits for no bishop."* (The English "time and tide wait
for no man" is first found about 1225 (?), and "tide" there meant "season".)

## 7. The bar

Every river of the game ends in one: a bank of sand thrown up where the river's stream meets the sea's. It is the
shallowest water of the whole voyage and the last.

- **It moves.** A winter's gales or a spring flood shift the channel. Last year's marks may be wrong. This is why a
  pilot at a river mouth is worth his pennies [Spine-Gerlef].
- **It is crossed on the last of the flood,** when the water is deepest and still running in with the boat. Coming
  out, at the top of the tide, before the ebb runs hard.
- **Wind against tide.** When the ebb runs out against a wind from the sea the waves on the bar stand up and break,
  in weather that is easy a mile outside. More small boats are lost here than at sea (D).
- **Waiting outside.** A boat that comes to the bar at the wrong tide anchors off or stands away, and that is when
  a wind from the sea catches her on a lee shore.
- **A boat like the player's** (7.7 m, drawing well under half a metre loaded (D, see `ships.md`)) can cross where
  a knarr cannot, and can be beached. That is her one great advantage on this coast and the game should let the
  player feel it.

The 794 raid shows what a river mouth could be for strangers: the heathen plundered the monastery at the mouth of
the Don (Jarrow, or a house on the Don), a leader was killed, "some of their ships were broken to pieces by bad
weather, and many of the men drowned, and some came alive to shore and were quickly slain at the river's mouth"
(our rendering) [ASC 794].

Words: Old English *mūða* (a river mouth: Wislemūða, Yarmouth), Old Norse *áróss* (river mouth: Aarhus is
*Áróss*), *eyrr* (a gravel spit), *grunn* (a shoal). The word "bar" itself is LATER (French).

## 8. The sailing season

**The old rule.** Vegetius, a Roman of about 400 whose book was copied all through the Carolingian age (Hrabanus
Maurus made an abridgement of it about 855), gives the Mediterranean calendar [Veg IV.39]:

| Dates | The sea is |
|---|---|
| 27 May to 14 September | safe |
| 10 March to 27 May, and 14 September to 11 November | doubtful |
| 11 November (Martinmas) to 10 March | closed |

**The northern rule, written later.** The King's Mirror, a Norwegian father's advice to his son (about 1250), says
a man should hardly go on the open sea after the beginning of October, because the sea grows restless then and the
days short, and may put out again about the beginning of April [KM] (?) LATER. It also says: have the ship ready
when summer begins and travel while the season is best; never be at sea in late autumn if it can be helped [KM c. 4]
(?). The Norse year has only two seasons and they fit this: summer begins on a Thursday in mid April, winter on a
Saturday in mid October ("the winter nights") [Norse-Cal] (?).

**What the sources of the game's own years show.**

- Fleets "sat" over winter. The Chronicle's great change is 851: "the heathen men for the first time stayed over
  winter" (on Thanet; Sheppey in 855) [ASC]. Before that, raiders and traders alike went home in autumn.
- Late-season sailing was done when the fair was worth it. Frisians and Saxons came to the fair of Saint Denis by
  Paris, which opened on 9 October, the saint's feast; King Pippin's charter of 753 names them [StDenis] (?). So a
  Frisian's last voyage of the year might be up the Seine in October and home along the coast in November.
- Autumn gales took whole fleets. 877: the Danish ship-army, sailing west from Wareham, met a great storm at sea,
  "and there perished 120 ships at Swanwich" [ASC 877, Giles]. 1009: a storm threw eighty of King Æthelred's new
  ships ashore and broke them [ASC 1009] (?).
- Ice. The Baltic ports, the Schlei and the inner Danish waters freeze in a hard winter; the Rhine at Dorestad
  carries ice and floods. Hávamál's "praise ice when it is crossed" is practical advice (saying 28).
- The English poem of the Seafarer makes the cuckoo the sign: the woods blossom, the fields grow fair, and "the
  cuckoo urges with its sad voice, summer's guard sings" and sends the heart to sea [Seaf 48-55].

**For the game (D).** Use feasts, not dates, when a person speaks (BRIEF rule 2):

## 9. Laying up, and fitting out

**Out of the water.** A boat left afloat in winter is eaten by ice and worm and strained by gales. She is drawn up.
Old Norse has the phrases: *setja upp* (to draw a ship up), *setja fram* (to launch), *ráða skipi til hlunns* (to
bring a ship to the rollers, that is, to lay her up); *hlunnr* is the roller of wood she slides on, and *naust* the
boat-house, a long shed open to the shore, of which hundreds of foundations line the Norwegian coast [CV],
[Naust] (?). On the Frisian coast and at Dorestad there is no rock: a boat is hauled on to the bank or the foot of
the terp and propped (D).

**The autumn work.** The King's Mirror: "when autumn comes, tar your ship well and keep her tarred through the
winter" (our rendering) [KM c. 4] (?) LATER. In order (D, with `ships.md` section 7.3 for the detail):

1. Unstep the mast, strip the rigging, take the sail home dry. A wool sail rots if stored damp.
2. Scrub off weed. Look at every plank end, rivet and the lashings or nails of the ribs.
3. Drive fresh caulking (tarred wool, hair or moss) into any seam that weeps.
4. Tar inside and out with pine tar. Tar is a trade good: it comes from the pine lands of the east and north.
5. Cover her from sun and snow with her own tent or with brushwood.

**The spring work.** Dress the sail again (the replicas use tallow or fish oil with ochre, which is why a sail is
red-brown and not white [VSM] (?)). New ropes of lime bast, hemp, or, best, walrus or seal hide, which Ohthere took
as tribute in lengths of sixty ells [Oros-Oht]. Launch on a spring tide, and let her take up: a boat dry all winter
leaks for a day or two until her planks swell.

**What to carry.** The King's Mirror's list for a merchant: two or three hundred ells of wadmal fit for mending the
sail, many needles, thread and cord; nails, both spikes and rivets, of the sizes the ship needs; boat-hooks, broad
axes, gouges and augers [KM c. 4] (?) LATER. The Dorestad boat of 800 carries less, but the same kinds of thing.

**Winter as play.** The clock does not stop for winter. What a merchant does with it: mends, tars, lends and
collects, hears the news that came in with the last ships (now months old), marries off a daughter, hears the law
read, and waits for the cuckoo.

## 10. Reading the weather

**No theory, only signs.** Nobody in 800 knows why weather comes. They know what comes before it. The learned had
lists of signs from the Romans: Virgil's first Georgic (read in every cathedral school), Pliny's Natural History
book 18, Isidore of Seville's *On the Nature of Things*, and Bede's book of the same name (about 703), whose chapter
on the signs of storm and fair weather is Isidore's and Pliny's list handed on [Virg], [Pliny], [Isid], [Bede-DNR]
(?). Seamen had the same signs without the Latin. The 60 sayings in section 15 are the working list.

**What a skipper looks at, in order of worth** (D):

1. **The wind's shift.** On this coast bad weather comes in from the west and south-west, and a gale's wind goes
   round from south to west to north-west as it passes. A wind that shifts against the sun's path (west back to
   south) means more is coming.
2. **The sky at dusk and dawn.** Red in the west at evening: the weather to windward is clear. Red in the east at
   morning with cloud coming over: it is not. Matthew 16 says so, and it is right more often than not here.
3. **High cloud.** Thin streaks and a sky like fish scales come a day ahead of rain and wind.
4. **Rings.** A ring round the sun or moon: rain within a day, more often than not.
5. **The swell,** as in section 5.
6. **How far he can see.** Land standing unnaturally clear and near often comes before rain.
7. **Birds and beasts.** Gulls sitting inland, swallows low, porpoises playing. Half true at best; everybody
   believed them.
8. **The moon's horns, shooting stars, the day of the week it thundered.** Not true. Believed by the learned most
   of all.

**Signs in the heavens that were not weather.** The Chronicle for 793: "dire forewarnings came over the land of the
Northumbrians, and woefully terrified the people: these were immense whirlwinds and flashes of lightning, and fiery
dragons were seen flying in the air. A great famine soon followed these tokens; and a little after that... the
harrying of the heathen men destroyed God's church at Lindisfarne" (the text is in the chest as `chronicle_793`).
Comets, a red cross in the sky after sunset (776), eclipses: all are entered beside the deaths of kings [ASC]. A
crew that sees the northern lights from the Frisian coast, where they are rare, will take them for fire-dragons.

**Fog.** The North Sea's spring and early-summer fog comes with a mild, damp wind over cold water and lies until
the wind changes; an autumn morning's river fog burns off by the third hour (D). In fog a coaster anchors if he
can reach bottom, and listens.

## 11. What was said and believed at sea

Read this section with BRIEF rule 2 in mind. Much of what "everybody knows" about seamen's beliefs (no whistling, no
women aboard, no sailing on a Friday, a ghostly drowned man in half a boat) is first written down many centuries
after 1066. It is marked LATER here and should stay out of cards before 1066, or be used with a `(?)`.

### 11.1 Heathen: the sea's own powers

| Who or what | What the texts say | Source and its date |
|---|---|---|
| **Njǫrðr** | He lives at Nóatún, "ship-yard". "He rules the course of the wind and stills sea and fire; on him men should call for sea-faring and for fishing." He is so rich that he can give land and goods to those who call on him. | Snorri, Gylfaginning 23 (about 1220), our rendering [Snorri] |
| **Ægir** | The sea itself as a giant and a host: the gods feast in his hall. Poets call the sea his, and ale "Ægir's brewing" is not said, but his hall is lit by gold, so gold is "Ægir's fire". | Snorri, Skáldskaparmál; Lokasenna [Snorri] (?) |

**Use.** In 793 nobody had written any of this down. A Dane or Northman of 800 surely named Njǫrðr and Thor at sea
(place-names in Njarð- and Þór- line the Norwegian coast [NjPlace] (?)); what he *said* is ours to write, in plain
words: *"Njord gives the wind. Ran takes what falls overboard. You keep on the right side of both."*

**The Frisians' own gods** are almost unrecorded. Fosite of Heligoland is one (section 2). The other is a thing a
curious player can find: at Domburg on Walcheren and in the Eastern Scheldt, Roman altars to the goddess
**Nehalennia**, set up about 150 to 250 by merchants and skippers on the Britain run, with a dog, a basket of
fruit and a ship's prow, and the words "for goods kept safe" (*ob merces recte conservatas*) [Nehal] (?). The
Domburg altars came out of the dunes after a storm in 1647. In 800 they are under the sand; a card may let a storm
uncover one stone, and nobody on Walichrum can read it but a priest.

### 11.2 Christian: who a sailor called on

**In 800 it is not Saint Nicholas.** His feast is kept in the East, and he comes north with the Greek empress
Theophanu after 972; his great fame in the West begins when sailors of Bari carried his bones off from Myra in
1087 [Nich] (?). **Use him only from about 1000, and as a new thing.**

**Saint Clement** is nearer, and later than one would think. Clement, the pope, was drowned in the Black Sea with
an anchor tied to his neck, the legend says, and the sea drew back each year to show his tomb. Cyril and Methodius
brought what were said to be his bones to Rome in 867 or 868 [Clem] (?). His churches then stand in the harbours of
the Northmen's new Christian towns of about 1000 to 1050: Trondheim (the sagas give Olaf Tryggvason a Clement's
church there about 997), Oslo, Aarhus, Roskilde, Schleswig, Visby, and Saint Clement Danes outside London's wall
[Crawford] (?). **Use him from about 990, as the Danes' and Northmen's own sea saint.** After 1030 Saint Olaf joins
him.

**Whom a Christian of 800 did call on:**

| Saint or prayer | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Christ in the boat | He slept in the storm, was woken, "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, Peace, be still" (Mark 4:39, King James wording). Read at Mass every year. | [Mark] |
| Peter | A fisherman, who walked on the water and sank. The patron of every minster dedicated to him on a shore (Wearmouth, Dorestad's Upkirika was the bishop's (?)). | [Matt 14] |
| Mary | The hymn *Ave maris stella*, "Hail, star of the sea", is in a 9th-century book of Saint Gall. A monk aboard might sing it; a skipper has not heard it in 793. | [AMS] (?) |
| Martin | The Franks' great saint. His feast, 11 November, closes the sea. | [Veg] (D) |
| Willibrord (died 739) | The Frisians' own apostle, who sailed to the Danes and was driven on Heligoland. Buried at Echternach; the church at Utrecht is his. | [Alc-VW] |
| Boniface (killed at Dokkum, 754) | Went by boat down the Rhine and over the Almere to his death. His body came back up the river to Utrecht and on to Fulda. | [VBon] (?) |
| Cuthbert (died 687) | As a boy he saw monks' timber rafts swept out of the Tyne mouth to sea on the ebb while the heathen crowd on the bank jeered; he knelt, and the wind turned. Northumbrian sailors are his. | Bede, Life of Cuthbert c. 3 [Bede-VC] (?) |
| Columba (died 597) | Adomnán's Life is full of it: winds turned by prayer, a whale foretold, the whirlpool of Brecan. The Irish and the men of the Isles are his. | [Adom] (?) |
| Brendan | The Voyage of Saint Brendan (written by about 800 (?)): Easter Mass on the back of a whale, an island of birds that sing the psalms. Told as truth in any Irish house. | [Nav] (?) |

**Oil on the water.** Bede has it from a priest who had it from the man himself: Utta, sent by sea about 651 to
fetch King Oswiu's bride from Kent, was given a flask of blessed oil by Bishop Aidan and told a storm would come;
when the anchors dragged and the ship was filling, he poured the oil on the sea and it grew calm [Bede-HE III.15].
Oil does smooth breaking water. A flask of blessed oil is a true and useful thing for a priest to give the player.

**Storms sent, storms stilled.** Germanus of Auxerre, crossing the Channel in 429, was woken in a storm raised by
devils and sprinkled the waves with water in the name of the Trinity [Bede-HE I.17]. Æthelwald, the hermit of
Farne, stilled a storm for monks rowing home about 699 [Bede-HE V.1]. Ecgberht, meaning to sail to the Frisians and
Saxons, was warned by a vision, and then by a storm that laid his loaded ship on her side at the quay: he stayed,
and Willibrord went in his place [Bede-HE V.9].

**The Dead.** A drowned Christian wants burial in a churchyard and Masses. A body found on the shore is buried by
those who find it; the later Norse church laws make it a duty [Gul] (?) LATER. The Seafarer and the Wanderer (both
in the chest) give the mood: an exile at sea talks to his dead lord in a dream and wakes to see the gulls bathing.

### 11.3 Vows, lots and offerings

**The vow.** In danger a man promises something if he lives: a fast, alms, a candle of his own weight in wax, a
pilgrimage, a gift to a named church. It is the commonest act of religion at sea and it binds. The best account is
of heathens: about 854 the Svear besieging a town of the Curonians cast lots, found that none of their own gods
would help, and at the word of merchants who had heard Anskar preach vowed to Christ a fast of forty days and alms;
the town yielded, and when they came home they kept the fast [Rimb c. 30] (Robinson 1921 is the safe translation).
The engine has the act already: `act:vow`.

**Lots.** Cast to find the god's will before any voyage or raid. Rimbert again: the exiled king Anund's Danes at
Birka cast lots whether to sack it, and the lot said no; they cast again for another town and went there
[Rimb c. 19]. Radbod's lots on Heligoland (section 2) chose who should die. And every Christian knew Jonah 1:7: in
the storm "they cast lots, and the lot fell upon Jonah", and they threw him over, and the sea was still. **A crew
that casts lots to find who has brought the bad luck is doing a thing both faiths understand.** The engine has
`act:ask_omen`.

**Jettison.** Jonah 1:5: "the mariners were afraid, and cried every man unto his god, and cast forth the wares that
were in the ship into the sea, to lighten it." Roman law (the "Rhodian law of jettison", Digest 14.2) says that
what is thrown over to save the ship is a loss shared by all whose goods were saved [Dig] (?). Whether a Frisian of
800 knew the rule is unknown; partners in a boat would have their own custom. A card may put the choice plainly:
whose bale goes over?

**Offerings.** A first share of the catch or the profit to the god or the saint. Adam's pirates gave a tenth to
the hermits of Heligoland [Adam IV.3]. Rimbert says the Svear, falling back into heathen ways, made vows and
offerings to their gods for a good voyage [Rimb] (?).

### 11.4 Omens, and names not spoken

**Attested for these years:**

- Signs in the sky (section 10). Whirlwinds, lightning, "fiery dragons", comets.
- The day the year began on, and the day of the first thunder. Old English books of the 11th century carry lists:
  if the first day of January is a Sunday, the winter will be good and the spring windy; if the first thunder of
  the year comes on a Sunday, it betokens the death of kings' children; and so on through the week [Prog] (?).
  A monk's lore, from Latin.
- Unlucky days. Three "Egyptian days" in the year, and two in every month, on which no work should be begun and
  no blood let, are listed in English books of the 10th and 11th centuries [Prog] (?). A priest might warn a
  skipper off sailing on one.
- Birds. Ravens to the Northmen (Flóki; the raven banner that flew or drooped, at Ashingdon in 1016 in the
  *Encomium Emmae*, and in the Chronicle for 878 [ASC 878]). Tacitus says the Germans of his day took omens from
  the cries and flight of birds [Tac] (?).
- Dreams. The sagas are full of them, written later.
- The drowned at their own funeral feast. In the saga of the people of Eyr, Thorodd and his men, drowned off the
  coast about 1000, walk dripping into their own funeral ale night after night. "Men held it a good sign" at first,
  "that drowned men were well received by Rán if they came to their own funeral feast; for there was still a little
  of the old belief, though men were baptized" (our rendering) [Eyrb c. 54] (?) LATER as a text.

**Names not spoken.** The custom of never saying certain words at sea, and using other words for them (for the
land animals, for the priest and the church, for the knife, for the fish itself), is recorded among Shetland, Faroe
and Norwegian fishermen from the 17th to the 19th centuries. Many of the Shetland sea-words are plain Old Norse that
had died out ashore [Jak] (?) LATER. Nothing proves it for 800. What *is* old is the habit of second names for
everything at sea. The poem Alvíssmál gives each thing a name among men, gods, giants, elves and dwarves [Alv] (?):

| Thing | Men say | Others say |
|---|---|---|
| the sea | *sær* | *sílægja* ("ever-lying", the gods), *vágr* (the Vanir), *álheimr* ("eel-home", giants), *lagastafr* (elves), *djúpr marr* ("the deep sea", dwarves) |
| the wind | *vindr* | *váfuðr* ("waverer"), *gneggjuðr* ("neigher"), *œpir* ("wailer"), *dynfari* ("din-farer"), *hviðuðr* ("squaller") |
| calm | *logn* | *lægi* ("the lying"), *vindslot* ("wind's end"), *ofhlý* ("the sultry"), *dagsevi* ("day's ease"), *dagsvera* ("day's stay") |
| cloud | *ský* | *skúrván* ("shower-hope"), *vindflot* ("wind-floater"), *úrván* ("drizzle-hope"), *veðrmegin* ("weather-might"), *hjálmr huliðs* ("helm of hiding") |

**For the game:** a Norse or Danish crewman may refuse to name the wind by its name while becalmed, and call it
"the waverer" instead. Mark such a line `(?)` in its note. It is in the spirit of the sources and is not in them.

**LATER, keep out before 1066** (first recorded long after): no whistling aboard; no women aboard; no priests
aboard; never sail on a Friday; the *draugr* in half a boat; Saint Elmo's fire by that name (Pliny has the thing:
one flame on the yard is deadly and is called Helena, two are Castor and Pollux and are safe [Pliny II.101]); the
mermaid as an omen; a caul against drowning.

### 11.5 The wife on the shore

The Old English Maxims (the Exeter Book, copied about 970, the verses older) have the one picture of a Frisian
seaman's homecoming in all the literature of the age [Max-I, lines 94-99]. Our rendering:

> Dear is the welcome one to the Frisian wife when the ship stands in.
> His keel has come and her man is home, her own provider;
> and she calls him in, washes his sea-stained clothes and gives him fresh ones,
> and gives him on land what his love asks.

It goes on to say that a wife should keep faith with her man, that many are steadfast and many are curious and love
strangers when he is far away, and that "a sailor is long on his voyage, yet one must always look for the loved
one". This is the player's own house. It should be quoted somewhere in Dorestad.

## 12. The words

**How far to trust each column.**

- **Old English** is written in the game's own years. Ohthere's and Wulfstan's accounts (about 890) use *steorbord,
  bæcbord, segl, wīcian, ambyrne wind* as working words. The rest is from poetry and from glossaries, chiefly
  Ælfric's of about 995 [BT].
- **Old Norse** is written down from the 12th and 13th centuries, but the skalds' verses of the 9th to 11th
  centuries use the same ship words, and the rune stones have *skip, knǫrr, stýrimaðr, drengr* [CV], [Jesch] (?).
- **Old Frisian is not written until the 13th century**, in law books. It has few ship words because laws seldom
  need them. Where the Frisian column is blank, nothing is recorded; a form with a star is my guess from the
  sister tongues and must not be shown to the player as attested. In 800 Frisian and English seamen could still
  largely follow each other (the game's tongue table treats them as close), so an English form heard in a Frisian
  mouth is not a mistake.

Do not put these words in a person's mouth as decoration (BRIEF rule 3). They are for names of things on a panel,
for a word a foreigner uses and the player learns (`learn`, `tongue`), and for the artist's notes.

## 13. Wreck and salvage law

**The plain state of things in 800: there is very little written law, and the custom is hard.** What the sea
throws up belongs to the lord of the shore, or to whoever is on the beach. A stranded stranger has no kin and no
lord there; unless a king's peace, a treaty or a church protects him, he and his goods are fair game. Most of what
is written is kings and churches trying to soften that.

and lagan as law; the Church's ban on wrecking (the Lateran council of 1179).

**For the game (D).** Three questions settle a stranding card: Is she afloat or aground? Is anyone alive aboard to
claim her? Whose shore is it, and does the player hold his peace (a charter, a truce, a friend, a church's
protection)? Reputation and charters should move the odds by a word. And the mirror: when the player finds a wreck
or a drifting bale, the same law makes it the shore lord's, the church's or his own, and each choice is a deed
people remember.

**A wreck in the player's own trade.** The loss of the boat is the house's worst day and the BRIEF says the house
goes on. The true forms of relief in these years: partners share a loss (*félag*); a lord or a church may lend
again against a vow or a pledge; kin take the children. There is no insurance and no sea loan in the north before
the 12th century (?).

## 14. Ohthere's and Wulfstan's voyages, with their days and distances

Both were told to King Alfred and written into the Old English version of Orosius's history, about 890. The oldest
copy (British Library Additional 47967, about 892 to 925) has lost the leaves with Wulfstan and the end of Ohthere;
those are known from a copy of the 11th century (Cotton Tiberius B.i) [WP-Oht]. The chest has both texts in Old
English with Bosworth's translation of 1859 (`ohthere`, `wulfstan`); quote from there.

All distances below are mine, measured roughly along the likely track, in sea miles: **(D) (?) throughout**. Where
they repeat `ships.md` section 5.2 they agree with it.

### 14.1 Ohthere, north: round the North Cape to the White Sea

He lived "furthest north of all Northmen", on the coast of Hålogaland; where exactly is not known (the Tromsø
district is the usual guess (?)). He went, he said, to find out how far the land ran north and whether anyone lived
north of the waste; and for the walrus.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | North along the land, waste land to starboard, open sea to port, as far as the whale hunters go | 3 | from his home toward Sørøya | about 100 | about 35 |
| 2 | North again as far as he could sail in three days | 3 | to the North Cape | about 100 | about 35 |
| wait | The land bent east, "or the sea into the land, he knew not which". He waited for a west wind and somewhat north. | not said | | | |
| 3 | East along the land | 4 | North Cape to the mouth of the White Sea | about 350 to 400 | about 90 to 100 |
| wait | The land bent south. He waited for a wind from due north. | not said | | | |

| end | "A great river lay up into the land." They turned into it and dared not sail past, "for unpeace": the far bank was all settled, by the Beormas. | | | | |

What the legs show: on legs 1, 2 and 4 he coasts by day and makes 30 to 60 miles; on leg 3, along a shore with
nowhere he wished to land, in midsummer when it is light all night, he ran day and night and made three times that.
He changed course only twice, and each time he had to wait for a new wind: **a square-sailed ship goes where the
wind lets her and waits when it does not.**

What he saw and said (for people and cards):

- All the way the land to starboard was empty but for fishers, fowlers and hunters, "and they were all Finnas"
  (Sami). The Beormas told him many tales of their own land and the lands about, "but he did not know what was
  true, because he had not seen it himself". He thought the Finnas and the Beormas spoke nearly one tongue.
- He went chiefly for the walrus ("horse-whales"), "because they have very fine bone in their teeth", and he
  brought some of the teeth to the king; "and their hide is very good for ship-ropes".
- He was among the first men of that land, yet had no more than twenty cattle, twenty sheep and twenty swine, and
  "the little that he ploughed he ploughed with horses". His wealth was six hundred tame reindeer, six of them
  decoys, and the tribute of the Finnas: skins, feathers, whale-bone, and ship-ropes of whale and seal hide. The
  highest-born pays fifteen marten skins, five reindeer, one bear skin, ten measures of feathers, a coat of bear or
  otter skin, and two ship-ropes, each sixty ells long.
- The land of the Northmen is "very long and very narrow": all that can be grazed or ploughed lies by the sea, and
  wild moors lie to the east above it.

### 14.2 Ohthere, south: Hålogaland to Sciringes heal to Hedeby

| Leg | His words | Days | Distance | Per day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Home to Sciringes heal (Kaupang in Vestfold) | A man could sail there "in a month, if he camped at night and had each day a fair wind; and all the while he must sail by the land". To starboard first "Iraland", then the islands between Iraland and Britain, then Britain; to port, Norway all the way. | 30 or so | about 950 to 1,000 | about 32 |
| Sciringes heal to Hedeby (*æt Hæþum*) | Five days. For three, Denmark to port and the open sea to starboard. Then for two, Jutland, Sillende and many islands to starboard ("in those lands the English dwelt before they came hither"), and to port the islands that belong to Denmark. | 5 | about 380 | 76 if he sailed by day only; about 3.2 knots if day and night |

Three cautions:

1. **"In a month" or "not in a month".** The Old English as it stands (and as the chest's `original` has it) says a
   man *could* sail it in a month. Bosworth's translation in the chest says he "could not". Bosworth altered the
   text. Follow the Old English: a month, with a fair wind every day, which is the best case and not the usual one.
2. ***Ambyrne wind*** is found nowhere else. Most read "fair"; a few read "contrary" [WP-Oht].
3. "Denmark" to port for three days is the first time the name is written (*Denamearc*): he means the lands of the
   Danes east of the Kattegat, now the west coast of Sweden.

Of Hedeby he says only that it "stands between the Wends and the Saxons and the Angles, and belongs to the Danes".

### 14.3 Wulfstan: Hedeby to Truso

| | |
|---|---|
| Time | "Seven days and nights", "the ship running under sail all the way". No night camps. |
| To starboard | Wendland, the whole way to the mouth of the Vistula. |
| To port, in order | Langeland, Lolland, Falster and Skåne, "and all these lands belong to Denmark". Then Bornholm ("the land of the Burgendas"), "who have their own king". Then Blekinge, Möre, Öland and Gotland, "and these lands belong to the Svear". |
| The end | The Vistula flows out of Wendland into the Estmere (the Vistula Lagoon), which is "at least fifteen miles broad". The Ilfing flows into it from the east, out of the lake on whose shore Truso stands. |

What the numbers show: the replicas sail at 5 to 7 knots in a good wind (`ships.md` 5.1), and Wulfstan made good
less than half of that over a week. A week's passage is calms, head winds and feeling a way in, as well as sailing.
**For the game: a real passage speed is about a third to a half of a ship's good-wind speed.**

How he could list Öland and Gotland "to port" when they lie far north of his track: he is giving the lands as he
learned them, as a string, from the men he sailed with. This is the oral route-list of section 2, caught in
writing.

What he saw among the Este (for Truso's people and cards):

- Many towns, and a king in each. Much honey and much fishing. "The king and the richest men drink mare's milk, and
  the poor and the slaves drink mead." No ale is brewed among the Este, but there is mead enough.
- A dead man lies unburnt in his house among his kin and friends a month, sometimes two; kings and great men longer,
  sometimes half a year. All that time there is drinking and play in the house, until the day they burn him.
- On that day what is left of his goods is laid out in five or six heaps along a mile or so, the biggest furthest
  from the town. All the men with the swiftest horses in the land gather five or six miles off and race: the first
  takes the biggest heap, and so on. "And for that reason swift horses are extremely dear there."
- There is a people among the Este that can make cold: that is why the dead can lie so long without decaying. Set
  two vessels full of ale or water before them and they can freeze both, summer or winter.

## 16. Notes for the game

All (D). Nothing here adds a variable, verb, deed or role (BRIEF rule 5): they are ways to use what exists.

1. **A route is a taught thing.** A person teaches a run as Ohthere tells it: days, and which hand the land is on.
   Use `learn` for the route and let a learned route better the odds word on its cards.
2. **The lead is a card choice.** "Sound and go slow" costs time; "stand on" is quicker and worse by a word over
   banks and bars. A pilot (`act:hire_pilot`) makes the bar "sure" on a good tide.
3. **The tide is a clock inside the clock.** At 1 second to 1 minute a tide turns every six minutes of play. Bars
   and tidal harbours can ask for `act:wait_wind`'s sister: waiting for water. The moon's age gives the hour
   (section 6); springs give more water and harder streams.
4. **Lost is a state.** Fog or long overcast out of sight of land hides the ship's place on the chart (the fog of
   the chart is already in the design). The sun gives back the heading; a sounding, a seamark or birds give back
   the place.
5. **Sayings are forecasts with odds.** A crewman says saying 1; the game knows it is "sound", so tomorrow's
   weather agrees more often than not. A learned passenger says saying 6; it is "empty". The player learns whom to
   believe. Never show the worth; let it be found.
6. **The season is a risk word, not a wall.** Section 8's table. A voyage after Michaelmas is allowed and is one
   word worse; after Martinmas, two.
7. **Winter is a panel.** Laying up costs days and tar (a good with a source in the east); skipping it costs the
   hull next year (`ships.md` 7.3).
8. **Faith acts fit the sea.** `act:vow` before or during danger; `act:ask_omen` (lots) before a voyage or to find
   who brought bad luck; `act:offer` at a heathen landing; `act:alms` and `act:hear_mass` at a Christian one. A
   kept vow is a deed; a broken one is remembered. Blessed oil is a thing a priest may give.
9. **Saints by year.** Before about 990: Christ in the boat, Peter, Martin, Willibrord, Cuthbert, Columba. From
   about 990: Clement, among Danes and Northmen. From 1030: Olaf. Nicholas only from about 1000 and as a novelty
   from the south.
10. **Stranding.** The three questions of section 13. A charter or a truce is worth most on the day the boat is on
    somebody else's sand.

**Small true things for a curious player** (for `easter_eggs`):

- The Roman altars to Nehalennia under the sand at Walichrum, set up by skippers for "goods kept safe" (11.1).
- The well on Heligoland where nobody speaks (2).
- A monk who watches the lead go down and quotes Acts 27 (3).
- Bede's sentence that the tide rises in one place while it falls in another, read by a monk of Jarrow who has
  never been to sea (6).
- A Northman who will not say "wind" in a calm (11.4).
- The lighthouse at Boulogne, dark in 810 and lit in 811 (2).
- "Starboard" is the side the steering oar hangs on, and a ship lies with her other side to the quay so as not to
  crush it. In these years the other side is the "back-board"; "port" for it is LATER (12).
- The first time the name Denmark is written, it is a Northman telling an English king which hand it was on (14).

**Needs** (for whoever owns the shared tables; I have added nothing): none required. If a "waiting for water" act
is wanted beside `wait_wind`, it would be a new act id.
