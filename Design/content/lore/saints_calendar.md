# The year: feasts, fasts, fairs and things (793 to 1066)

Written 6 October 2026 for Kapman. The data is in `calendar.json` beside this file: a plain list, one row for each
day or rule. Its fields: `name`; `month` and `day` (0 where the day moves) and `rule` (easter+N is N days after
Easter Sunday); `days` (how long it lasts); `where` and `ports`; `kind` (feast, movable, fast, saint, fair, thing,
heathen, due, law, season); `what_happens`; `what_it_means_for_trade`; `from` and `to` (years inside the game: 793
and 1066 are the game's ends, not the custom's); `attested` (period, late, inferred); `source`.
Port ids are those of `Assets/Kapman/Game/Data/ports_793.json`. Fair dates agree with `fairs_793.json`, the month
lines with `trade_year_793.json`, and the debt's day with `chains/house_debt.md` (Michaelmas).

**How far to trust it.** Keys in square brackets are in section 14. I read six pages on the day of writing (marked
"read" there). Everything else is from memory of the sources named and was not looked up again: the plain facts I am
sure of stand plain, and anything I could not be sure of carries (?). **late** means the source was written after
1066 about an earlier time. Nothing here is quoted at length, so no translation is copied.

## 1. How a merchant counts time

- **One calendar for all Christians: the Julian.** Twelve Roman months, a leap day every fourth year. Latin
  writing counts days back from kalends, nones and ides; nobody says that aloud. People name a day by its feast and
  count from it: "three nights before Saint John", "the Friday after Martinmas", "between the gang days and
  midsummer" [ASC, passim]. The game's own rule (BRIEF 2) is the period's.
- **The day begins at evening.** A feast starts at vespers the evening before (its eve, its vigil). Germanic peoples
  count nights, not days: a week is seven nights, two weeks fourteen nights [Tacitus, Germania 11; the English
  laws count "nights" throughout].
- **The calendar has slipped.** By 793 the Julian year runs about four days behind the sun, by 1066 about six. The
  true longest day falls about 17 June, yet "midsummer" is Saint John, 24 June; the true shortest day about 17
  December, yet midwinter is Christmas [reckoned; Bede knew the equinox had moved, DTR 30].
- **The year's number.** Years "from the Lord's incarnation" were spread by Bede and by Easter tables; the annals
  the game's chronicle voice comes from began as notes in the margins of those tables. Where the year turns is not
  agreed: at Christmas in England and at the Frankish court, at 1 September by the indiction in Bede's own history,
  at 25 March in some houses later (?). A merchant does not care: for him the year turns when the ships come out.
- **The moon.** Everybody watches it: Easter hangs on it, the tides hang on it, and a heathen reckons his whole year
  by it. A priest reckons the moon's age from tables (the epact), a seaman by looking.
- **The week** is the Church's gift to the north. The heathen Norse took the seven days and gave them their own
  gods' names before they took the faith (Tyr's, Odin's, Thor's, Frigg's day); the names are older than the
  conversion (?). Sunday begins on Saturday afternoon: in England "from noon on Saturday till dawn on Monday"
  [II Edgar 5; I Cnut 14].
- **The game's first day** is 21 June 793. By reckoning it is a Friday, a fast day, three nights before Saint John.
  Easter that year was 7 April and Pentecost 26 May; the raid on Lindisfarne, 8 June, fell on a Saturday.
  Michaelmas 793, when the debt falls due, is a Sunday [reckoned from the Easter rule in section 3].

## 2. The fixed feasts

### 2.1 What the law says must be kept

Two lists made inside the game's years tell which days stop work and trade.

**The Franks: the council of Mainz, 813, canon 36** [Mainz 813]. Kept with rest from work: Easter Sunday and its
week; the Ascension; Pentecost, like Easter; Saints Peter and Paul; Saint John the Baptist; the Assumption of Mary;
Saint Michael; Saint Remigius; Saint Martin; Saint Andrew; four days at Christmas; the octave of the Lord (1
January); the Epiphany; the Purification; and in each diocese the feasts of the martyrs and confessors whose bodies
lie there, and the day the church was hallowed.

**The English: Alfred's law 43, about 890** [Alfred 43]. Days given to free men, "but not to slaves and unfree
workmen": twelve days at Christmas; the day Christ overcame the devil (15 February); Saint Gregory's day (12 March);
seven days before Easter and seven after; one day at Saints Peter and Paul; in harvest the full week before Saint
Mary's mass (15 August); one day at All Saints. The four Wednesdays of the four Ember weeks are given to slaves, to
sell what they have been given or have earned in their spare hours: on those four days a ship's buyer may find
slaves selling small things at the market.

Later English law adds Edward the Martyr, 18 March, from 1008 [V Æthelred 16], and Dunstan, 19 May, under Cnut
[I Cnut 17].

1066, the day Edward was buried; William on Christmas Day 1066 [ASC 1066].

## 3. The movable feasts, and how Easter is reckoned

**The rule.** Easter is the Sunday after the first full moon (the "fourteenth moon") that falls on or after 21
March, the equinox by the Church's reckoning. The moon is not looked at: it is taken from a table that repeats
every nineteen years. This is the reckoning of Alexandria, put into Latin by Dionysius Exiguus in 525 and taught
to the north by Bede's *The Reckoning of Time* (725) [DTR]. Easter can fall no earlier than 22 March and no
later than 25 April.

**Who keeps it.** By 793 everybody the player meets. The Northumbrians chose it at Whitby in 664 [Bede, HE III.25];
Iona gave way in 716 [HE V.22]; the Britons of Wales in 768 [Annales Cambriae]. The Greeks and the Rus (after 988)
keep the same Easter: the split between the two reckonings is centuries after the game. A priest must know the
reckoning: Charlemagne ordered schools to teach it in 789 [Admonitio generalis 72]. Hrabanus wrote on it at Fulda in
820, Abbo taught it at Ramsey about 986, and Byrhtferth wrote it out in English in 1011 (?).

**What a merchant does** is ask. The priest reads the date of Easter aloud at the Epiphany (?), and from then every
port knows when Lent begins, when the fish will be wanted, and when the king will hold his Easter court.

**In the game's years** (reckoned by the rule above): Easter fell in April in 205 of the 274 years and in March
in 69. The earliest possible, 22 March, came in 851, 946 and 1041; the latest, 25 April, in 919 and 1014.

**Feast days draw raiders.** A town at a feast is full of people and silver and has its men in church. The
Northmen took Nantes on Saint John's day, 24 June 843, and killed the bishop in his cathedral [AAng; Chronicle of
Nantes]; they came into Paris on Holy Saturday, 28 March 845 [AB 845]; they burned Trier on Maundy Thursday, 5 April
882 [AF; Regino]; Guthrum rode on Chippenham "at midwinter after Twelfth Night" 878 [ASC 878]. And once the other
way about: King Æthelred had the Danes in England killed on Saint Brice's day, 13 November 1002 [ASC 1002].

## 4. Fast days, and what could not be eaten

**The rule in one line:** on a fast day no meat, one meal, and that meal late.

| Fast | When | How hard |
|---|---|---|
| **Lent** | Ash Wednesday to Holy Saturday: forty weekdays (Sundays are not fasted but are still meatless) | No flesh of beast or bird. By the strict rule nothing that comes from flesh either: milk, butter, cheese, eggs [Gregory the Great, as later quoted in canon law (?)]. One meal a day, after vespers; by the 9th century often after the ninth hour, in mid-afternoon. No weddings, no feasting, no hunting. |
| **Fridays** | every week, unless a feast falls on it | No meat. In England by law: "every Friday, unless it be a feast" [V Æthelred 17; I Cnut 16]. |
| **Wednesdays** | the old second fast day | Kept by monks and the devout; fading for lay folk in the game's years (?). The Irish names for Wednesday and Friday mean "first fast" and "fast". |
| **Ember days** | four times a year: Wednesday, Friday, Saturday of one week | By the council of Mainz, 813: the first week of March, the second of June, the third of September, and the full week before Christmas Eve [Mainz 813, c. 34; W:Ember]. The weeks were not the same everywhere until 1095 [W:Ember]. Priests are ordained on the Ember Saturdays. |
| **Vigils** | the eve of a great feast | In England: before every feast of Saint Mary and of every apostle except Philip and James [V Æthelred 14]. Also the eves of Christmas, Pentecost, Saint John, Saint Laurence, All Saints. |
| **Rogation days** | 25 April, and the three days before the Ascension | A fast with a procession. |
| **Advent, "Saint Martin's Lent"** | from Martinmas, or from the Sunday nearest Saint Andrew, to Christmas | In Gaul by old rule three days a week, Monday, Wednesday and Friday [Mâcon 581 (?)]. Monks keep it whole. For lay folk it is lighter than Lent and not the same in any two dioceses. |
| **A fast called in need** | when the king and bishops order it | In 1009, with the great army in the land, all England was to fast on bread, herbs and water on the Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday before Michaelmas, go barefoot to church, and give a penny from every hide [VII Æthelred]. |

**What may be eaten:** bread, pottage of peas and beans, leeks, onions, cabbage, apples, nuts, honey; fish of every
kind, fresh, salt, dried or smoked; eels; shellfish; oil where it can be had. Ale and wine are drink, not food, and
are not forbidden. Theodulf of Orléans, about 800, tells his priests that a man who can go without eggs, cheese,
fish and wine in Lent does very well, and that one who cannot may take them without sin, so long as he fasts
till evening [Theodulf, Capitula I.40 (?)]: the strict rule and the rule as lived were not the same.

**What counts as fish** is argued. Whale and porpoise are eaten as fish (the men of Rouen brought "craspice", fat
fish, to London about 1000 [IV Æthelred 2]). The beaver's tail is said to be fish (the claim is written down only
after 1066 (?)). The barnacle goose that "grows on trees" and so is not flesh is a 12th-century tale: do not use it.

**Who need not fast:** children, the old, the sick, women with child, and those on a hard journey or at heavy work
(by the penitentials, and by a priest's leave) (?). A crew at sea in Lent is a fair case for a card: the fish is to
hand anyway.

**The law behind it.** In Saxony, under Charlemagne's first law for the conquered, a man who ate meat in Lent
"out of contempt for the Christian faith" was to die, unless a priest judged he had done it from need
[Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae 4, about 782 to 785]. In Kent a man who gave his household meat on a fast day
paid the fine for it [Wihtred 14, 695]. Among English and Danes the fine for breaking a lawful fast was written
down again about 1000 [Edward and Guthrum 8].

**Horse.** Christians do not eat it: Pope Gregory III told Boniface in 732 to forbid it as "filthy" [Boniface,
letter 28]. The heathen north does, at its feasts. When Iceland took the faith in 1000 the old law on eating horse
was let stand for a few years [Ari 7]. What is on the table tells a guest where he is.

**What the fasts do to trade** (the game's seasons already carry most of it):

- Fish is wanted before and through Lent: herring, stockfish, dried fish, eels, salmon. Stock up from Candlemas.
- Meat, cheese, butter and eggs do not sell in Lent. Slaughter stock is sold before Martinmas and salted (salt is
  dearest in October and November).
- Wax and oil are wanted before Candlemas and Easter; incense and wine before every great feast.
- After Easter the hungry weeks: grain is dearest in April and May (`seasons_793.json`).
- At Easter and Pentecost new Christians are baptised in white: fine linen sells before them (?). Northmen were said
  to take baptism more than once for the white clothes [Notker, II.19].

Ælfheah of Canterbury (19 April, killed 1012); Olaf of Norway (29 July; killed at Stiklestad 1030, his body taken
up and declared holy at Nidaros 3 August 1031) [ASC; Sigvat's memorial verses; Adam II.61]. Within a lifetime
Olaf has churches in York, London, Dublin and Novgorod: the first saint of the whole northern trade.

**Market law that follows the calendar:**

- **No market on Sunday.** Francia: no servile work on Sunday [Admonitio generalis 81, 789]; no markets on it
  [capitulary of 809 (?)]. England: Æthelstan forbade Sunday trading about 930 and then let it go again [II
  Æthelstan 24; IV Æthelstan 2]; Cnut forbade it once more, with Sunday meetings [I Cnut 15]. Among English and
  Danes a man who traded on Sunday lost the goods and paid twelve ores if a Dane, thirty shillings if English
  [Edward and Guthrum 7; the text is of about 1000]. Ireland: the Law of Sunday forbids journeys, buying and selling
  from Saturday evening to Monday morning [Cáin Domnaig, 8th or 9th century].
- **Buy in a port, before witnesses.** England: no buying outside a port, and with the port-reeve's witness
  [I Edward 1]; nothing over twenty pence outside a port [II Æthelstan 12].
- **The peace of the feast.** No oaths and no ordeals on feast days, on Ember days, from Advent to a week after
  the Epiphany, or from Septuagesima to two weeks after Easter [V Æthelred 18; I Cnut 17]. A merchant who
  needs an oath to clear himself may wait a month for it.
- **The Truce of God** (late game, Francia only): no fighting from Wednesday evening to Monday morning and in the
  holy seasons; preached in the south from 1027, in Normandy at Caen in 1047, in Flanders at Thérouanne in 1063 (?).
  The earlier Peace of God (from 989) names merchants among those not to be robbed (?).

## 7. Things and assemblies

Where free men meet, law is said, debts are claimed and peace is sworn. Trade follows every one of them.

**The Franks**

- **The general assembly.** Once the "March field"; Pippin moved it to May in 755 [continuator of Fredegar (?)].
  Under Charlemagne it meets where the king is, between May and August, before the summer's campaign; the gifts
  owed to the king are brought to it. A second, smaller meeting of the great men in autumn or winter plans the next
  year [Hincmar, On the governance of the palace 29 to 30, written 882].
- **The count's court (mallus).** Free men must come to the general sittings: one about summer and one about
  autumn in the capitulary of 769, three a year later (?). Debts and thefts are heard there. Not on Sundays.
- **The king's feasts.** Where the king keeps Christmas and Easter is written in the annals every year: Aachen most
  often, also Nijmegen (`noviomagus`), Worms, Frankfurt, Ingelheim by `mogontia` [ARF]. A court eats a district bare
  and pays for wine, wax, spices and furs.

**The Saxons**

- Before the conquest they met once a year at **Marklo** on the Weser, twelve men of each rank from every district
  [Old Life of Lebuin, 9th century]. Charlemagne's law forbade any general meeting of Saxons unless his own man
  called it [Capitulatio 34]. In the game there is no Saxon thing, only the count's.

**The Frisians**

- No thing of all Frisians is attested in the period. The meeting at the Upstalsboom near Aurich, on the Tuesday
  after Pentecost, is first written of in the 13th century: do not use it. The Law of the Frisians (about 802)
  knows courts and oaths in church, and the ordeal by lot.

**The English**

- **The hundred** meets every four weeks; **the borough court** three times a year; **the shire court** twice a
  year, with the bishop and the ealdorman [Hundred Ordinance 1; III Edgar 5; from about 950]. Before that: the
  "folk-moot" under a reeve [Alfred 22, 34], and merchants bring the men they take up country before the king's
  reeve at it [Alfred 34].
- **The king's council** gathers at the great feasts. Laws are given at them: Grately; Exeter at midwinter
  [V Æthelstan]; Enham at Pentecost 1008 [V and VI Æthelred]; Winchester at Christmas [I and II Cnut].
- **London.** The husting, a court with a Norse name, is in being by the late 10th century, and silver is weighed
  "by the husting's weight" (?). The folk-moot at Saint Paul's three times a year is written of only after 1066.
- **The Danelaw.** The wapentake for the hundred; the "thing" in the place-names (Thingwall by `legacaestir`).

**The Norse and Danes**

- **Birka.** A thing sat in the town: in 852 King Olof put Anskar's request to the lots, then to one assembly at
  Birka and a second elsewhere in his kingdom, and an old man spoke for the Christians' god because the way to
  Dorestad had grown dangerous [Rimbert 26 to 27]. The only thing in the north described by a man of its own time.
- **Uppsala.** "All the things of the Svear" were held with the Disting [Snorri, **late**]. Adam, about 1075, has
  the Svear's kings unable to act against the people's will [Adam IV.22].
- **Gotland.** The Gutnalthing at Roma, the island's own assembly with no king over it [Guta saga, **late**].
- **Norway.** The Gulathing in the west and the Frostathing in Trøndelag, in summer; in the written laws about
  Saint Botolph's day, 17 June (?) [the laws are 12th century and later: **late**]. Kings are taken at the Eyrathing.
- **Denmark.** Kings are taken at Viborg in Jutland; things at Ringsted for Zealand and Lund for Skåne (after
  about 990) [Saxo and the provincial laws, **late**; Magnus was taken as king in 1042].
- **Iceland** (off the chart, but its ships are not): the Althing from 930, two weeks at midsummer, opening on the
  Thursday when nine weeks of summer had passed, and from 999 when ten had passed [Ari 7].
- **Dublin.** The Thingmote mound east of the town is written of only later; the town had it (?).

**The Irish**

- The **óenach**: a king's assembly and fair at a burial place of his line, at Lughnasa above all. Tailtiu for the
  king of Tara, Carman for Leinster (every third year (?)). The Norse kings of Dublin held Carman's when they
  could [verse on Carman, 11th century (?)].

**The Wends and Balts**

- The Liutizi decide by a meeting where all must agree, and one who holds out is beaten or his goods burnt, at the
  temple of Riedegost (Rethra) [Thietmar VI.23 to 25, written 1018: contemporary]. The harvest feast at Arkona,
  with the great honey cake and the horn, is **late** [Saxo XIV, of 1168]. For Prussians and Curonians no dated
  assembly is known; Wulfstan gives only their funeral: the dead lie unburnt a month or more while the kin drink,
  and the goods are raced for on horseback [Wulfstan, in the Old English Orosius, about 890].

The one honest sentence: **we have the names of the feasts from the 13th century and the fact of the feasts from
the 9th to 11th.** A card may show a heathen feast; it should be careful about its name and its day.

### 8.3 What a card may safely show

- A feast at the start of winter, one at midwinter and one in spring: yes, everywhere in the heathen north.
- Ale brewed for it, a beast killed, the meat shared, horse on the table: yes.
- A market and a thing with the feast at Uppsala in late winter, reached by sledge over the ice: yes ("it is said").
- A great feast every ninth year at `hleidr` (January) and `uppsalir` (about the equinox) with men killed: yes, as
  rumour heard in a Christian port, never as a thing the player is shown in plain sight. The game does not know
  which years were the ninth; pick them and keep to them (a guess to be marked).
- Doors shut to strangers on the elves' night in autumn: yes, in Svealand and Götaland about 1019.
- Not to show: named rites with set words, priests in robes, temples with gold roofs seen at first hand (Adam's
  gold chain is hearsay), "Ostara" as a Norse or Frisian feast, the wheel of the year with eight spokes, Yule
  logs, mistletoe, the Wild Hunt by that name.

**The meeting of the two years.** Through the whole game they lie side by side. At Hedeby about 850 Christian
merchants from Dorestad and Hamburg hear a bell on Sunday while their hosts brew for winter [Rimbert 24]. A
Northman who wants to trade with Christians can take the "first signing" with the cross without baptism, which
lets him eat and deal with them and keep his own gods [Rimbert 24; Egils saga 50, **late**]. In Norway the Yule ale
becomes the Christmas ale by law, blessed "for a good year and peace" [Gulathing law 7, **late**].

## 9. The Irish year

Four quarter days, each at the start of a season, each kept from the evening before; named and explained by an
Irish bishop inside the period [Cormac's Glossary, about 900]:

| Day | Name | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1 February | Imbolc; Saint Brigid | The ewes come into milk; spring begins. |
| 1 May | Beltaine | Cattle driven between two fires and out to the summer grazing. |
| 1 August | Lughnasa | The first of the harvest; the great fairs. |
| 1 November | Samain | Cattle home, the beasts not to be wintered killed, rents and tribute brought to the king, the end of the fighting and the sailing season. The king of Tara's feast is set here in the tales (**late**). |

Wednesday and Friday are both fast days by their names. The Irish keep three Lents in the stricter houses: before
Easter, before Christmas, and after Pentecost [the rule of the Céli Dé, about 800 (?)].

## 11. The sailing season, and when the sea is shut

**The old rule** every clerk could read: Vegetius, about 400, copied all through the Carolingian age (Hrabanus
made an abridgement of him about 856). Safe sailing from 27 May to 14 September; doubtful from 10 March to 26 May
and from 15 September to 10 November; from 11 November to 10 March "the seas are shut" [Vegetius IV.39]. Martinmas
is the day the sea closes.

**The northern rule**, written **late** by a Norwegian father for a merchant son: go to sea no earlier than the
start of April, and do not be on it after the start of October (?), for then the nights are long and dark, the
seas heavy, the rain cold; tar the ship in autumn and let her stand so all winter; settle with your partners
before you sail [King's Mirror, about 1250].

**What the period itself shows:**

- The Chronicle's hosts move by the same year: they come "after midsummer" or "after Lammas", and go into "winter
  quarters" about Martinmas (1006: the great fleet came to Sandwich after midsummer and at Martinmas went to its
  winter base; 1009: after Lammas, and after Martinmas back to Kent) [ASC 1006, 1009].
- "Winter quarters" is itself news: 851, the heathen men first stayed the winter on Thanet [ASC 851].
- William crossed on the night of 27 to 28 September 1066, the eve of Michaelmas, after waiting six weeks for a
  wind; Harald Hardrada had come in September [ASC 1066; William of Poitiers]. Late crossings are a gamble kings
  take.
- Ohthere and Wulfstan, about 890, give day counts and no dates; both voyages are plainly summer ones.
- Frozen rivers are chronicle news because they are rare in the west: the hard winters of 810/11, 821/22 (the
  Rhine, Danube, Elbe and Seine bore loaded carts for thirty days and more), 859/60 [ARF 821; AB 860; in
  `chronicle_793.json`].
- In the east the ice is the calendar. Mälaren freezes from about December to April: no ship reaches `birka`, but
  sledges do. Lake Ladoga and the Volkhov are shut from November to late April or May. The Rus go round their
  tribute lands in winter, from November, and come down to Kiev when the ice melts in April; the boats leave in
  June [Constantine Porphyrogenitus, On the administration of the empire 9, about 950].

**The year a master of Dorestad lives by** (a working calendar for cards and for the trade lines):

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Candlemas, 2 Feb | The ship is looked over; fish bought for Lent. The Disting, far off. |
| Lent (February to April) | Caulking and tar; river trade only. Fish dear, meat unsold. |
| Easter (22 March to 25 April) | The crew is hired and sworn; debts and partnerships made for the year. |
| From mid April (Tiburtius, 14 April: summer begins by the Norse count (?)) | First short runs: the delta, the Almere, the Rhine. |
| The Rogation days and the Ascension (May) | The fields are blessed; the first crossings to England. |
| Pentecost to Saint John (late May to 24 June) | The sea is open by anybody's rule. Wool is shorn. The long voyages start: Ribe, Hedeby, the Baltic. The game begins in this week of the year. |
| Saint John to Lammas (24 June to 1 August) | High season. The northern markets are full. Salt is made on the Loire coast. |
| Lammas to the Assumption (1 to 15 August) | Harvest: no hired hands, cheap new grain coming. |
| The Assumption to Michaelmas (15 August to 29 September) | The last long voyages come home. The herring come to the English coast (?). |
| Michaelmas, 29 Sep | Reckoning. Debts due. |
| Saint Denis, 9 Oct | The fair at Paris for a month: the year's wine, bought to carry north before the frost. |
| Mid October | Winter Nights in the north: the Northmen are home, and their markets empty. |
| All Saints, 1 Nov | The careful are in harbour. |
| Martinmas, 11 Nov | The sea is shut. Slaughter, new wine, rents. The boat is hauled up. |
| Advent to Christmas | Fasting, mending, telling. The price of everything a ship brings climbs till spring. |
| Christmas to Epiphany | Twelve days of nobody working. The news of the year is put together at the fire: this is when a player's stale knowledge is all the knowledge there is. |

**For the game's rules.** The sea is not locked like a gate on 11 November: it grows worse by the week, and the
sources show men crossing in every month when they had to (Guthrum's winter ride, William's Michaelmas crossing,
monks fleeing in any season). What closes it is what the game already has: short days, gales, cold that kills a
crew in an open boat, ice in the east, and nobody to trade with on the other side.

## 12. Ideas the calendar gives the cards

Every one is from a fact above. They are offered to the card writers; none is written here.

1. **The fast at sea.** A Friday in Lent and the crew wants the salt pork. (Friday rule; who need not fast.)
2. **The slaves' Wednesday.** On an Ember Wednesday in an English port, slaves sell their own small goods.
   (Alfred 43.)
3. **Sunday on the quay.** A buyer offers a good price on a Sunday; the reeve is at church. (Edward and Guthrum 7.)
4. **No oath till after the Epiphany.** Accused of selling short measure in Advent: no oath can be sworn for a
   month. Wait, pay, or leave. (I Cnut 17.)
5. **Bread, herbs and water.** England, the three days before Michaelmas 1009: nothing sells but those, and every
   stranger is looked at. (VII Æthelred.)
6. **Saint Brice's day.** 13 November 1002 in an English port with Danes in the crew. (ASC 1002.)
7. **Doors shut.** An autumn night in Svealand: every farm is keeping the elves' feast and nobody takes a guest.
   (Sigvat.)
8. **The pole by the door.** Hedeby at its feast: a host shows his slaughtered beast on a pole and asks a
   Christian guest to eat. (al-Tartushi; the horse-meat rule.)
9. **Buying off.** Uppsala in a ninth year: the Christians of Birka are told to pay rather than come.
   (Adam IV.27.)
10. **The white clothes.** Easter eve at a Frankish court: Northmen come to be baptised for the gift of linen.
    (Notker II.19.)
11. **The feast and the fleet.** Saint John's eve at Nantes, 843; Holy Saturday at Paris, 845. A rumour of sails
    at a feast is the game's best warning. (Section 3.)
12. **The emperor's men.** Christmas at London about 1000: the Rhinelanders pay pepper and gloves and get the
    right to sell from the ship. (IV Æthelred 2.)
13. **The sledge road.** Late winter, Mälaren hard as a floor: furs go to the Disting over the ice, and the ship
    stays where she lies. (Snorri; the Birka finds.)
14. **Martinmas wine.** New wine reaches Dorestad as the sea shuts; the man with a hold of it at Candlemas names
    his price. (Seasons table.)

## 13. What is not known, and what was not checked

- No feast, fair or assembly is known for `witla`, `wijnaldum`, `reric` or `grobin`: they have no row of their own in
  `calendar.json` and keep only the general feasts of their people.
- The patron of Dorestad's own church; any dated fair at Dorestad, Quentovic, Cologne, Mainz, Rouen, London, Hamwic
  or York before 1066. I know of none and have written none.
- The days of the heathen feasts before the 13th-century calendar: all are approximate. Which years were "ninth
  years" at Lejre and Uppsala is not recorded.
- Rent days on Frankish estates: Martinmas and Saint Remigius are from general memory of the estate books (Saint-
  Germain about 825, Prüm 893) and are marked (?). The exact dues are not given here.
- The King's Mirror's dates for the northern season; the Uppland law's wording on the Disting; the moon rule for
  the Disting; Theodulf's chapter number; the capitulary of 809 on Sunday markets; the Truce of God's dates in
  the north; the first Flemish fairs: all (?) and worth a look before a card leans on them.
- The weekdays and Easter dates were reckoned by the Julian rule, not read from a table.
