# The words of the period, 793 to 1066

For a writer, and for an editor going back over the cards already written. Made 7 October 2026 with
`Assets/Kapman/Game/Data/story/tongues_793.json` (the word lists and the real lines) and
`Design/content/lore/tongues_notes.md` (how the game should use them).

**The rule in one line.** Write plain modern English; call the period's own things by the names they had; never
put a modern thing or a modern idea in a mouth of 793; never write mock-old speech ("thee", "prithee", "mayhap").

**How the sources are marked.** `[read]` the text was read on 7 October 2026 (the page is named in
tongues_notes.md). `[chest]` it stands in `texts_793.json`. `[mem]` given from memory of the source named: right in
substance, to be checked before it is quoted. `(?)` unsure. `[lore]` already sourced in `law_tolls.md` or
`economy/silver_coin.md`: go there for the detail.

**Plain English is allowed.** "Coin", "market", "merchant", "price", "crew", "cargo" and "pay" are later words in
English, but the things existed and no reader stumbles on them. The test is the idea, not the dictionary: would
the speaker have had the thing and the thought? A penny, yes. A percentage, no.

**How distance is told.** In days, never in miles. "Seven days and nights, the ship running all the way under
sail" (Wulfstan, Hedeby to Truso). "Three days with the waste land on the steering side" (Ohthere). "A month, if a
man camped each night and each day had a fair wind" (Ohthere, down the coast of Norway). [chest] This is the
measure on the map.

**The names of the seas.** The Baltic is the **East Sea** (English *Ostsǣ*, Norse *Eystrasalt*) [mem: Old English
Orosius]; "Baltic" is first used by Adam of Bremen about 1075. The North Sea has no one name: to the Danes it is
the **Western Sea**, to Latin writers sometimes the **Frisian Sea** (?), to the English just the sea. The
Mediterranean is the **Wendel Sea** (English *Wendelsǣ*, Frankish *wentilseo*) [mem: Hildebrandslied]. The
Atlantic is the **Ocean**, the **Outer Sea**, English *gārsecg* [mem]. Nobody says "the English Channel": one goes
"over sea".

### Coins and weights

Full detail is in `economy/silver_coin.md` and `law_tolls.md` 15. For the writer:

| Where | What changes hands | Reckoned in |
|---|---|---|
| Francia | the silver **penny** (*denarius*), heavier from 793/4 [lore] | 12 pennies = 1 **shilling** (*solidus*); 240 = 1 **pound** (*libra*). The shilling and the pound are sums, not coins |
| England | the silver **penny** (*pening*) | the shilling (4 pennies in Mercia, 5 in Wessex [mem]); the **mancus** (30 pennies, gold or its worth [lore]); the pound (240) |
| The North, Wendland, the East | **silver by weight**: cut rings and coins on a folding scale. No king's coin a seller must take | the **mark** (*mörk*) = 8 **ounces** (*aurar*, one *eyrir*) [mem] |
| Ireland | **cows**; no coin at all | the cow (*bó*), the *sét*, the *cumal* (a bondwoman, or three cows) [mem] |

- Say **silver**, **pennies**, **a pound of pennies**, **a mark of silver**, **weighed silver**, **hack-silver**. Not
  "money" where "silver" will do, and never "cash", "gold coins" (gold is rare, and a treasure) or "change".
- A penny buys **twelve wheat loaves of two pounds** in Francia by the law of 794 [lore]. It is not small change.
- One word serves for cattle, goods and money: Norse *fé*, English *feoh* [read] Hávamál 76; The Wanderer 108.
  Wealth is still counted on the hoof.
- Measures: the **ell** (cloth), the **fathom** (depth and rope), the **foot**; the **pound** and the **ounce**;
  the **bushel** (*modius*) for grain and the **sester** for wine and honey [lore]; a **load** (*hlæst*) for a
  cargo. The **hundred** of trade is often the **long hundred, 120** [mem]: say "six score" when it matters.
- **False weight** is a sin and a crime: Proverbs 11:1 [read], and the king's law of 789 [lore].

Churchmen everywhere: **pope**, **archbishop**, **bishop**, **abbot**, **abbess**, **priest** (mass-priest),
**deacon**, **monk**, **nun**, **clerk**. In a minster, the **cellarer** buys and sells and the **porter** keeps the
gate [read: Rule of Benedict 66; mem: 31]. The whole house, servants and tenants too, is its *familia* [read:
Annals of Ulster 806].

Not yet: **knight** (English *cniht* means a boy or servant), **baron**, **sheriff** (10th century on), **mayor**,
**sergeant**, **marshal** as a commander (a *mariscalcus* still looks after horses [mem]), **captain**, **admiral**.

## (b) Words and ideas that must not appear in a line of 793 to 1066

**Found in the cards on 7 October 2026** (whole-word counts in `writing.json` and `writing/*.json`): *England* 127,
*hour(s)* 193, *business* 76, *luck/lucky* 60, *sorry* 44, *cathedral* 33, *soldier(s)* 35, *mile(s)* 32,
*Denmark* 21, *please* 18, *deck* 17, *dinner* 17, *captain* 14, *pirates* 11, *prison* 8, *cabin* 5, *religion* 5,
*Bible* 4, *goodbye* 4. Each needs a look; some are fine in the narrator's voice and wrong in a mouth.

**Speech**

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| hello, hi, hey | "Hail." "Be well." "God keep you." Or the speaker's own: *heill*, *wes hāl*, *Deo gratias* |
| goodbye, bye | "Farewell." "Go safe." "A fair wind." (*goodbye* is "God be with ye", 16th century) |
| please | "I ask you." "If you will." "Of your kindness." Or nothing: an order is an order |
| sorry (as apology) | "Forgive me." "I did you wrong." "I will make it good." (To make it good is the period's whole idea of apology) |
| okay, OK, sure thing, fine by me, no problem, deal! | "So be it." "Done." "It is agreed." "Hands on it." |
| yeah, nope, wow, cool, great, awesome, nice | "Yes." "No." "A wonder." "Good." "Well done." |
| sir, madam, mister, my lady (as French courtesy) | "lord", "lady", "master", "father" (to a priest), "friend"; see (d) |
| guys, folks, kid, boss, buddy, mate (as address) | "men", "lads", "boy", "master", "friend", "shipmate" |
| actually, basically, literally, obviously, definitely, absolutely, honestly | cut the word |
| thee, thou, thy, ye, prithee, mayhap, methinks, verily, 'tis | plain "you", "perhaps", "I think", "truly", "it is". No mock-old speech, ever |

**Time**

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| minute, second, o'clock, half an hour | "a short while", "the time it takes to say a psalm", "before the pot boiled" |
| hour (as sixty minutes) | The day has **hours of prayer**, heard as bells: **prime** (dawn), **terce** (mid-morning), **sext** (noon), **none** (mid-afternoon), **vespers** (evening), **compline** (bedtime), and the **night office** [mem: Rule of Benedict 16]. Laymen say dawn, mid-morning (*undern* [mem]), noon, evening, nightfall, cockcrow |
| 8 June, the 3rd of May | a feast: "the day after Saint Boniface", "a week before Midsummer", "at Martinmas", "at Lammas" (see `saints_calendar.md`). A clerk says "the sixth day before the Ides of June" [chest: the 793 annal] |
| year 793, the 790s, a decade, a century | "the year the minster burned", "the fifth year of King Charles as emperor", "ten winters", "in my grandfather's time". Clerks know the year of the Lord; boatmen do not use it |
| he is thirty years old | "he has thirty winters" [mem: both English and Norse count age in winters] |
| weekend, holiday, vacation | "the feast day", "a holy day", "Sunday", "Yule", "the twelve days" |
| the Viking Age, the Dark Ages, the Middle Ages, medieval, Carolingian, Anglo-Saxon (as an age) | nothing: nobody knows they are in one |

**Things that are not there**

Potatoes, tomatoes, maize, tobacco, coffee, tea, chocolate, sugar (honey), oranges, lemons, rice, turkeys. Forks
(knife, spoon and fingers), glass in house windows, chimneys (a smoke hole), buttons (pins, brooches, ties),
pockets (a pouch at the belt), clocks, spectacles, soap in a bar. Windmills, spinning wheels, wheelbarrows.
Distilled drink of any kind. Hopped beer as the rule (ale; hops are just starting in Frankish abbeys (?)).
Breakfast, lunch and dinner: there are **two meals**, the day-meal and the night-meal [mem]. Stirrups are new and
not everywhere. Books are few, and a whole Bible in one volume is a king's gift.

**Famous lines that are not real.** Do not use them as if they were:

- *A furore Normannorum libera nos, Domine* ("from the fury of the Northmen deliver us, O Lord"): not found in any
  early service book in those words [mem]. Use Psalm 106, or the Chronicle for 793.
- "Skål" as a toast from skulls; horned helmets; "blood eagle" as plain fact; "Valhalla awaits": later, doubtful or
  modern.
- *Eala Frya Fresena* ("hail, free Frisians"): a late motto, not Old Frisian (?).

Three things to keep straight:

1. **"Dane" is the English word for any Northman**, and "Northman" the Frankish. The first three ships at Portland
   are "ships of Northmen" and "the first ships of Danish men" in the same annal [mem: Chronicle 787 for 789].
2. **"Welsh" means foreigner.** English *wealh*, Frankish *uualha* and Norse *Valir* are one word, used by each
   Germanic people for its Roman or British neighbours. In English it also means slave.
3. **The Church's word is "heathen", not a people's name.** *Pagani*, *gentiles*, *hǣðene men*, *geinti*: for the
   first sixty years the annals rarely care which Northmen they were.

A real jibe, for flavour: "Stupid are the foreigners, clever are the Bavarians; there is little cleverness in the
foreigners; they have more stupidity than cleverness" (*Tole sint uualha, spahe sint peigira...*), written in a
phrase list at Regensburg about 810 [read: Kassel glosses]. Every people had one of these about its neighbour.

## (d) Forms of address, by rank and by faith

The plain rule: **name first, then what he is.** "Offa king", "Wulfred bishop", "Harald king": the title follows the
name in English and Norse [read: the Jelling stone, "Haraldr kunungʀ"]. In English prose write "King Offa" for the
reader, but let a herald or a charter say "Offa, king of the Mercians".

| To | An English speaker says | A Northman says | A Frank or a clerk says | An Irishman says |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a king | "*lēof*" (dear lord), "lord", "king" [mem: the Fonthill Letter opens "Lēof"] | his name, or "king". No bowing words: every man is "you" (one man, one *þú*) [mem] | *domne rex* (lord king); "my lord"; from 800 "lord emperor". Frankish *hērro* [read: Strasbourg, "min herro"] | *a rí* (O king) [mem] |
| a great lord | "lord" (*hlāford*), "*lēof*" | "jarl", or his name | *domne*; "lord count" | *a thigerna* [mem] |
| a lady | "lady" (*hlǣfdige*) | "*húsfreyja*" (mistress of the house) [mem] | *domna* | *a ben* (woman: no slight) [mem] |
| a bishop or abbot | "father", "lord bishop" | (a heathen) "priest of the Christians"; (baptised) "bishop" | *domne*, *pater*. The abbot is "lord and abbot" [read: Rule of Benedict 63] | *a chléirig* (cleric) [mem] |
| a priest or monk | "father", "brother" | "priest" | *pater*; monks: an elder is *nonnus*, a younger is *frater*; never the bare name [read: Rule of Benedict 63] | "brother"; a hermit is "servant of God" (*céle Dé*) [mem] |
| an equal, a trader | "friend" (*wine*, *frēond*), "shipman" | "*félagi*" (partner), "*vinr*", "*frændi*" (kinsman, warmly) [read] | "friend"; Frankish *gisello*, *ginôz* (fellow) [mem]; Saxon *genôt* [read] | "son of X" |
| a stranger | "guest", "*lēof*" if he looks rich | "*gestr*" (guest: the same word as stranger) [read: Hávamál 2] | *hospes*; a pilgrim is *peregrinus* [read] | "stranger" |
| a servant or slave | "boy", his bare name | "*þræll*", his bare name | *puer*, his bare name | *a gillai* (lad) [mem] |
| a child | "child", "bairn" | "boy", "girl" | *fili* (my son), from any elder or priest | |

**Greeting and parting, by faith.**

- **Christian to Christian:** "God keep you." "The Lord be with you" (*Dominus vobiscum*) and the answer "The Lord
  bless you" [read: Ruth 2:4]. At a minster gate the porter says *Deo gratias* or *Benedic* [read: Rule 66]. At
  parting: "Go in peace"; the watchman's blessing in Beowulf, "the Father who rules all keep you safe on your ways"
  [chest]. Any plan ends "if the Lord wills" [read: James 4:15].
- **Heathen Northman:** *heill* ("be whole"); to a host, "hail to the giver" [read: Hávamál 2]. At parting, "go
  safe, come back safe" [read: Vafþrúðnismál 4]. He swears by the ring and by his gods; on a stone he asks Thor to
  hallow the runes [read: Glavendrup].
- **Christian to heathen:** the same plain words, without the blessing; in anger, "heathen". A heathen who has
  taken the first signing may eat and trade with Christians [mem: Egils saga 50].
- **Heathen to Christian:** by name or trade. Christ is "the White Christ" in later Norse telling [mem].
- **To a Jew or a Muslim** (rare in these waters; real at Mainz, Verdun, Cordoba): by people, "the Jew",
  "the Saracen"; Jewish merchants trade under the king's letter in Francia [mem].

**What people do not say.** "Your Majesty", "Your Highness", "Your Grace", "Your Excellency", "Your Holiness" in
speech: all later court forms. "Sire" and "milord": French and later. "Mr", "Mrs", "Miss": never.
