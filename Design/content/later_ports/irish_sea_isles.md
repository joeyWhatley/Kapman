# Ports the game lacks: Ireland, Scotland and the islands, 793-1066

Written 6 Oct 2026 for `Assets/Kapman/Game/Data/ports_793.json`, which has only three ports on these waters:
`duiblinn` (Dublin), `hi` (Iona) and `legacaestir` (Chester). This file proposes 25 more: 11 must have, 9 good to
have, 5 to leave out (with the reason). Each has a ready row in the table's own format.

**How far to trust this.** Ten pages were read on the web for this file (English Wikipedia: Woodstown, History of
Waterford, History of Limerick, Whithorn, Gásir, Linn Duachaill, Brough of Birsay, Jarlshof, Portmahomack, Bornish).
Everything else is from memory of the annals, the sagas and the excavation reports, not read again. `(?)` marks each
thing that is doubtful or that I could not check. Annal years are the corrected years AD. Saga dates (Orkney, the
Faroes, Iceland before about 1000) are tradition written down 200 to 300 years later: treat all of them as likely, not sure.
Latitude and longitude are for the landing place, to three decimals; those of Woodstown, Linn Duachaill, Birsay,
Jarlshof, Gásir, Portmahomack, Bornais and Whithorn church are from the pages read; all the others are from memory
and must be checked against the map before the bake (?).

Short names: **AU** Annals of Ulster; **AFM** Annals of the Four Masters; **AI** Annals of Inisfallen; **FA**
Fragmentary Annals; **CGG** Cogad Gáedel re Gallaib (written about 1100, a boast for Brian's house: use with care);
**OS** Orkneyinga saga (about 1200); **FS** Færeyinga saga (about 1200); **Ldn** Landnámabók; **ASC** Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.

## What this sea is, for a merchant from Dorestad

- **793 to 840: almost nothing to sail for.** Ireland and the north have no town and no coin. The markets are the great
  churches and the kings' fairs. The rock of the Clyde and Whithorn are the only two places here that take wine and
  give hides the old way. The islands north of Scotland are being taken by Norwegian farmers.
- **841 to 902: ship-forts.** The Northmen winter in Ireland from 841 (Dublin, Linn Duachaill), then on every big
  river mouth. They sell captives and church silver and want grain, wine, weapons. Dumbarton falls in 870. Iceland is
  settled from about 870, the Faroes somewhat before.
- **914 to 980: towns.** Waterford (914), Dublin again (917), Limerick (922), Cork and Wexford about the same time. Man,
  Whithorn and the Hebrides are stations on the road between Dublin, York and Norway. Silver goes by weight.
- **980 to 1066: the towns under Irish kings, the islands under earls.** Brian and his heirs hold Limerick, Cork and
  Waterford; Leinster holds Wexford and from 1052 Dublin. Dublin strikes pennies from about 995, Man from about 1025 (?).
  Orkney under Sigurd and Thorfinn rules from Shetland to Man. Iceland has a law, a yearly assembly and, from 1056, a bishop.

**A warning about the boat.** The player's first boat is 7.7 m with four men. It can coast to every place in Ireland,
Man and Scotland in this file. It should not be allowed across open ocean to the Faroes or Iceland (roughly 180 sea
miles from Shetland to the Faroes and 240 more to Iceland (?), with no shelter): those two need a sea-going cargo
ship. Shetland by way of Orkney and Fair Isle is the far edge of what a small boat does in summer.

## 1. Veðrafjǫrðr / Port Láirge (Waterford)

- **Name then:** Irish *Port Láirge*; the bay is *Loch dá Cháech* in the annals. Norse *Veðrafjǫrðr* (the name is first
  written down later; "ram fjord" or "weather fjord" (?)).
- **Modern place:** Waterford city, the triangle between the Suir and the old St John's River. **52.260 N, 7.106 W.**
- **Active:** 914 to the end of the game and beyond. (The older ship-fort was 9 km upstream: see Woodstown, no. 12.)
- **Kind:** a ship-fort that became a walled town in the 10th and 11th centuries; the second town of Ireland after Dublin.
- **Who ruled:** 914 a great new fleet in the bay (AU 914); Ragnall, grandson of Ímar, there 917 before he took York.
  About 969 to 1000 Ímar of Waterford, who also held Dublin for short spells in the 990s (?). From 984 an ally, in fact
  a client, of Brian of Munster. Ímar's sons and grandsons to 1035-1037, when the town was burned by Diarmait of
  Leinster (1037) (?). After that under Leinster and Munster kings by turns.
- **Traded:** out: hides, wool, captives (to about 1000), grain and malt from the Suir, Nore and Barrow valleys. In:
  wine and salt from the Loire and Bordeaux, English and Frankish silver, weapons, quernstones, pots.
- **How a boat comes in:** past Hook Head on the east side of the harbour mouth, then about 15 sea miles up a wide
  tidal river. The flood carries a boat up and the ebb will not let her; time the tide. The town is on the south bank
  where a small river makes a creek to lie in. Three rivers meet just below (Suir, Nore, Barrow): a small boat can go
  far inland on each.
- **Why go:** the nearest Irish town to Francia and to Bristol; the door to the richest farmland in Ireland; wine sells.
- **Evidence:** annals from 914. Large excavations 1986-1992 in the old town found streets, some eighty houses of the
  Dublin kind, a town bank and ditch, mostly of the 11th and 12th centuries; 10th-century levels are thin (?).
  Reginald's Tower stands where a Norse fort stood; the stone tower is later than the game.
- **Sources:** AU 914, 917; Hurley, Scully and McCutcheon, *Late Viking Age and Medieval Waterford: Excavations
  1986-1992* (1997); Downham, "The historical importance of Viking-Age Waterford", *Journal of Celtic Studies* 4
  (2004); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Waterford (read).

## 2. Hlymrekr / Luimnech (Limerick)

- **Name then:** Irish *Luimnech* (first the name of the estuary); Norse *Hlymrekr*. The island is *Inis Sibtonn*.
- **Modern place:** King's Island, Limerick. **52.669 N, 8.624 W.**
- **Active:** ships lay at Luimnech in 845 (the abbot of Armagh was carried to them there, AU 845); the lasting base is
  from 922. A town to the end of the game.
- **Kind:** a ship-fort on an island at the head of the tide; a town from the later 10th century.
- **Who ruled:** 922 Tomrar son of Elgi with a fleet (AU 922). In 924 Dublin attacked it and failed; in 937 Dublin broke
  its fleet on Lough Ree and took its king, Amlaíb Scabby-head. In 967 Mathgamain of the Dál Cais won at Sulchóit
  and sacked the town (CGG). Ímar of Limerick was killed on Scattery Island in 977 by Brian. From then it is the
  Dál Cais kings' own town: Brian to 1014, then his son Donnchad to 1063, then Tairdelbach.
- **Traded:** out: captives (the Shannon churches were its hunting ground, 922-937), hides, salmon, wool. In: wine (a
  later text says the town owed its king a tun of wine for every day of the year (?), CGG), silk and fine cloth, silver, salt.
- **How a boat comes in:** about 50 sea miles up the Shannon estuary from Loop Head. The tide runs hard and the upper
  part dries to mud at low water: go up on the flood, anchor for the ebb. Scattery Island, with its church, is the
  waiting place at the mouth. Above the town are rapids; boats were dragged past them to Lough Derg and the upper Shannon.
- **Why go:** the only town on the Atlantic side; the way to Clonmacnoise and the midlands by water; from 977 the
  market of the strongest king in Ireland.
- **Evidence:** annals 845, 922-937, 967, 977. CGG lists the loot of 967: saddles, gold, silver, silk and cloth of all
  colours, and the captives. Digging has found little of the Norse town: sunken houses of the 11th-12th centuries under
  the later castle (?).
- **Sources:** AU 845, 922, 924, 937; AI 967, 977; CGG, ed. and tr. J. H. Todd (1867, public domain);
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Limerick (read).

## 3. Corcach (Cork)

- **Name then:** *Corcach Mór Muman*, "the great marsh of Munster". No Norse name is known for it.
- **Modern place:** Cork: the church on the ridge south of the river (St Fin Barre's), the Foreigners' town on the
  marsh island below it (South Main Street). **51.897 N, 8.475 W.**
- **Active:** the monastery from the 7th century to the end; a Norse fort by 848; a second settlement about 915 (?, CGG);
  a small town in the 11th century.
- **Kind:** in 793 a great monastery with its own market, the chief church of south Munster. Later a monastery with a
  Foreigners' trading town beside it.
- **Who ruled:** the abbot, of the kin of the local kings, under the king of Cashel. The monastery was raided about
  822 and 839 (?). In 848 the king of Munster attacked "the fort of the Foreigners" at Cork (AFM 846 = 848). In 867 a
  chief of the Foreigners of Cork was killed (?). From the 980s under Brian; in the 11th century under his heirs, with
  the local kings pressing in.
- **Traded:** out: hides, wool, butter, grain, books and church work. In: wine for the Mass and the abbot's table
  (Munster's wine came from the Loire and Bordeaux), salt, oil, silver.
- **How a boat comes in:** through a narrow mouth into one of the great natural harbours of Europe, sheltered from
  every wind, then about 10 sea miles up among islands and mudbanks to where the Lee splits into channels through the
  marsh. Tidal all the way. Lie in a channel beside the island.
- **Why go:** the best shelter on the south coast; a rich church that buys wine in 793 when no town in Ireland exists.
- **Evidence:** annals. Digging on the island has found timber houses and plank paths, the oldest timbers felled in
  the later 11th century (?); nothing of the 9th-century fort has been found.
- **Sources:** AFM 846 (= 848); CGG; Hurley, "Viking Age towns: archaeological evidence from Waterford and Cork" in
  *Early Medieval Munster* (1998) (?); Jefferies, "The history and topography of Viking Cork", *JCHAS* 90 (1985).

## 4. Veisafjǫrðr / Loch Garman (Wexford)

- **Name then:** Irish *Loch Garman*; Norse *Veisafjǫrðr*, "the fjord of the mud flats" (?).
- **Modern place:** Wexford town. **52.337 N, 6.462 W.**
- **Active:** Foreigners "of Loch Garman" are named with those of Waterford in a defeat of about 892 (AFM 888) (?);
  again in the 930s (?). A town by about 1000 to the end.
- **Kind:** a small Foreigners' town, the smallest of the five, on a shallow harbour.
- **Who ruled:** its own chiefs, never called kings in the annals. The land behind is Uí Chennselaig, south Leinster:
  from the 1040s Diarmait son of Máel na mBó, who took Dublin in 1052 and sheltered Harold Godwinson's house.
- **Traded:** out: grain, hides, captives, fish. In: English silver, cloth, iron, wine. Its natural partners are St
  Davids, Bristol and Chester.
- **How a boat comes in:** over a bar of shifting sand at the mouth of a wide, very shallow harbour. A small boat is
  the right boat here: she goes in at half tide where a deep ship waits. Carnsore Point and the Tuskar rock to the
  south are the worst corner on this coast: tide races and rocks awash.
- **Why go:** the shortest crossing from Ireland to Wales (about 50 sea miles to St Davids (?)); cheap Leinster grain.
- **Evidence:** annals (thin). Digging at Bride Street found a row of houses of the Dublin kind rebuilt again and
  again on the same plots from about 1000 to 1300.
- **Sources:** AFM 888 (?); Bourke, "Two early eleventh century Viking houses from Bride Street, Wexford",
  *Journal of the Wexford Historical Society* 12 (1988-89); Hore, *History of the Town and County of Wexford* (1900-11).

## 5. Hwiterne / Candida Casa (Whithorn)

- **Name then:** Latin *Candida Casa*; English *Hwitærn*, *Hwiterne* ("white house"); Irish *Rosnat*, *Futerna* (?).
- **Modern place:** Whithorn in Galloway; its harbour is the Isle of Whithorn, about 5 km south-east. The row is for
  the harbour: **54.699 N, 4.365 W** (the church is at 54.735 N, 4.416 W).
- **Active:** all of the game. A Northumbrian bishop's church to the mid 9th century; a craft and trading place of
  Irish-Norse kind in the 10th and 11th.
- **Kind:** in 793 a bishop's minster with the shrine of Saint Ninian. Later a small market town around a church.
- **Who ruled:** Northumbria in 793: Bishop Badwulf (consecrated 791, last heard of about 803) is the last bishop
  named. The church burned about 845 (?). From about 870 the land belongs to the *Gall-Goídil*, Irish-speaking
  Northmen who give Galloway its name. Suibne, "king of the Gall-Goídil", died 1034 (AU). Echmarcach, driven from
  Dublin and Man, died in 1065 "king of the Rhinns" (Marianus Scotus). OS says Earl Thorfinn stayed in these parts.
- **Traded:** made here in the 10th-11th centuries: antler combs, leather, iron, and furs (many skinned cats) (?). In
  the Northumbrian time: wine, glass, coin (many copper *stycas* were found). Out: hides, wool, cattle.
- **How a boat comes in:** a small rock-girt harbour on the east side of the point, dry at low water, safe from the
  west. Burrow Head just south has a hard tide race. Then an hour's walk to the church.
- **Why go:** Ninian's shrine (pilgrims pay their way); the halfway house between Dublin, Man and the Solway; in 793
  the only bishop's household between Chester and the Clyde.
- **Evidence:** Bede (III.4) for the church; the bishops' list; Symeon of Durham says Cuthbert's community came here
  with the saint's body about 880 after their ship was beaten back from the Irish crossing and the gospel book fell
  in the sea (?). Excavations 1984-1991 found the minster's halls and church, the burning, and then houses of the
  Dublin kind with workshop waste.
- **Sources:** Hill, *Whithorn and St Ninian: The Excavation of a Monastic Town 1984-91* (1997); Bede, *HE* III.4;
  Symeon, *Historia Dunelmensis Ecclesiae* II.11-12 (?); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whithorn (read).

## 6. Alt Clut (Dumbarton Rock)

- **Name then:** British *Alt Clut*, Irish *Ail Cluaithe*, "rock of the Clyde"; Bede's *Alcluith*.
- **Modern place:** Dumbarton Rock, where the Leven meets the Clyde. **55.936 N, 4.563 W.**
- **Active:** from before the game to 870. After 870 the kingdom's centre moves upriver (Govan, no. 14).
- **Kind:** royal seat: the fortress of the kings of the Britons of the Clyde.
- **Who ruled:** British kings (little more than names: Dumnagual (?), then Artgal who was king in 870). In 870 Amlaíb
  and Ímar of Dublin besieged it for four months until the well dried, then plundered and wrecked it (AU 870; the well
  is from FA). In 871 they came back to Dublin with 200 ships and a great prey of English, Britons and Picts (AU 871).
  Artgal was killed in 872 on the advice of Constantine, king of the Picts.
- **Traded:** in: wine, glass and fine pots from Gaul (found on the Rock, mostly 6th-8th century); salt. Out: hides,
  cattle, hounds (?), captives.
- **How a boat comes in:** up the Firth of Clyde, some 40 sea miles of sheltered water behind Arran and Bute. The
  river shoals above the Rock; a boat lies in the mouth of the Leven under the east side. Tidal.
- **Why go:** the last British king's hall in the north, and a real customer in 793: wine for hides. The 870 siege and
  the 871 slave fleet are among the best dated scenes on this sea.
- **Evidence:** AU 870, 871, 872; Bede I.1. Excavations 1974-1975 found a timber and rubble rampart burned down (taken
  to be 870), Gaulish pottery and glass, and two pieces of Norse metalwork (a sword pommel and a lead weight) (?).
- **Sources:** AU; Alcock and Alcock, "Reconnaissance excavations ... Alt Clut, Clyde Rock, Strathclyde, 1974-75",
  *PSAS* 120 (1990); Clarkson, *Strathclyde and the Anglo-Saxons in the Viking Age* (2014).

## 7. Holmr / Inis Pátraic (Peel, Isle of Man)

- **Name then:** the island is *Mon*, *Manau*, Norse *Mǫn*. The islet at Peel is St Patrick's Isle, *Insula Patricii*,
  later *Holm*; what it was called in the game's years is not recorded (?). Use "Mǫn" on the chart and "Holmr" for the landing.
- **Modern place:** Peel, west coast of Man: the tidal islet at the mouth of the Neb. **54.226 N, 4.698 W.**
- **Active:** all of the game: a church site before the Norse, a Norse burial place in the 10th century, a fort and
  the kings' seat by the end of the 11th.
- **Kind:** a church and landing; later a chieftain's harbour. Man has no town in these years.
- **Who ruled:** British kings to the 9th century (?). Norse settlers by about 900; the Dublin exiles of 902 came
  here. 914 a sea-fight off Man between Ragnall and Bárid (AU). Maccus and Gofraid, sons of Aralt, "kings of the
  Isles", about 970-989; a battle at Man in 987 with a thousand dead (AU). The earls of Orkney about 990-1014 and
  under Thorfinn (OS). An English fleet wastes Man in 1000 (ASC). Echmarcach about 1052-1061; tribute to Dublin and
  Leinster from 1061. In 1066 Godred Crovan fled here from Stamford Bridge (Chronicle of Man) and won the island in 1079.
- **Traded:** out: grain, cattle, herring, lead (?). In: silver, soapstone, whetstones, combs, wine.
  Man is where silver comes to rest: about twenty hoards of 950-1070 (?), and its own pennies, copied from Dublin's,
  from about 1025 (?).
- **How a boat comes in:** an open bay on the west side with the islet as a breakwater; boats lie on the sand in the
  river mouth behind it. Safe from the east, bad in a north-westerly. The tides round the Calf of Man at the south
  end are fierce. The east-side landings (Ramsey, *Hramsá*; Ronaldsway) serve for England.
- **Why go:** the middle of the Irish Sea: about one day's sail to Ireland, Galloway, Cumbria or Wales. News from
  all four meets here. The yearly assembly at Tynwald at midsummer (the mound is 4 km inland from Peel; its age is not proven) (?).
- **Evidence:** excavations on the islet 1982-1987 found a Christian graveyard with several heathen-style burials of
  the 10th century, among them a woman with a necklace of some seventy beads from many lands. Ship burials at
  Balladoole and Knock y Doonee; some thirty runic crosses of about 930-1020 (Gautr's among them); the hoards.
- **Sources:** Freke, *Excavations on St Patrick's Isle, Peel, 1982-88* (2002); Wilson, *The Vikings in the Isle of
  Man* (2008); AU 914, 987; ASC 1000; *Chronica Regum Manniae*, tr. Munch and Goss (1874, public domain).

## 8. Byrgisey (Birsay, Orkney)

- **Name then:** *Byrgisey*, "fort island", and the district *Byrgisherað*; the islands are *Orkneyjar*.
- **Modern place:** the Brough of Birsay and Birsay Bay, north-west Mainland, Orkney. **59.137 N, 3.335 W.**
- **Active:** all of the game: a Pictish lord's place with fine bronze-casting in the 8th century; Norse from the 9th;
  the earls' chief seat in the 11th.
- **Kind:** royal seat (an earl's hall and, from about 1050, a bishop's church).
- **Who ruled:** Picts in 793. Norse earls from about 870-890 by saga tradition (Rognvald of Møre's kin: Sigurd,
  Torf-Einar) (?). Sigurd the Stout, about 985 to 1014, killed at Clontarf. Thorfinn the Mighty, about 1020 to 1065,
  who went to Rome about 1050 and came home to build Christchurch at Birsay, the first bishop's seat in Orkney (OS;
  Adam of Bremen). His sons Paul and Erlend sailed with Harald of Norway in 1066: the king's fleet lay in Orkney that
  summer before Stamford Bridge.
- **Traded:** out: barley and oats (Orkney feeds Shetland and sells north), butter, dried cod and ling (growing fast
  from about 1000), wool, seal and whale oil. In: timber (there are no trees), soapstone pots from Shetland and
  Norway, whetstones, reindeer-antler combs, iron, wine and wax for the church. Silver by weight: the Skaill hoard
  (buried about 950-970, some 8 kg) is the largest in Scotland.
- **How a boat comes in:** the islet joins the land by a causeway that dries for about two hours each side of low
  water. Boats are hauled up in the boat-slips on the point facing it. The bay is open to the Atlantic: in a westerly,
  do not try. The safe anchorages are on the other side of the island (Scapa Flow, the bay of Kirkwall).
- **Why go:** the earl's hall is the court of the north: from 1014 he rules or taxes from Shetland to Man. Every ship
  between Norway and Dublin passes Orkney and pays him or is his guest. Grain can be had when Norway is hungry.

## 9. Hjaltland (Jarlshof, Sumburgh, Shetland)

- **Name then:** the islands are *Hjaltland*. The farm's own name is lost: "Jarlshof" was made up by Walter Scott in
  1821. The headland is *Dynrastarnes* (?), from *Dynrǫst*, the roaring tide race off it.
- **Modern place:** Jarlshof at Sumburgh, the south tip of Mainland, Shetland. **59.869 N, 1.291 W.**
- **Active:** Pictish farm in 793; a Norse farm from the 9th century, lived in without a break to the 1300s.
- **Kind:** village: a big farm of several longhouses by a landing. Shetland has no town and no market place we can name.
- **Who ruled:** Picts to about 800; Norse farmers after; the earls of Orkney from the late 9th century (saga
  tradition) to the end of the game.
- **Traded:** out: soapstone pots, lamps and sinkers (quarried at Cunningsburgh, a day's row north, and on Unst),
  wadmal, dried fish, seal and whale oil, feathers. In: grain (never enough), timber, iron, combs.
- **How a boat comes in:** onto the sand at the head of the West Voe, sheltered from north and east. Sumburgh Roost off
  the head is a tide race that can swamp a small boat in any wind against tide: come in at slack water. Fair Isle
  (*Friðarey*) is the stepping stone from Orkney, some 25 sea miles each way.
- **Why go:** the first landfall from western Norway (about 190 sea miles (?): two days with a fair wind) and the
  crossroads for the Faroes and Iceland; soapstone at the quarry price; a grain-hungry people.
- **Evidence:** excavated in the 1930s and 1950s: the first Norse longhouse found in Britain; 150 loom weights,
  soapstone, line sinkers, and slates scratched with ships and men's faces.

## 10. Þórshǫfn (Tórshavn, Faroes)

- **Name then:** *Þórshǫfn*, "Thor's harbour"; the islands *Færeyjar*, "sheep islands". The point is *Tinganes*.
- **Modern place:** Tinganes, Tórshavn, Streymoy. **62.007 N, 6.772 W.**
- **Active:** Irish hermits were on the islands for about a hundred years before Northmen drove them off: Dicuil,
  writing in 825, says so. Norse farms from the 9th century. The assembly met on Tinganes each summer from
  about 900 (?) (FS; no 10th-century building has been found on the point).
- **Kind:** market: an assembly place with a summer market on a bare rock point, not a town. Nobody lives there all year (?).
- **Who ruled:** the chief farmers at the assembly. By saga: Tróndur of Gøta against Sigmundur Brestisson, who

## 11. Eyrar (Eyrarbakki, Iceland)

- **Name then:** *Eyrar*, "the gravel banks"; the island *Ísland*.
- **Modern place:** Eyrarbakki, by the mouth of the Ölfusá, south coast. **63.863 N, 21.150 W.**
- **Active:** from the settlement (about 870-930) to the end of the game and long after. The chief harbour of the
  south in the sagas (?).
- **Kind:** market: a summer landing where ships are drawn up and their goods sold from booths. No town in Iceland.
- **Who ruled:** nobody. The chieftains (*goðar*) set prices on a ship's goods when she lands. One law and a yearly
  assembly at Þingvellir (a long day's ride inland) from 930; Christian by law from 999 or 1000; a bishop, Ísleifr, from
  1056 at Skálholt, also about a day's ride from Eyrar. From about 1022 an agreement with King Olaf of Norway fixes
  what an Icelander pays in Norway, and Norwegians' rights here.
- **Traded:** out: wadmal (the island's money: six ells to an ounce of silver (?)), shaggy cloaks, sulphur, falcons,
  fleeces, walrus ivory and hide rope in the first generations. In: grain and malt, timber, tar, iron, linen, wax,
  wine and incense for the church, weapons.
- **How a boat comes in:** a low coast of black sand with surf and almost no shelter for 200 sea miles; the skerries
  off Eyrar make a gap. Come in at high water in quiet weather and haul her out at once. Ships come in early
  summer and most cannot leave before the next: a trader winters as some farmer's guest.
- **Why go:** grain and timber sell very dear, and a falcon bought here is a king's gift in England or Germany. But it
  is a two-year voyage and a wreck coast.
- **Evidence:** Ldn and Íslendingabók (Ari, about 1125) for the settlement and the law; the settlement ash layer of
  about 877 dates the first farms; the Olaf agreement survives in the law-book *Grágás*. Sailing: Ldn gives seven
  days from Stad in Norway to Horn in east Iceland. No Viking-age harbour works have been dug at Eyrar itself (?).
- **Sources:** Ari, *Íslendingabók*; Ldn (tr. Ellwood 1898, public domain); *Grágás*; Gelsinger, *Icelandic
  Enterprise: Commerce and Economy in the Middle Ages* (1981); Byock, *Viking Age Iceland* (2001).

## 12. Woodstown (the first ship-fort on the Suir)

## 25. Loch Cuan / Strangfjǫrðr (Strangford Lough, with Nendrum)

A sea-lough with a narrow mouth where the tide runs at seven knots or more (the Norse name means "strong fjord").
Fleets lay in it in 877 (when Fair and Dark heathens fought and a king of the Dark fell), in 924-926 and 933 (AU) (?).
On an island in it, the monastery of Nendrum (*Nóendruim*) had the oldest dated tide mill known (timber of 619-621);
its abbot was burned in his house in 976. **54.498 N, 5.647 W** (Nendrum). **Leave out**: no lasting trading place,
and a mouth that is a trap for the unwary; better as a card and a hazard on the chart. Bangor (*Bennchor*), the great
monastery just north on Belfast Lough, raided 823-824, is the alternative if Ulster needs a port.
Sources: AU 877, 924, 926, 976; McErlean and Crothers, *Harnessing the Tides: The Early Medieval Tide Mills at
Nendrum Monastery* (2007).

  good small thing for a curious player to find, and proof that merchants lay there.

  Limerick. Inland and past rapids; worth a place if the Shannon is baked as a river.

# What the shared tables would need (not added by me)

- **Realm ids that do not exist yet:** `gaill` (the Foreigners' towns in Ireland: by year their lord is Dublin's
  dynasty, then Munster or Leinster), `strathclyde`, `isles` (Man and the Hebrides), `picts` (Alba from 900), `orkney`
  (the earldom, from about 870-890), `faroese`, `icelanders`. `isles`, `picts` and `orkney` already exist as "parts" in
  `Design/content/realms/irish_sea.json`. In the rows, `realm` is the lord in 793, or at the founding where the place
  is later, as the table's header says. Birsay and Jarlshof therefore say `picts`, and pass to `orkney` in the 9th century.
- **Region keys that do not exist yet:** `galloway`, `strathclyde`, `man`, `hebrides`, `orkney`, `shetland`, `faroes`,
  `iceland`, `pictland`. What they make and want is under "Traded" in each entry. In short: the Irish towns sell
  hides, captives and wool and buy wine, salt and silver; the northern islands sell wadmal, dried fish, oil, soapstone
  and feathers and buy grain, timber and iron; Iceland adds sulphur and falcons.
- **The table has no way to change a place's kind, size or realm by year.** Cork (monastery, then town), Whithorn
  (minster, then craft town), Birsay and Jarlshof (Pictish, then Norse) and the existing `duiblinn` (still "monastery,
  size 1" for all years, though it is the biggest town on this sea after 917) all need it. The places files' `eras` could carry this.
- **A rule that keeps small boats off the ocean** for `thorshofn`, `eyrar` and `gasir`.
- **Wintering:** an Iceland voyage should cost a whole year.
