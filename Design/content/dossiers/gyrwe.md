# Gyrwe (Jarrow): dossier

Port `gyrwe`: Gyrwe, modern Jarrow, 54.98 N 1.474 W; realm northumbria, region northumbria, kind monastery, landing beach, size 2 [ports]. Researched 4 Oct 2026. Source keys are spelled out in section 9. (?) marks what I could not check; (memory) is known to me but not opened. Web searching was shut for this task and page fetching closed part-way through, so more than usual rests on memory.

**Read this first.** This is Bede's house, fifty-eight years after his death: St Paul's, one monastery with St Peter's at Wearmouth. Its rich record stops in 735. The game's years have four fixed points: Alcuin's warning (793), the raid and the wreck (794), the taking of Bede's bones (about 1022), and the ruin found in 1074. Between them the texts are nearly silent.

## 1. The place in 793

- **What it is.** The monastery of St Paul, on a low rise where the small river Don meets the Tyne, on the south bank about 5 km above the river mouth; St Peter's is about 10 km south-east on the Wear (both my measure on the map). Symeon, after Bede: "The monastery of Peter and Paul, situated at the mouth of the river Wear, and near the river Tyne, at a place called Jarrow" [LDE]. Gyrwe means "fen dwellers", perhaps for the Slake; but for Norman tongues the name would now be "Yarrow" [enwp Jarrow].
- **Who rules.** King Æthelred I, until he is killed at Corbridge, up this same river, on 18 April 796; Osbald for 27 days; Eardwulf from 14 May 796 [repo chronicle]. The land was King Ecgfrith's gift, forty hides [HA]. The bishop is Hexham's: one ordained Bede [enwp Bede]. The abbot of 793 is not known by name (section 3).
- **Who lives there, how many.** Monks, boys given to the house (Bede came at seven [EH]) and craftsmen. The two houses had "about six hundred brethren" in 716 [HA]; there is no figure for 793. Of 111 sure burials, 39 were infants, children or youths [HER 1227], so lay families were buried here too (my inference). Size 2 is fair.
- **Coming in.** The Tyne mouth has a bar with about 1.8 m over it at low water, Herd Sand to the south, and to the north the Black Middens rocks, covered at half flood. Springs range 4.6 m, neaps 2.4 m: modern figures carried back [repo water]. On the north headland is the monastery of Tynemouth, where King Osred was buried in 792 [ASC; HR]. On the south headland, the Lawe, lies the Roman fort [enwp Arbeia] (how much stood is not known), with a house of nuns near it in Bede's day [VCH]. Inside, the river is brown and shallow, with bars and islands. Jarrow Slake opens on the south side: a broad tidal mudflat where the Don comes in [repo water; enwp Jarrow].
- **Where boats lie.** Not recorded. The Slake is a natural beaching ground [repo water]; the guesthouse and workshops were down by the river [EH]. Symeon, about 1110, calls Jarrow "the port of king Ecgfrid" [LDE]: the only text that puts ships here.

## 2. What stood there

- **The great church of St Paul**, dedicated 23 April 685. Its dedication stone survives in the tower wall; it names the fifteenth year of King Ecgfrith and the fourth of Abbot Ceolfrith [enwp Abbey; GEC]. Chancel, nave with four side chambers (porticus) on each side, west porch [HER 1229]. Pulled down in 1782-83; it lies under the present nave [EH desc].
- **The east church**, in line with it: the present chancel, standing to the roof. Three small first-build windows in the south wall held coloured glass. The stone was probably robbed from Roman sites [EH desc].
- **The tower** joining the two is later: 9th to 12th century in stages [EH desc], or late 11th [HER 1229]. In 793 the churches most likely stood apart.
- **Two long stone halls** south of the churches. One had floor settings, perhaps for benches, and fish bones: the refectory. The other had a large, finely decorated room with a stone seat for the abbot [EH]. From memory only (?): about 28 by 8 m and 18 by 8 m, floored in pink Roman-style concrete.
- **By the river**: a guesthouse with painted plaster and coloured glass, workshops for metal and glass, and terraced gardens on the south slope [EH].
- **The paintings** Benedict brought from Rome for St Paul's, pairing Old Testament and New: Isaac with the wood and Christ with the cross; Moses' serpent and the crucifixion [HA].
- **The graveyard** south of the church: about 132 burials, heads to the west, plain earth graves, ten with coffin stains, almost no goods (glass beads, one pot, an iron pin with a silver head). 59 graves had builders' rubble in the fill: plaster, lead, window glass [HER 1227; HER 1228].
- **Ditches** at Church Bank, north of Jarrow Hall, with 7th-8th century pottery; a large one may be the monastery's bound [HER 1232; HER 1233].
- **Carved stones**: baluster shafts, a socket with letters, and a cross slab whose Latin says that in this sign life is given back to the world [HER 1224; HER 1225; enwp StPaul].
- **Bede's porch**, on the north side of St Paul's, consecrated in his honour: Symeon's words [LDE]. Its date is not known.
- **Not found**: a writing room, library, quay, well, wall, market or mint.
- **Near by**: Tynemouth and St Oswine's shrine [enwp Tynemouth]; the nuns at the Lawe [VCH]; Monkton, two miles off, Bede's birthplace by later tradition [enwp Bede].

## 3. Who could be met

- **Æthelbald** and **Friduin** (Frithuwine), abbots of Wearmouth and Jarrow: Alcuin wrote to each on his election, between 764 and 804 [VCH]; one letter opens "Sanctissimo patri Friduino" [Alc]. Which ruled in 793 is not known.
- **Alcuin** (died 804), in Francia, wrote to the brothers after Lindisfarne: "Vos maritima habitatis, unde pestis primo ingruit", you live by the sea, from which the plague first came (our rendering) [Alc; repo chronicle].
- The dead whom every monk can name: Benedict Biscop, founder (died 12 January 690); Ceolfrith, first abbot here (died at Langres, 25 September 716); Hwætberht (abbot 716 to the 740s); Cuthbert, abbot in 764; Bede (died 26 May 735) [HA; VCH; enwp Biscop; enwp Hwaet; enwp Bede].
- Bishops of Hexham: Æthelberht (789 to 16 October 797), Heardred (29 October 797 to 800), Eanberht (800 to 813), Tidfrith (813 to 821), the last; the see then lapses [enwp Hexham].
- Kings after Eardwulf: Ælfwald II, Eanred, then Osberht and Ælla, both killed at York in 867 [repo chronicle].
- **Halfdan**, 875: "entering the Tyne with a considerable fleet" [LDE].
- **Guthred**, king at York from about 883 (died 894 [HR]), with Abbot Eadred and Bishop Eardwulf of St Cuthbert's people [LDE; enwp HSC].
- **Elfred son of Westou**, priest and sacrist of Durham under bishops Edmund, Æthelric and Æthelwine, active about 1020 to after 1056: here every year on Bede's day [LDE; enwp Westou].
- **Harald of Norway**, 1066, at the river mouth [HR].
- Ordinary people the evidence shows: scribes and singers; oblate boys; glass and metal workers; masons (the first came from Gaul [HA]); whoever caught the refectory's fish; families burying children by the church; "the Angles" who killed the raiders' chief in 794 [HR]. No moneyer, reeve or merchant is recorded.
- Beornwine and Sigewine are the game's own, invented [spine].

## 4. What was traded

No market, fair, toll or mint is recorded here.

- **Out: books.** Boniface asked Abbot Hwætberht for Bede's works in 744-747 [Kylie; enwp Hwaet]. Ceolfrith had three whole Bibles made; the one sent to the Pope survives (Codex Amiatinus): 1,030 leaves, over 75 pounds, the skins of 515 cattle [enwp Amiatinus].
- **In, for book and altar**: calfskin, pigments, wax, wine, oil, incense [repo seasons]. Bede's own box in 735 held pepper, napkins and incense [enwp Bede]: pepper reached Jarrow.
- **Gifts as trade.** Two silk cloaks from Rome bought three hides by the Wear from King Aldfrith; a book of the Cosmographers bought eight hides by the river Fresca [HA]. Boniface sent a coverlet of goat hair and asked for a "cloak" [enwp Hwaet; Kylie]; the Latin is cloccam, a bell (memory) (?). In 764 Abbot Cuthbert sent Lul of Mainz Bede's lives of St Cuthbert, knives and an otter-skin robe, and asked for a glassmaker and a harper; the winter had numbed his scribes' hands (memory) (?).
- **Glass.** Glaziers were fetched from Gaul for Wearmouth [HA]; glass was worked on the Jarrow riverside [EH].
- **Finds.** "Imported pottery, coins" are on show [enwp JarrowHall]; the kinds are not given.
- **Coin.** Northumbria's own: about 8,000 copper-alloy stycas lay in a bucket at Hexham, 39 km up the Tyne, buried about 850 [repo hoards].
- **The land.** Barley, oats, wool, hides, salmon [goods_793; repo seasons]. In 1074 the vill of Jarrow came with Preston, Monkton, Hedworth, Hebburn, Westoe and Harton [VCH]; whether these were the monks' farms in 793 is not known.
- **Ships.** The game keeps one minster coaster here [repo traffic]. No ship has been found. The Tyne is tidal for about 30 km [repo water].
- **Seasons.** In 793 the famine makes grain dear [repo chronicle]. Bede's day drew visitors by about 1020 [LDE].

## 5. The years 793 to 1066

- 792, 14 September: Osred, once king, is killed. "His body is deposited at Tinemouth" [ASC].
- 793: portents, famine, Lindisfarne on 8 June; Alcuin's letter [repo chronicle; Alc].
- 794: the raid. The heathens "plundered the monastery of King Everth at the mouth of the Wear. There, however, some of their leaders were slain; and some of their ships also were shattered to pieces by the violence of the weather" [ASC; "Wear" is Ingram's misreading of Donemuthan]. Symeon: "ravaging the harbour of king Ecgfrid, plundered the monastery at the mouth of the river Don. But St. Cuthbert did not allow them to depart unpunished; for their chief was put to a cruel death by the Angles" [HR].
- 796, 18 April: King Æthelred killed at Corbridge [repo chronicle].
- 800: Wendover alone has the heathens plunder Hartness and Tynemouth (?); 24 December, a gale and a sea flood [repo chronicle].
- 810: cattle plague through Britain [repo chronicle].
- About 850: the Hexham hoard is buried [repo hoards].
- 867 (my reading of the context (?)): after York, "the barbarians advanced no further north than the mouth of the river Tyne" [LDE].
- 875: "Halfdene, the king of the Danes ... entering the Tyne with a considerable fleet, he landed at Tornemuthe" (so printed) [LDE] and "fixed his winter-quarters by the river Tine" [ASC]. Wikipedia has him sack Tynemouth and Jarrow [enwp Jarrow]; I did not find Jarrow named in Symeon (?).
- Later 9th century: the monastery ends. "About 860 ... finally abandoned in the late 9th century" [enwp Abbey]; the county history cites only Matthew Paris [VCH]. No year is safe.
- About 883: Guthred gives St Cuthbert "the whole of the district lying between the Wear and the Tyne" [LDE]. Jarrow is now Cuthbert's land.
- About 1022: Elfred carries Bede's bones to Durham [LDE; VCH].
- 1065: St Oswine's bones are raised at Tynemouth [HR; enwp Tynemouth].
- 1066: "Harold Harvager, king of the Norwegians ... came unexpectedly to the mouth of the river Tyne with a very powerful fleet" [HR].
- After the game: in 1069 Bishop Æthelwine, fleeing with St Cuthbert's body, sleeps the first night in St Paul's, and King William's army burns the church; in 1074 Aldwin finds "many buildings of the monks with half-ruined churches"; in 1083 the monks go to Durham [HR; LDE; VCH].

## 6. Faith and custom

- **St Paul** here, St Peter at Wearmouth; their feast is 29 June (memory). The dedication day is 23 April [GEC].
- **The house's own dead**: Benedict, 12 January [enwp Biscop]; Bede, 26 May [LDE]; Ceolfrith, 25 September [HA].
- **Roman ways.** The chant was taught by John, archcantor of St Peter's in Rome; a papal letter guards the house from outside meddling [HA].
- **Reckoning.** Bede's book on time (725) and his Easter tables were made here (memory); the game's riddles already use this [repo riddles].
- **Burial**: west-east, unfurnished, by the church [HER 1227].
- **The old gods**: no trace here. Bede tells of country people at the Tyne mouth jeering at monks blown out to sea on timber rafts: "Nobody shall pray for them: may God spare none of them! for they have taken away from men the ancient rites and customs" [VCuth ch. 3]. The monastery was the one south of the river mouth, later the nuns' house; the date, about 650, is from memory (?).
- **The wreck of 794** was read as St Cuthbert's punishment [HR].
- **Sanctuary**: from about 883, thirty-seven days' safety for anyone who flees to Cuthbert's body [LDE]: at Chester-le-Street, not here.
- Alcuin scolds the house for boys who dig out foxes and course hares instead of singing (memory) (?).

## 7. Stories and oddities

- In the plague of 686 every monk here who could read or chant died, save Ceolfrith "and one little boy" [VCH]: almost certainly Bede, about fourteen [enwp Bede].
- One Bible took 515 calves; its dedication was later altered and it passed for an Italian book [enwp Amiatinus].
- Elfred came yearly for Bede's day, left early and alone one year, and "did not trouble himself ever again to visit that said monastery of Jarrow, but he conducted himself like a person who had secured the object of his desires" [LDE]. Some scholars doubt the tale [enwp Westou].
- "Bede's chair" in the church is younger than Bede [enwp StPaul; GEC].
- Abbot Hwætberht, called Eusebius, is thought to have written sixty Latin riddles [enwp Hwaet; repo riddles].
- The oldest church dedication stone in England [EH desc].

## 8. What we do not know, and what the writer must not claim

- That Donemuthan is surely Jarrow. The Chronicle names only Ecgfrith's monastery; "that is, Jarrow" is Symeon's gloss three centuries on [LDE]; other sites have been argued (memory) (?).
- The raiders' homeland, number, season or chief's name; one leader or several; that the house burned in 794 (the texts say plundered).
- The abbot, the head count or the state of the library in 793; the year the monks left; who served the church in the 10th century.
- A market, fair, toll, mint, quay, well, wall or ale-house. "Port of king Ecgfrid" is one late phrase: give Ecgfrith no fleet here.
- "The largest library north of the Alps" [enwp Jarrow] and glass "from about AD 600" [enwp Abbey]: modern boasts; the house began in 681.
- Bede's chair, Bede's Well and Monkton as fact; anything marked (memory).
- Coal, salt pans and shipyards: later Tyneside. The table's "beach" is a mudflat.
- Quotation: Stevenson (1855), Giles and Ingram are safe; Alcuin's Latin is ours to render. Do not copy English Heritage or Wikipedia wording.
