# Legacæstir (Chester): dossier

Port `legacaestir`: Legacæstir, modern Chester, 53.19 N 2.891 W; realm mercia, region mercia_west, kind town, landing riverbank, size 2 [ports_793.json]. Researched 6 Oct 2026. Source keys are in section 9. (?) marks what I could not check; (memory) is known to me but the page was not opened (the search ration was spent, so more rests on memory than I would like).

**Read this first.** In 793 this is a Roman shell with a church or two and very little else that can be proved. A hundred years later the Chronicle still calls it a waste city. Everything that makes Chester a port (walls mended, mint, Dublin trade, moneyers, the laws) begins in 907 and is at its height about 920 to 970. The writer should make 793 quiet and let the place wake in 907.

## 1. The place in 793

- **What it is.** The legionary fortress of Deva, 25 ha (62 acres), a fifth larger than York's, on the south end of a sandstone ridge 42 m high in an S-bend of the Dee [enwp Deva; enwp Chester]. Bede calls it a city: in English Legacæstir, in British Carlegion, in Latin the City of the Legions [Bede II.2 (memory); VCH; enwp Chester]. The Welsh: Cair Legion [Historia Brittonum, via enwp Chester].
- **Who rules.** Mercia since soon after the battle of about 616 [VCH]. In 793 the king is Offa (died 29 July 796), then Ecgfrith for 141 days, then Coenwulf (796-821) (memory). No reeve, lord or bishop is named at Chester before the 10th century. It lies in the diocese of Lichfield (memory).
- **Who lives there.** Not known. Fifth or sixth century amphora sherds inside the fortress show somebody of rank after Rome; then almost nothing until late 9th-century hut remains, a brooch and Carolingian jar sherds on Lower Bridge Street, by the river [VCH]. VCH warns that "a deserted city" in 893 need not mean empty for three centuries. Clergy at St John's, a few households by the landing, are a fair guess and only a guess (?).
- **Coming in.** The estuary is 23 km long, between the Point of Ayr and Hilbre Point, with Hilbre Island in its mouth [enwp Dee]. The old channel kept to the Wirral side, past Parkgate, Burton, Shotwick, Saughall and Blacon [enwp Dee]. The weir is Norman (Earl Hugh, after 1070) [enwp Dee], so in our years the tide ran up to and past the town (inference). West of the walls, where the Roodee is now, was marsh and water [enwp Chester].
- **Landmarks.** The red sandstone walls, about 4.9 m high in Roman times, the west wall still largely standing in the 10th century [enwp Walls; VCH]; the bath-house, whose walls once rose 16 m [enwp Deva], how much stood in 793 is not known (?).
- **Where boats lie.** On the river bank south of the fortress, below the later Bridge Street: the "early harbour", where the clearest pre-Conquest finds come from [VCH]. Outside the estuary, Meols on the north Wirral shore, a beach market since the Iron Age with Anglo-Saxon and Viking-age finds [enwp Meols].

## 2. What stood there

- **The Roman walls and gates.** Standing but unrepaired until 907. Then mended; a wall probably run from the south-east corner down to the river, making an L with the Dee as the other two sides; a gravel road laid inside the west wall [VCH; enwp Walls]. Wolfeld's Gate stood in the new stretch before 1066 [VCH].
- **St John's**, outside the walls to the south-east, on the cliff above the river beside the ruined amphitheatre. Founded by King Æthelred of Mercia in 689 by late tradition only [enwp St John's; VCH "possibly"]. Tied to the bishops of Lichfield. In 1066 a dean and seven canons. A masons' yard here cut circle-headed grave crosses from the red stone of "Redcliff"; the type is found in Wirral and Flintshire too (10th to 11th century (?)) [VCH; enwp St John's]. A church of St Mary stood beside it by 1066 [VCH].
- **The minster of St Werburgh** (earlier St Peter and St Paul by tradition), in the north-east quarter of the fortress. Certainly there by 958, when King Edgar gave it land; probably founded or refounded by Æthelflæd after 907, with Werburgh's body brought from Hanbury. The date 875 for that is late and unreliable [VCH; enwp Cathedral; enwp Æthelflæd]. In 1066 a warden and twelve canons [VCH]. Possible Saxon masonry found under the nave in 1997 [enwp Cathedral].
- **St Peter's**, in the market place at the cross of the Roman streets, by 1066; tradition says Æthelflæd dedicated it in 907 [VCH; enwp History (?)].
- **St Bridget's**, the Irish and Norse settlers' church, south of the fortress, probably from their arrival; **St Olave's**, not before 1030 (Olaf died that year) [VCH].
- **The amphitheatre**, 95.7 by 87.2 m, robbed for stone, a hollow in the ground [enwp Amphitheatre; enwp Deva].
- **Lower Bridge Street**: bow-sided huts of Scandinavian kind, cellared buildings (memory), an ingot mould (silver worked near the mint) [VCH].
- **Inside the fortress, 10th century**: sunken huts and timber halls at Hamilton Place, Crook Street, Hunter Street; bone-working at Abbey Green by the Northgate [VCH].
- **The mint**, from about 890 (probably), certainly signed from Æthelstan [VCH]. Its house is not found.
- **The bridge.** There was one before 1066: Domesday has the reeve call a man from every hide to mend the wall and the bridge [DB (memory)]. Not attested for 793 (?).
- **Henwald's Lowe**, a mound north-east of the walls (later Gorse Stacks): "Henwald's barrow", perhaps an early lord's grave [VCH].
- **Across the river**: Handbridge, taxed in carucates (a Norse mark); the Roman quarry with a rock-cut shrine of Minerva, still there today [VCH; enwp Deva]. "Edgar's Field" there is the traditional site of his hall, tradition only [enwp Edgar].
- **Hoards**: Castle Esplanade, buried about 965, coins with ingots and cut jewellery like Manx hoards (found 1950, about 520 coins, in a pot (memory)); Eastgate Street, about 970; Pemberton's Parlour, probably hidden at the raid of 980 [VCH].

## 3. Who could be met

Nobody is named at Chester in 793. Later:

- **Hingamund (Ingimund)**, Norse leader driven from Dublin in 902, beaten off Anglesey, settled in Wirral by Æthelflæd's leave, then attacked the town [FA, via enwp Ingimundr; VCH].
- **Æthelflæd**, Lady of the Mercians, refounded the burh 907; died 12 June 918 at Tamworth. Her husband **Æthelred**, lord of the Mercians (died 911), was ill through the siege story [enwp Æthelflæd; FA (memory)].
- **Edward the Elder**, put down the revolt of the Chester men and the Welsh in 924 and died at Farndon, up the Dee, 17 July 924 [VCH; date (memory)].
- **Æthelstan** (924-939): the mint's great years [VCH].
- **Irfara**, moneyer under Edward the Elder: a Norse nickname, "the Ireland-farer" [VCH]. Other moneyers' names from the coins, such as Mældomen, Oslac, Tidgar, Wulfgar, Boiga and later Gillicrist, Sweartcol, Colbrand, Huscarl (memory) (?): check against a coin catalogue before use. Norse and Gaelic names are near 40 per cent of the moneyers in the early 11th century [VCH].
- **Ealdormen**: Æthelmund (from 940), Ælfhere (after 965, died 983 (memory)), Leofwine (from the 990s) [VCH].
- **King Edgar**, here with his fleet in 973 [ASC 973].
- **Earls of Mercia**: Leofric (died 1057), who enriched both minsters, with his wife Godgifu (Godiva); Ælfgar, who brought his Irish hired fleet here to be paid off (1055 or 1058 (?)); Edwin (from about 1062) [VCH; enwp Cathedral].
- **Harold Godwinson**, based here against Gruffudd ap Llywelyn at Rhuddlan, 1063; **Ealdgyth**, Ælfgar's daughter, Gruffudd's widow and then Harold's queen, sent here by Edwin after Hastings [VCH].
- **Plegmund**, archbishop of Canterbury 890 to 914 or 923, a Mercian, by late tradition a hermit at Plemstall near the town before 890 [enwp Plegmund].
- **Ordinary people the evidence shows**: moneyers (7 in 1066), the king's reeve and the earl's reeve, twelve judges of the city, canons, masons at St John's, bone and antler workers, potters of Chester ware, Dublin skippers with marten skins, ale-wives (the bad-ale law), slaves for sale [VCH; DB (memory)].

## 4. What was traded

- **793**: nothing is known. A few Carolingian sherds on the river bank are the only sign of anything from oversea [VCH].
- **After 907, out**: Chester-ware pots (many in Dublin by Æthelstan's time), Cheshire salt for Dublin's fish and hides, cloth, disc brooches, and slaves: Welsh captives and people enslaved by law [VCH].
- **In**: marten skins from Dublin, the only import named before 1066; later hides, fish, farm goods [VCH]. A lead customs tag struck from Chester dies of Eadwig's reign turned up at Coppergate, York: Irish goods going on over the hills under the reeves' eye [VCH].
- **Coin.** About 16 moneyers by 916-918; at least 25 under Æthelstan, when it rivalled or passed London; about 17 to 955; about 20 in 970; five after Edgar's reform of 973; seven in 1066. Before 970 the coins carry no king's head, perhaps to suit Dublin. Chester cut dies for Derby and Tamworth [VCH]. Its pennies are thick in Irish and Manx hoards [VCH].
- **Tolls and law, as before 1066** [DB (memory) (?), VCH for the outline]: a ship coming or going without the king's leave, 40 shillings from every man aboard; a ship coming against the king's peace forfeit with her men and goods; with leave, they sell in peace and pay 4 pence a load on leaving, two parts to the king, one to the earl. The reeve may order marten skins shown to him first; 40 shillings if not. False measure 4 shillings; bad ale, the dung-stool or 4 shillings. A merchant who opens a bale between Saturday nones and Monday, or on a feast, without the bishop's man's leave: 4 shillings to the bishop.
- **The farm in 1066**: 45 pounds and three timbers of marten (120 skins) [VCH].
- **Ships**: Dublin and Manx Norse boats; the king's fleets in 973 and 1000 [ASC]. Wine ships from Aquitaine and Spain are 12th century, not ours [VCH].
- Seasons and fairs: none recorded before 1066 (?).

## 5. The years 793 to 1066

- c. 616. Æthelfrith of Northumbria beats the Welsh here; Bede has about 1,200 monks of Bangor killed [enwp Battle].
- 689. St John's founded (tradition) [enwp St John's].
- 793. Offa's Mercia. No event recorded.
- 893 (Chronicle's 894). A Danish army rides day and night to "a waste city in Wirral, which is called Legaceaster"; the English besiege it two days, take the cattle, burn the corn; the Danes go into Wales [ASC 894 (Ingram prints "western" for "waste"); VCH].
- c. 890. A mint in north-west Mercia, most likely here [VCH].
- 902. The Norse driven from Dublin; Hingamund to Anglesey, then Wirral [enwp Ingimundr].
- 907. "Chester was rebuilt" [ASC 907].
- 907 or 910. Hingamund's attack fails [FA; enwp Æthelflæd].
- 914, 915. Burhs at Eddisbury and Runcorn [ASC; enwp Ingimundr].
- 918. Æthelflæd dies; Edward takes Mercia [enwp Æthelflæd].
- 924. Chester revolts with the Welsh; Edward retakes it, dies at Farndon [VCH].
- 958. Edgar's grant to St Werburgh's [enwp Cathedral].
- c. 965. The Castle Esplanade hoard buried [VCH].
- 973. Edgar "led all his marine force to Chester; and there came to meet him six kings; and they all covenanted with him, that they would be his allies by sea and by land" [ASC 973, Ingram].
- 980. "The county of Chester was plundered by the pirate-army of the North" [ASC 980, Ingram].
- 1000. Æthelred wastes Cumberland; his fleet from Chester cannot meet him and harries Man [ASC 1000; VCH].
- 1016. Edmund and Uhtred plunder Staffordshire, Shrewsbury and Chester [ASC 1016].
- 1057. Leofric dies [VCH].
- 1063. Harold strikes Rhuddlan from here [VCH].
- 1066. 487 houses, perhaps 2,500 to 3,000 people [VCH].
- 1069-70. William takes the town, builds a castle; 205 houses lost by 1086 [VCH]. 1075: the bishop moves his seat to St John's [enwp St John's].

## 6. Faith and custom

- **Saints.** Werburgh, daughter of King Wulfhere, abbess, died 3 February 700 at Trentham; feast 3 February [enwp Werburgh]. Oswald's cult at the minster, perhaps brought by Æthelflæd [VCH]. John the Baptist at St John's (24 June (memory)). Brigid (1 February (memory)) for the Irish Norse; Olaf after 1030 [VCH].
- **Burial.** St John's and St Werburgh's shared the right to bury [VCH]. Circle-headed crosses mark graves at St John's [VCH].
- **Holy days in law** [DB (memory) (?)]: blood shed between Monday morning and Saturday noon cost 10 shillings; from Saturday noon to Monday morning, and on the twelve days of Christmas, Candlemas, Easter, Ascension, Pentecost, the feasts of Mary and All Saints, 20. A free man working on a feast day paid the bishop 8 shillings.
- **The old gods.** No heathen grave or shrine is known in the town. The Wirral Norse came from Christian Dublin's edge and built St Bridget's [VCH]. Minerva's carved figure across the river is Roman [enwp Deva].
- **Law.** Twelve judges from the king's, earl's and bishop's men, fined for missing court; a Norse-looking custom [VCH].

## 7. Stories and oddities

- **The siege with beer and bees** [FA, 11th-century Irish saga-history, via enwp Ingimundr; details (memory)]: the Norse under hurdles sapped the wall; the townsmen poured boiling ale and water on them; when they covered the hurdles with hides, the English threw down every beehive in the town. The Lady had first sent word to the Irish among the attackers to change sides. Historians call it "clearly over-dramatised".
- **Werburgh's goose**: she restored to life a goose that had been eaten [Goscelin, via enwp Werburgh].
- **Edgar rowed on the Dee** by eight kings, he at the helm, from his hall to St John's: 12th-century writers (John of Worcester, William of Malmesbury). The Chronicle has six kings and no boat [enwp Edgar; ASC 973].
- **"The Ireland-farer"** struck the king's pennies [VCH].
- **One mould, two towns**: a Borre-style brooch from Princess Street is from the same mould as one found in Dublin [VCH].
- **Harold lived**: a later Chester tale says Harold survived Hastings and died a hermit by St John's (memory; Gerald of Wales (?)). Legend, and after our end date.
- **Bangor's monks**: Welsh visitors would remember the 1,200 [enwp Battle].

## 8. What we do not know, and what the writer must not claim

- Who, if anyone, held Chester for Offa in 793; how many lived there; whether the bridge stood; whether any market was held. Do not give 793 a mint, a toll, moneyers, a reeve by name, Norse settlers or St Werburgh's relics.
- Do not state 689 or 875 as fact; say "they say".
- Do not show the rowing of 973 as fact, or name eight kings as certain.
- The Domesday customs describe 1066, not 793 or even 907; I quote them from memory and the wording needs checking.
- No heathen cult site; no named fair; no known ship type or wreck from the town.
- Moneyers' names in section 3 marked (memory) are unchecked.
- Brunanburh (937) at Bromborough in Wirral is probable, not proved [VCH].
- The weir and the mills are Norman; no weir before 1066.
