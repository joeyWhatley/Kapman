# Dommoc (Dunwich): dossier

Port `dommoc`: Dommoc, modern Dunwich, 52.275 N 1.633 E; realm eastanglia, region england_east, kind town, landing beach, size 2, no first or last year [ports_793.json]. Researched 4 Oct 2026. Source keys are in section 9. (?) marks what I could not check; (memory) what I know but did not open.

**Read this first.** Dommoc is a name in Bede and in bishops' lists. That it is Dunwich has been said since the 15th century and is not proven; the other candidate is Walton Castle, the Roman fort by Felixstowe [enwp Dunwich; enwp Dommoc]. The game puts it at Dunwich, and the Dunwich of 793 is under the sea. No building of that year has been dug.

## 1. The place in 793

- **What it is.** The "city Dommoc", where Felix, first bishop of the East Angles, was given his see about 630 and after seventeen years "ended his days there in peace" [B II.15; enwp Felix]. About 672 the diocese was split: Dommoc kept the south, Elmham the north [B note 573; HBC]. In 803 its bishop signs "of the city of Dummuca" [HS 547].
- **Who rules.** Æthelberht, king of the East Angles, until Offa of Mercia has him beheaded in 794 [ASC 792]. No reeve, lord or king's hall of Dommoc is recorded.
- **Who lives there.** The bishop and his clergy: in June 793 probably Ælfhun, consecrated between 789 and 793; if not yet, Heardred [HBC]. Round them, people who ret hemp for rope (section 2) and farm plough and pasture, with oak and hazel woods, by the marsh pollen [S15 28].
- **How big.** Unknown; size 2 is the game's guess. The first count is of 1066 [DB].
- **The lie of the land.** A cliff of sand and gravel, heath behind. North of the town a shallow valley, then an open estuary, where the Dunwich river and the Blyth reached the sea; the harbour was the sheltered water in that mouth [DP location; enwp Dunwich]. Blythburgh lies up the Blyth.
- **The coast has moved.** The medieval town's east edge stood between the modelled shorelines of 1250 and 1300, now seabed [DP coast]. The shore of 793 lay further out: 1 to 2 km is the game's own low-confidence figure [repo water].
- **Coming in.** Not recorded. The shingle spit that choked the harbour did so in the storms of 1287 and 1328 [S15 3], so in 793 the mouth was open. Where boats lay is unknown; the estuary north of the town is likely.

## 2. What stood there

- **The bishop's church.** Felix's seat and first grave; his bones later went to Soham [enwp Felix, from William of Malmesbury]. Bishop Ælfhun was buried here in 797 or 798 [ASC 799]. Dedication and fabric unknown. The one church of 1066 was St Felix's, says the Victoria History [VCH2]; whether on the old site is not known.
- **A school (?).** King Sigeberht "founded a school wherein boys should be taught letters", with masters Felix brought from Kent [B III.18]. Bede does not say where; Dommoc is a later guess [B note 377].
- **A road.** Soil at the bottom of the hollow of Duck Street, cut by the cliff, dates to about 675 (655 to 770 at the outside). It may be older earth washed in [S15 13-14].
- **The town ditch (Pales Dyke).** 10 to 12 m wide, about 4 m deep, bank inside. First heard of in 1173. Its sherds are Romano-British and 11th or 12th century; its lowest silt dates to about 375 BC, perhaps washed in. Its age is open [S15 8-12].
- **The retting pond.** In the marsh by St James Street, a pond whose mud is up to nine-tenths hemp pollen, with hemp seeds: stalks were rotted here for rope and sail fibre, from about the 5th century to the 11th. The sea broke in after about 1100. Only the third such pit known in Britain [S15 24-28].
- **Roman leavings.** In the cliff top in 1858: Roman and "perhaps Saxon" sherds, animal bone, a rubbish pit of ash with oyster, cockle and whelk shells, bronze keys [VCH1 Roman]. A Roman shore fort is often supposed; divers have found no proof [DP history].
- **Blythburgh.** King Anna, killed by Penda in 653 or 654, lay buried there, his tomb still honoured in the 12th century [enwp Anna, from the Liber Eliensis]. Dug in 2008: mid-7th-century burials and Ipswich ware. Found earlier: a whalebone writing tablet with runes, British Museum [enwp Blythburgh].
- **If Dommoc was Walton:** a Roman stone fort of about six acres, walls nine feet thick, gone over the cliff by 1754 [enwp Walton; enwp Dommoc].

## 3. Who could be met

Bishops of Dommoc [dates HBC]:

- **Heardred**, to 789×793. **Ælfhun**, 789×793 to 797/798.
- **Tidfrith** (Tidferth), 798 to 816×824. His written oath to Archbishop Æthelheard survives [HS 511]. He attests at Tamworth in 799, Chelsea about 801, Clofesho in 803, Chelsea in 816 [HS 547, 579, notes to 799]. Alcuin wrote to him and Alhheard of Elmham between 798 and 804 [HS 551-552].
- **Wærmund**, to 824/825. **Wilred**, 825 to 845×870.
- **Æthelwald**, from 845×870, the last known. His oath to Archbishop Ceolnoth and his seal survive [HS 659-660; VCH1 seal].

The bishop's men at Clofesho, 803: abbots **Wulfheard** and **Lull**; priests **Ceolhelm, Cynulf, Tilberht, Eadberht** [HS 547]. Lull had praised both East Anglian bishops to Alcuin in person [HS 552]. Their houses are not named.

Kings and lords over the place (more in section 5):

- **Æthelberht**; **Offa**; **Eadwald**, about 796 to 798, known only from some twelve coins; **Coenwulf** of Mercia; **Æthelstan**, about 827 to 840; **Edmund**, killed 869 [enwp Eadwald; enwp Æthelstan].
- **Guthrum**, baptised Æthelstan; **Eohric**; **Ulfcytel**, who led the East Angles in 1004 and 1010; **Thorkell**, earl from 1017 [ASC].
- Bishops over Suffolk after the see lapsed: **Theodred** of London (died 951×953); from about 955 the bishops of Elmham, among them **Stigand** (1043) and **Æthelmær** (1047-1070) [enwp Theodred; enwp Elmham].
- **Edric of Laxfield**, lord of Dunwich in 1066 [DB]. **Lul**, moneyer for Æthelberht, Offa and Coenwulf, but not here [enwp Æthelberht].

Ordinary people the evidence shows: priests; hemp growers and rope-makers; ploughmen and herdsmen; by 1066 burgesses, cottagers and herring fishers [S15; DB].

## 4. What was traded

No cargo, toll or market of Dommoc is recorded before 1066.

- **Made here:** hemp fibre for rope and sails, before 793 and until the 11th century [S15 26-27].
- **Herring.** By 1086 the town gave 60,000 herrings a year "by way of gift", and Gilbert Blund's eighty men 8,000 more. Beccles gave 60,000, Southwold 25,000 to the abbot of Bury [DB; VCH1 fishery]. The shoals come August to October [repo seasons]. For 793 the fishery is likely, not proven.
- **In the region:** Ipswich ware, found at Blythburgh [enwp Blythburgh]. A bishop's church wants wine, oil, incense and books [repo seasons].
- **Coin.** East Anglian pennies; the mint is not named on them (Ipswich (?)). None struck here: in 1066 there was "no exchanger (cambitor) therein", but one at Blythburgh [DB]. Guthrum struck his own [enwp East Anglia]. Fines in 1086 are reckoned in ores, a Danish unit (memory) [DB].
- **Law, as written in 1086.** Two or three men must go to the Hundred court when warned, or forfeit 2 ores. A thief caught at Dunwich is tried there and punished in body at Blythburgh; his goods go to Dunwich's lord [DB].
- **Fair.** The first on record, three days at St Lawrence (10 August (memory)), was given to Eye priory after 1066 [VCH2].
- **Ships.** None found; the wreck off the old harbour is later than 1750 [DP news].

## 5. The years 793 to 1066

- 794, 20 May by his cult: "Offa, King of Mercia, commanded that King Ethelbert should be beheaded" [ASC 792; enwp Æthelberht].
- 796: Offa dies; Eadwald king for a year or two [repo chronicle; enwp Eadwald].
- 797 or 798: "Bishop Alfun died at Sudbury, and was buried at Dunwich. After him Tidfrith was elected to the see" [ASC 799; HS 511 note gives 797, from manuscript F].
- 798: Tidfrith's oath to Canterbury [HS 511].
- 803: Clofesho; Tidfrith signs with six of his clergy [HS 547].
- 816, 27 July: Tidfrith and Sibba of Elmham at Chelsea, King Coenwulf present [HS 579].
- 825: Wilred signs at Clofesho first as bishop elect, then as bishop [HS, notes to 825].
- About 825: the East Angles ask Ecgberht of Wessex for "peace and protection against the terror of the Mercians" and kill Beornwulf of Mercia; Ludeca falls after him [ASC 823, 825].
- 841: "in Lindsey, East-Anglia, and Kent, were many men slain by the army" [ASC 838].
- 865: the great army winters in East Anglia, "where they were soon horsed" [ASC 866].
- 869, 20 November: Edmund killed; the Danes "destroyed all the monasteries to which they came" [ASC 870; repo chronicle]. No bishop of Dommoc is heard of again.
- 880: Guthrum's army "settled, and divided the land" [ASC 880, 890].
- 890: Guthrum dies [ASC 890]. 902: Eohric killed [enwp East Anglia].
- 917: "all the army in East-Anglia swore union" with King Edward [ASC 921].
- Before 953: Theodred's will names a bishopric at Hoxne. About 955: one bishop at Elmham for all East Anglia [enwp Theodred; enwp Elmham].
- 991: Ipswich plundered. 1004: Swein burns Norwich. 1010: the army lands at Ipswich and burns East Anglia for three months. 1017: Cnut gives East Anglia to Thorkell [ASC].
- 1066: Edric of Laxfield's manor: two ploughlands, one church, 12 cottagers, 120 burgesses, rendering 10 pounds. Ely abbey has 80 more burgesses, on 14 acres (counted 1086) [DB].
- After the game: by 1086 "2 carucates of land, now 1. The sea carried away the other"; 236 burgesses, 178 poor men, three churches, 50 pounds and the herrings [DB].

## 6. Faith and custom

- **What a bishop swore.** The Trinity; Christ born, dead, risen and coming to judge the world by fire; obedience to Canterbury all his days; signed with his own hand and the cross [HS 511, my paraphrase].
- **Alcuin to the two bishops.** Preach to everyone; your mouth should be the trumpet of God; a priest's rank is no worldly game; have my name remembered in your churches [HS 551-552, my paraphrase].
- **Saints.** Felix, 8 March [enwp Felix]. Æthelberht the king, 20 May; Falkenham, Hessett, Herringswell and Tannington churches in Suffolk bear his name [enwp Æthelberht]. Edmund, 20 November [repo chronicle].
- **Old gods.** Nothing heathen is found at Dunwich. From 869 the lords are heathen Danes; Guthrum was baptised in 878 [repo chronicle].

## 7. Stories and oddities

- **The seal.** Æthelwald's bronze seal is a cone with two rows of small arches, a beast's head in each with garnet eyes, and round a star the words SIG EDILVVALDI EP. A labourer dug it up 200 yards from Eye priory. A child was given it as a toy and threw it on the fire; the mother pulled it out. One garnet is left. British Museum, 1822 [VCH1 seal].
- **The Red Book of Eye.** Eye priory kept a book said to be Felix's own, from its cell at Dunwich. In 1536 it was valued at twenty pence: "garnysshed with a lytell sylver on the one side, the residewe lytell worth". It is lost [VCH2].
- **The bones in the fog.** Monks of Ramsey took Felix's body from Soham and got clear of the monks of Ely in a Fenland fog [enwp Felix]. The year is not given (?).
- **The other claim.** Rochester's monks, before 1251: "The blessed Felix founded the church which is now called Felixstowe and sat in it seventeen years" [enwp Dommoc].
- **The king's penny.** Four pennies of Æthelberht are known. One, struck by Lul, shows the she-wolf and twins; one found in Sussex in 2014 sold for 78,000 pounds [enwp Æthelberht].
- **Hemp.** The report says of the pond: "This was the non-toxic variety!" [S15 26].

## 8. What we do not know, and what the writer must not claim

- That Dommoc is Dunwich. Call the place Dommoc and leave Walton's claim alive.
- A Roman fort, a king's hall, a "capital of a Saxon Kingdom" (the museum's phrase), a wall or ditch standing in 793.
- A mint, market, fair, toll or reeve before 1066; the church's dedication in 793; that Felix's bones were still in it; that the school stood here.
- That the Danes burned Dommoc. No text says so: the bishops' list simply stops.
- That herring was sold here in 793 (the renders are of 1086), or any head-count before 1066.
- The harbour's shape, the bar, the tide and the shoreline of 793: all inference.
- Who was bishop on 21 June 793: Ælfhun probably, Heardred possibly.
- The year Ælfhun died: the chronicle id `799-aelfhun` follows Ingram's print; 797 or 798 is better.
- Many churches, friars, Templars, 3,000 people, bells heard under the sea: all after 1066 [enwp Dunwich]. The Dunwich of 1066 is a herring town, not a bishop's seat.
