# Streonæshalch (Whitby): dossier

Port `streonaeshalch`: Streonæshalch, modern Whitby, 54.489 N 0.608 W; realm northumbria, region northumbria, kind monastery, landing beach, size 2 [ports]. Researched 6 Oct 2026. Source keys are spelled out in section 9. (?) marks what I could not check; (memory) is known to me but was not opened. Web searching was shut for this task (the session's budget was spent), and the dig reports could not be opened, so most of the archaeology in section 2 rests on memory and is marked.

**Read this first.** This is Hild's double house of monks and nuns, 113 years after her death. Its rich record (Bede) stops about 731. For the game's years there is not one sure dated event at this place before 1066: no abbess is named after 714, no raid is recorded by a writer of the time, and the end "in 867" is a 12th-century story. The fixed points are what the ground shows (a busy house into the 9th century, then nothing), the Norse name Whitby, Earl Siward's rich manor before 1066, and the forty roofless chapels Reinfrid found about 1078.

## 1. The place in 793

- **What it is.** The monastery of St Peter [VCH3] on the East Cliff, a headland above the mouth of the Esk. Founded about 657 by King Oswiu in thanks for beating Penda, with Hild as first abbess, for men and women both [enwp Whitby; EH]. English Heritage speaks of an "Anglian town and monastery": a settlement, not only a cloister [EH].
- **The name.** Written Streanæshalc, Streneshalc, Streoneshalc in the 7th and 8th centuries [VCH2]. "Streon's nook of land" [enwp Whitby]; Bede's own gloss, "bay of the lighthouse", is from memory (?). That the place is Whitby is "generally accepted, but not absolutely certain"; Strensall near York is the other candidate [enwp Synod].
- **Who rules.** King Æthelred I until April 796, then Eardwulf [repo realm]. The house is in the diocese of York (memory). The abbess of 793 is not known (section 3).
- **How big.** No figure. About 500 metres of cliff have gone into the sea since then, so the settlement "was much more extensive than had been previously thought" [EH research]. Size 2 is fair.
- **Coming in.** A narrow gap between cliffs, with flat rock scars on either side that cover at half tide. Spring highs 5.8 m, a range near 4.7 to 5 m; the harbour dries. The tide runs about 3 km up to Ruswarp. The Esk is a peat-brown moorland river, quick to flood: mean flow 4.8 m3/s, floods to 350 [repo water; enwp Esk]. These are modern figures carried back. The East Cliff stands 57 m, of shale, sandstone and clay; the West Cliff is lower, boulder clay on sandstone, and slips [enwp Whitby]. The monastery on the East Cliff is the seamark (my inference: nothing says what could be seen from the sea).
- **Where boats lie.** Not recorded. On the drying sand inside the mouth, under the East Cliff (my inference from the ground). No pier, quay or bar is recorded for these years.

## 2. What stood there

- **The church of St Peter**, where kings were buried (section 6). Not found: the main monastic buildings have "not yet been excavated" [EH research]. It may lie under the later abbey church (memory) (?).
- **Buildings north of the later church**, dug in the 1920s under Sir Charles Peers: stone footings "originally interpreted as single-cell structures though more likely parts of more substantial ranges" [EH research]. The dig was published by Peers and Radford in 1943 (memory).
- **What the 1920s dig found** (memory, not opened): writing styli, book mounts, dress pins, strap ends, tweezers, keys, spindle whorls and loom weights, fittings of hanging bowls, glass, pottery from abroad, and a lead seal of a papal archdeacon Boniface (?). EH says only "large quantities of pottery, household goods and fine metal objects" [EH].
- **A bone comb cut with runes**: "my God, almighty God, help Cy..." (the name breaks off), 7th century; and a **jet spindle whorl** with the runes "ueu" [enwp runes].
- **A jet gaming piece**, Anglian [EH research].
- **Coins**: Northumbrian stycas were found in numbers, the series stopping about the 850s (memory) (?).
- **Carved and inscribed stones**: the Corpus of Anglo-Saxon Stone Sculpture lists 64 pieces from Whitby, 56 from the abbey [Corpus]. Plain crosses and name stones (memory); stones "believed to record" Abbess Ælfflæd's death were found in the 1920s [enwp Ælfflæd].
- **The cliff-edge settlement**: "timber buildings and domestic hearths, pits and wells of the Anglian period" [EH research].
- **The graveyard**: "part of a large Anglian cemetery", found in 2000 south of the church under the new car park [EH research]. The count of graves I could not check; over a thousand is my memory (?).
- **Forty oratories.** About 1078 the site "comprised about forty roofless and ruined monasteria vel oratoria", which the Victoria History likens to Irish houses of many small chapels and cells [VCH3].
- **Hackness**, the daughter house Hild founded in her last year, fourteen miles off [enwp Hild]. Its church keeps pieces of a cross of the late 8th or early 9th century with a Latin prayer for Æthelburh and an unread inscription, apparently runic [enwp Hackness]. It was standing and in use in the game's first years.
- **Not known here:** a mint, a market place, a wall, a well with a name, a toll house.

## 3. Who could be met

**Between 793 and 1066 no person is recorded at this place by name.** Those tied to it:

- **Siward**, earl at York from 1033 and of all Northumbria 1041 to 1055, held Whitby and its outlier Sneaton, 15 carucates [VCH2; repo realm]. Nothing says he ever came.
- **Tostig** (earl 1055 to 1065) and **Morcar** (1065 to 1068) [repo realm]: earls over it; no tie recorded.
- **Ingwar and Ubba**, leaders of the great army, blamed for the destruction of 867 to 870 [VCH2]: late tradition only [repo realm].
- **King Edmund** (939 to 946), said in one tradition to have carried Hild's bones to Glastonbury [enwp Hild].
- After 1066: **Reinfrid**, once a soldier of King William, monk of Evesham, who refounded the house about 1078; **William de Percy**, who gave him "the ruined monastery of St. Peter, with 2 carucates"; **Serlo de Percy**, prior after him; **Stephen**, who left for Lastingham and York [VCH3].

**The dead a visitor would be told of** (all before 793): Hild (died 17 November 680, aged 66) [Bede IV.23]; Eanflæd, Oswiu's widow, and their daughter Ælfflæd (654 to 714), abbesses after her, Ælfflæd known for surgery and care of the sick [enwp Ælfflæd]; Bishop Trumwine, who fled here in 685 and is buried in St Peter's [enwp Trumwine]; Cædmon the cowherd (section 7); the five bishops trained here: Bosa, Ætla (Giles prints "Hedda"), Oftfor, John of Beverley and Wilfrid II [Bede IV.23].

**Ordinary people the evidence shows:** nuns and monks; writers (styli, a Life of Pope Gregory written here about 713 "by a monk or, possibly, a nun" [enwp Gregory]); spinners and weavers (whorls, loom weights); a comb owner named Cy-; a cowherd and a reeve (Cædmon's story, section 7); players of board games; jet workers; lay families on the cliff edge (hearths, pits, wells) [EH research; enwp runes].

## 4. What was traded

- **Jet** is the one sure local good. Bede: "It has much and excellent jet, which is black and sparkling, glittering at the fire, and when heated, drives away serpents; being warmed with rubbing, it holds fast whatever is applied to it, like amber" [Bede I.1]. It was gathered by "beachcombing rather than quarrying"; the Romans carried it raw to York to be worked [enwp jet]. It burns like coal with a coal smell [enwp jet]. Northmen made rings and small beasts of jet; Whitby is the likely source, not proven [enwp jet]. York's jet workers flourish in the 10th century [repo goods].
- **In:** pottery from the south and abroad (?), glass, bronze, silver, wine for the Mass (memory; the finds, not texts).
- **Out, by inference only:** cloth from the house's looms, wool and hides [repo seasons]. Nothing records a cargo.
- **Coins:** stycas of York, small and base, which "do not travel" south of the Humber [repo realm]. No mint here.
- **Tolls, laws, fairs, ships:** nothing recorded before 1066. From 876 the law south of the Tees is the settlers' custom, reckoned in marks and ores [repo realm]. By 1066 the manor was assessed at the "large sum of £112" [VCH2], so there was wealth here late; what made it is not said.
- **Fish:** salmon and sea trout run the Esk, and it is the only Yorkshire river with pearl mussels [enwp Esk] (modern facts).

## 5. The years 793 to 1066

- **793, 8 June.** Lindisfarne plundered, up this same coast [ports].
- **794.** Raid on the Tyne; the raiders' leader killed and ships wrecked [repo realm] (see the gyrwe dossier).
- **Late 8th or early 9th century.** The Hackness cross is carved [enwp Hackness].
- **844.** King Rædwulf killed fighting heathen raiders, the first on this coast for forty years [repo realm].
- **About 850s (?).** The coin finds on the headland stop (memory).
- **866, 1 November; 867, 21 March.** The great army takes York; Kings Osberht and Ælla are killed, and the army harries north to the Tyne [repo realm; ASC].
- **867 to 870.** "Streoneshalch was laid waste by Danes in successive inroads under Ingwar and Ubba", and "was said to have remained desolate for more than 200 years" [VCH2]. No writer of the time says so [repo realm]. EH: abandoned "at some point in the 9th century. We do not know exactly when or how" [EH].
- **876.** "Healfden divided the land of the Northumbrians; so that they became afterwards their harrowers and plowers" [ASC]. The Norse names come after this: Hvítabýr, Whitby [enwp Whitby], and Prestebi, "the priests' farm", an 11th-century form [VCH2].
- **939 to 946 (?).** King Edmund is said to take Hild's relics to Glastonbury; another tradition says Gloucester [enwp Hild].
- **1033 to 1055.** Earl Siward holds Whitby with Sneaton [VCH2; repo realm].
- **1066, September.** Harald of Norway and Tostig burn Scarborough, down the coast, on their way to the Humber [repo realm]. Whitby is not named.
- **By 1066.** "The headland seems to have been abandoned, although there was a substantial town called Whitby down by the harbour" [EH].
- **1069 to 1070.** King William wastes the north [repo realm]; in 1086 Whitby is "all waste" but the abbot of York's lands [VCH2].
- **About 1078.** Reinfrid begins again among the ruins [VCH3].

## 6. Faith and custom

- **Dedication:** St Peter [VCH3; enwp Trumwine].
- **Saints and days:** Hild, 17 November [Bede IV.23; repo saints]; Ælfflæd, 8 February [enwp Ælfflæd]; Cædmon, 11 February [enwp Cædmon]; King Edwin, 12 October [enwp Edwin].
- **Royal graves:** Edwin's body is here (his head at York) [enwp Edwin; VCH2]; Oswiu, died 15 February 670; Eanflæd and Ælfflæd [enwp Oswiu].
- **The Easter rule.** In 664 King Oswiu heard Colman for Iona and Wilfrid for Rome here, with Cedd interpreting, and chose Peter, who holds the keys [enwp Synod]. By 793 everyone keeps the Roman Easter [repo saints].
- **The rule of the house** under Hild: Bede says she put it "under the same regular discipline" as her earlier house [Bede IV.23]; that goods were held in common is from memory.
- **Burial:** the large cemetery south of the church [EH research]; whether lay folk lay there is not checked.
- **After 876:** heathen settlers on the land [ASC]; Prestebi suggests priests living here in Danish times [VCH2]. Nothing more is known of worship here until 1078.

## 7. Stories and oddities

- **Cædmon.** A cowherd of the house who left the feast when the harp came round, slept among the cattle, and was told in a dream to sing of the making of the world. The reeve took him to Hild; he became a brother [enwp Cædmon; Bede IV.24 (memory)]. His hymn begins "nu scylun hergan hefaenricaes uard": now we must honour heaven's guardian [enwp Cædmon].
- **The king's joke.** Oswiu ended the Easter debate by asking who held the keys of heaven, and said he would not cross the doorkeeper [enwp Synod; Bede III.25 (memory)].
- **Begu at Hackness** saw Hild's soul carried up by angels on the night she died [enwp Hild].
- **Snakestones.** Hild is said to have turned the snakes to stone and thrown them from the cliff: the coiled ammonites on the shore. Sea birds are said to dip their wings to her [enwp Whitby; enwp Hild]. When these tales began is not known; do not date them to 793.
- **Jet and serpents:** Bede's jet "drives away serpents" [Bede I.1], which sits oddly well with the snake tale.
- **Edwin's bones** were found at Hatfield and brought here [VCH2]; the story of the priest shown the place in a dream is in the Whitby Life of Gregory (memory).
- **Angles and angels.** The Life of Gregory written here about 713 [enwp Gregory] is the oldest telling of the pun (memory).
- **The comb's prayer** breaks off in the middle of its owner's name [enwp runes].

## 8. What we do not know, and what the writer must not claim

- Any abbess, abbot, priest or reeve by name after 714. Invent ordinary people only.
- That Northmen raided Whitby in 793, or in any named year. The 867 story is late; say "it is said".
- That the house was a nunnery only: it was a double house. Whether it still was in 793 is not known.
- A market, mint, toll, quay, pier, beacon or lighthouse. None is recorded.
- That the ruined abbey seen today, St Mary's church (about 1110) or the 199 steps were there [enwp StMary]. Cædmon's cross is of 1898 [enwp Cædmon].
- That jet was mined, or worked here in a known workshop. It was picked up.
- The name Whitby before the Danish settlement. Use Streonæshalch to about 870, and Whitby (Hvítabýr) or Prestebi after.
- The numbers and finds marked (memory) or (?) in section 2, until the dig reports are opened.
- Where Hild's bones were at any date after 870.
