# The ships of 700 to 1100

What a merchant of the North Sea, the Channel, the Baltic and their rivers could own, hire or meet between 793 and
1066: the finds, the texts, the numbers, and the order in which a growing house would really have moved through them.
Written for the game's "different ships". It ends with what `Assets/Kapman/Game/Data/ship_classes_793.json` lacks or
has wrong.

**How to read the marks.**

- No mark: read this session in the source named by the key in square brackets (the keys are in section 13).
- **(D)**: derived by me from the numbers named beside it. A design number, not evidence.
- **(?)**: from memory or from a second-hand summary; not opened this session. Check before it is shown to a player.
- **LATER** / **EARLIER**: the evidence is outside 793-1066 and stands in for something that is not found.
- Lengths in metres, cargo in tonnes, speeds in knots (1 kn = 1.852 km/h), money in pennies (d).

---

## 1. The short answer

1. **Three ways of building shared the water.** The Norse keeled clinker ship, its planks riveted with iron and
   caulked with wool. The Low Countries' river craft, flat-bottomed barges and logboats widened and raised with planks,
   pegged with wood and caulked with moss. East of the Elbe, Slav clinker boats pegged with treenails. A Frisian
   merchant used the first at sea and the second on the Rhine [BJM].
2. **Dorestad's own sea-going find is Norse-built.** One hull fragment, 120 by 70 cm, wood felled after about 739,
   riveted clinker with wool caulking, apparently sunk in the northern harbour [MASS-Dor] [BJM]. So in 793 the Dorestad
   merchant's sea ship is a riveted keel ship. The cog and the hulk are, at that date, shapes on coins and no more.
3. **Cargo grew in dated steps; crews did not.** About 1 t in a six-oar boat (any year). 6 to 7 t in the Graveney
   boat, built about 895. About 20 t in the Äskekärr ship, built 900 to 920, and 24 t in Skuldelev 1, about 1030.
   40 to 60 t in Hedeby 3, about 1025. All of them sailed with five to eight men. That is the ladder (section 10).
4. **The ship built only to carry cargo under sail is not found before about 900** (Äskekärr). Before that the
   sea-going ship is also a rowing ship and carries more men for less cargo.
5. **Speed.** Replicas hold 4 to 6 kn in a fresh wind on or abaft the beam, make 2 kn at best toward the wind, and row
   at 2 to 4 kn. A loaded trader can hardly be rowed at all (the Skuldelev 1 replica: 1.5 kn at most) [VSM-Trials]
   [VSM-Ottar].
6. **Rivers.** Upstream is hauling at 10 to 12 km a day. At sea with a fair wind a ship makes 225 km in a day and a
   night. From the Rhine mouth, York and London (about 280 km by sea) are nearer in time than Dorestad (about 70 km
   upstream) [BJM, after Dijkstra 2011].
7. **Cost.** No price for a merchant ship survives from these centuries that I could find (?). What survives is
   tolls by size of ship, wills that leave ships, a ship levy, and what crews were paid. The prices in section 8 are
   derived from the hours the replicas took.
8. **A ship's life.** A sea-going clinker ship lasted 20 to 30 years with repairs [EXARC]; one stayed in use a
   hundred [sv-Ask]. River barges at Tiel lasted about ten [BJM].
9. **Four corrections to the project's notes** (details in section 11):
   - "Dorestat 1" is not Dorestad's find. Dorestat-1 is a volunteer-built replica of **Skuldelev 3** (Denmark, about
     1040) that has sailed from Wijk bij Duurstede since May 2021 [rodi]. Its numbers are Skuldelev 3's. The find is
     the fragment in point 2, and nobody knows that ship's length.
   - The Utrecht type **is** dated to the eighth century: Vleuten 1, felled after about 734, about 15 by 1.10 m [BJM].
   - The Zwammerdam barges and the Bruges boat are **Roman** (97 to 205, and "of the Roman period") [nl-Zwam]
     [Marsden]. They are ancestors, not ships of the game's years.
   - Upstream hauling is 10 to 12 km a day in the Dutch study, not the 18 in the table.

Three things follow for the game. Ship timber before about 850 comes from the German Rhineland, and between 950 and
1050 from the Ardennes, by way of the Maas: many of the ships were built upstream, outside the delta [BJM]. Iron
rivets of the Norse kind lie in the Frisian terps (Wijnaldum, Oosterbeintum, Hallum, Beetgum) from 400 to 800, before
any Viking: riveted clinker boats were at home in Frisia [BJM]. And there is a gap: no wreck at all between about 300
and 600.

The Slav boat is the Norse boat's cousin: clinker, double-ended, a keel, a mast step, a side rudder. It differs in
its fastenings (wooden pegs, not iron) and its caulking (moss, not wool) (?). The Dutch inventory calls it "a Slav
shipbuilding tradition closely related to the Scandinavian" [BJM].

### 2.6 Named in the brief, but not of these years

- **Zwammerdam** (on the old Rhine): six vessels of 97 to 205: three logboats used as fishing boats, and three
  flat-bottomed transports of 20 to 34 m that carried tile from Nijmegen and slate from the Eifel [nl-Zwam]. The
  early medieval barges are built the same way (a flat bottom of planks laid edge to edge, L-shaped chine planks, the
  sides set on them), but the relationship "is assumed, not shown", and nearly four centuries lie between the last
  Roman barge and Utrecht 6 [BJM].
- **The Bruges boat** (found 1899): of the Roman period [Marsden]. Older books made her a medieval forerunner of the
  cog (?). She is not evidence for 793 to 1066.
- **Hide boats**: no early medieval hull has ever been found. They are known from texts only (section 3).
- **Logboats in Britain**: 28 are reported for the early Middle Ages and none survives in an archaeological
  context [BJM, citing Cooper 2013].

## 4. The kinds of vessel, one by one

Each card gives what the kind was called, the evidence, its years and waters, its size, its crew and cargo, its sail
and oars, and where it stands in the game's table (`class` = an id in `ship_classes_793.json`; NEW = not there yet).
Speeds by point of sail are gathered in section 5, building and cost in sections 7 and 8.

### A. Boats

#### 4.1 The logboat

- **Called then:** Latin monoxylus, linter (?); Greek monoxyla for the Slav ones [WP-Dugout].
- **Evidence:** Oegstgeest about 612; 28 reported from early medieval Britain, none kept [BJM]; the Slavs sell them
  to the Rus about 950 [WP-Dugout]; the game's table has the English and Irish finds [JSON].
- **Years and where:** always, everywhere there is a tree and still water. In the delta they last until the twelfth
  or early thirteenth century, then vanish, perhaps because the great trees ran out [BJM].
- **Size:** 3 to 7 m by 0.6 to 0.8; draught a hand's breadth. One or two men, a few hundredweight.
- **Sail and oars:** paddle or pole. No sail.
- **Waters:** river, lake, creek, the flats at slack water. Never the sea.
- **In the game:** `logboat`. A fisherman's, a ferryman's, a fowler's. The player meets them; nobody buys one.

#### 4.2 The four-oar boat (a ship's boat)

- **Called then:** Old Norse bátr; the towed boat of a ship is an eptirbátr; a four-oared boat is a færingr (the
  Norse names are written down only later) (?). Old English bat.
- **Evidence:** the smallest of the three boats in the Gokstad mound, about 895: 6.5 x 1.38 m, draught 0.20, four
  oars, two or three men, "not clear whether the boat carried a sail"; the replicas are put at 4 kn at most
  [VSM-Joanna] [VSM-Karl]. Rivets in the Frisian terps from 400 to 800 [BJM].
- **Waters:** harbour, river, a fjord on a quiet day. Carried or towed by a ship and used to land where she cannot.
- **In the game:** inside `norse_small_boat`. Worth having as a **fitting** for a big ship (section 7.4): a knarr or
  the great cargo ship cannot be beached laden, and lands her goods by boat.

#### 4.3 The six-oar working boat (the player's boat)

- **Called then:** bátr; a six-oared boat is a sexæringr (later word) (?); Old English bat; Latin navicula.
- **Evidence:** the Gislinge boat, about 1130: 7.7 x 1.64 x 0.25 m, sail 10 m2, six oars, two to four men; the
  replica is put at 6 kn under sail and 5 kn under oars at the most [VSM-Lang]. She was a working boat on the
  Lammefjord, used about fifty years for fishing and for carrying goods and people [JSON]. For 793 the same kind of
  boat is vouched for by the Kvalsund boat (9.5 m, 780 to 800) [WP-Kval] and the terp rivets [BJM].
- **Crew and cargo:** four men and about 0.6 to 0.7 t beside them. The museum's "about one tonne" is "four men and
  ten sheep" [JSON], so it counts the men. The game's own files give both 660 kg (BRIEF.md) and 900 kg (the table):
  one of the two must go.
- **Waters:** river, flats, creek and coast. She draws 25 cm and can be dragged over a bar by her crew. The open sea
  only in settled weather: 34 cm of freeboard [JSON].
- **In the game:** `gislinge`. The right first boat. A merchant with only this is a pedlar by water: wine by the
  cask, querns by the pair.

#### 4.4 Skiff, punt, ferry and raft

No new evidence beyond the game's table (`skiff_punt`, `ferry_barge`, `raft`): the Frankish law on stolen boats, the
toll capitularies, Bede's timber rafts on the Tyne [JSON]. One thing to add: the London toll of about 1000 puts "a
small boat" at a halfpenny and "a larger one with a sail" at a penny, so the line between a boat and a small ship was
drawn at the sail [JSON].

#### 4.5 The hide boat

- **Called then:** Latin curuca; Old Irish curach [JSON].
- **Evidence:** texts only: Adomnán, Dicuil, the Voyage of Brendan (a mast amidships, a sail, hides cured with oak
  bark) [JSON] [WP-Cur]. No hull. LATER: Tim Severin's Brendan (1976-77): 11 m, two masts, a frame of ash and oak
  lashed with three km of leather thong under 49 ox hides; four men; 7,200 km from Kerry to Newfoundland by the
  Hebrides, the Faroes and Iceland in two summers [WP-Sev]. She would not work to windward (?).
- **Waters:** the Irish Sea, the Hebrides, the ocean in summer. Light enough to carry up a beach.
- **In the game:** `currach`. Met west of Britain; not sold to a Frisian.

### B. River craft

#### 4.6 The Utrecht type: a logboat widened, raised and lengthened

- **Called then:** not known. It is not the "hulk" by any proof: "whether the Utrecht type is a proto hulc remains
  uncertain" [MASS-Utr].
- **How she is made:** one great oak is hollowed thin enough to bend, with rows of small gauge holes drilled to check
  the thickness and plugged afterwards. The shell is spread and held by floor timbers, lengthened with end plates
  that give her a banana profile, and raised with one or more clinker planks on wooden pegs. The seams are caulked
  with moss under laths, held by iron clamps or wooden wedges. Some have a mast step in a floor timber; the question
  is whether the mast carried a sail or a tow line [BJM].
- **Years:** Vleuten 1 after about 734 (15 x 1.10 m); Zeewolde about 900; Utrecht 2 (12.8 x 3 m) and Utrecht 1
  (17.45 x 3.84 m, after about 1019) in the eleventh century; the last in the twelfth or early thirteenth [BJM].
  Utrecht 1 is "about the largest a logboat could be made".
- **Where:** the Rhine, the Vecht, the Almere; fragments at Antwerp and in London [BJM]. The forerunner on the Weser
  is the Eke boat, 8th century, 10.25 x 1.4 to 1.5 m [JSON].
- **Crew and cargo:** three or four men aboard. Vleuten 1's size gives 2 to 3 t (D: 15 x 1.1 x 0.3 m of draught).
  Utrecht 1 carried about 13 t on 0.7 m [nl-Utr]; 10 to 15 t [MASS-Utr].
- **Sail and oars:** hauled upstream on a line from a short mast well forward; poled and sculled; she drifts down.
  A sail before the wind is possible and not proved.
- **Waters:** river and lake. The inventory judges the type "sturdier" than the barges and "able to manage a short
  sea crossing"; one scholar holds it was preferred because the log bottom "was much stronger than a planked bottom
  and much more suitable for crossing rough waters" [BJM, citing Van de Moortel 2009].
- **In the game:** `logboat_expanded` (10 m, 1.7 t) is the eighth-century size. NEW: the full-size ship of 950 to
  1100 (17 m, 13 t). This is the Rhine merchant's own freighter: Dorestad to Cologne and back, wine down, querns
  down, cloth and fish up.

#### 4.7 The river barge

- **Called then:** Latin navis in the toll charters [JSON]. "Aak" and "Prahm" are modern words for it.
- **How she is made:** a near-rectangular plan; a flat bottom of planks laid edge to edge; L-shaped chine planks; the
  sides set on them; pairs of floor timbers and knees; the ends raked up a little so she can land on a sloping bank
  with no quay. Moss in the seams. Often no iron at all (Vleuten 2) [BJM].
- **Years and sizes:** small in the seventh century (Utrecht 6, about 680, 7 m and more by 2.1). About 15 to 20 m in
  Charlemagne's time (Krefeld-Gellep 15.2 m and 7 t; Kalkar about 802 with its stone; Cothen after 826, about
  20 x 2.5 to 4 x 1.25 m). 20 to 23 m about 1000 (Vleuten 2, 959, about 20 x 3.5; Utrecht 3, after 1004,
  23 x 3.3 to 4) [BJM] [JSON].
- **Crew and cargo:** two or three aboard and a hauling gang on the bank. 7 t in a 15 m barge [JSON]; 15 to 25 t in a
  20 to 23 m one (D: 20 x 3.5 x 0.6 m of draught, a box four-fifths full, less 6 to 8 t of hull).
- **Waters:** "great loads on slow rivers. The ships are not seaworthy" [BJM].
- **Life:** short. Tiel I under ten years, Tiel II about ten, Deventer II "made quickly" of sapwood. Worn-out barges
  end as quay fronts and well linings [BJM].
- **In the game:** `river_barge` is the Carolingian one. NEW: the large barge from about 826. Bulk on the Rhine:
  stone, grain, wine in tuns, timber. Cheap, slow, and a river toll at every bridge.

#### 4.8 The river ship with cabins

`river_estate_ship`. The one text is Rimbert: a boat with two cabins, given by the bishop of Cologne in 826 for the
voyage down the Rhine [JSON]. No hull. For a merchant it is a thing to hire a place in, or to carry a bishop in for a
favour.

### C. Sea traders of the west

#### 4.9 The small coaster, 10 to 12 m

- **Called then:** scip, navis; a Norse one a byrðingr (later word): the museum calls the rebuilt Skuldelev 6 a
  "byrding" [VSM-Kraka].
- **Evidence:** toll charters for the ships of minsters and abbeys [JSON]; Kvalsund boat 9.5 m [WP-Kval]; Truso and
  Reric boats of 9 to 15 m [JSON]; LATER Skuldelev 6, about 1030: 11.2 x 2.5 x 0.5 m, built as a 14-oared fishing
  boat and rebuilt as a carrier with fewer oars and five or six men [VSM-S6] [VSM-Kraka].
- **Crew and cargo:** four to six men; 1.5 to 2.5 t.
- **Sail and oars:** sail about 25 m2. The replica makes 10 kn at the most; under four oars 2.5 kn, under fourteen
  5 kn [VSM-Kraka]. She came through a stiff gale to Anholt "extremely seaworthy", though she "rolls somewhat in the
  high waves" [VSM-Trips].
- **Waters:** coast, estuary, the Wadden, the Baltic in summer; she lands on a beach.
- **In the game:** `coaster_minster_ship`; add Danish and Norse to its peoples, or a NEW Norse row. The first step up
  from the six-oar boat.

#### 4.10 The keeled clinker sea trader, 13 to 15 m

- **Called then:** Old English ceol (keel) and scip; Latin navis. "Keel" and "hulk" pay the same four pence at
  London about 1000 [JSON].
- **Evidence:** the Dorestad keel ship (after 739), the Domburg planks (after 769), the ship on the deniers of
  Dorestad and Quentovic, the Graveney boat (about 895), the Tiel keel ship (995 to 1008) [BJM] [MASS-Grav]. Texts:
  Boniface crossing from London to Dorestad in 716 "with favourable winds" [JSON]; the "Frisian pattern" of 896.
- **Size:** Graveney: 13.6 x 4 m, 1 m deep amidships; a flat plank keel, so she sits upright on a drying creek bed.
- **Crew and cargo:** five or six men (D); 6 to 7 t [MASS-Grav].
- **Sail and oars:** one square sail, perhaps 45 to 60 m2 (D). Nothing of mast, sail or oars survived at Graveney.
- **Waters:** estuaries, the Channel, the southern North Sea in fair weather. Low freeboard: she is "less capable of
  handling rough seas" [MASS-Grav].
- **In the game:** `north_sea_merchant_ship`. The Dorestad merchant's "ship" from 793. Dorestad to London, Quentovic,
  Hamwic, Ribe.

#### 4.11 The hulk

- **Called then:** hulc, in the London toll of about 1000 [WP-Hulk]. The word may come from Greek holkas, a towed
  boat, "which would be consistent with the use of the hulk as a river barge" [WP-Hulk].
- **Evidence:** the banana-shaped ship on the coins of Dorestad [de-Holk] [BJM, figure 7]. The shape of the Utrecht
  type. And that is all: "Ship remains of the hulk type, round, with a hollow hold and built clinker, have not yet
  been identified" [BJM]; "no wreck ... has been found" [WP-Hulk].
- **What can honestly be said:** about 1000, at Billingsgate, a hulk is a large ship from the Low Countries that pays
  the top toll. Whether she is a sea-going Utrecht type or a round all-plank ship, nobody knows.
- **In the game:** no hull to model. Use the word: an English toll man about 1000 calls the player's Utrecht ship or
  big Frisian trader "a hulk". No NEW row is needed unless the Utrecht ship is to go to sea (section 6).

#### 4.12 The cog

- **Called then:** cog. Frisians are "Cokingi", cog-men, in a ninth-century annal; a royal due on the Almere is
  "cogsculd" in the tenth; Frisian districts owe rowed "kogge" or "heerkogge" for war [BJM].
- **Evidence for the hull:** the angular ship on the Hedeby coins from about 825 [BJM, figure 7]; iron caulking
  clamps of the kind the later cog uses, traced to the Frisian area [de-Kogge]. The oldest cog found is Kollerup, of
  oak felled about 1150 in south Jutland: 20.9 x 4.92 m, draught 1.35 m [de-Kogge] (about 1145 in [BJM]). LATER by a
  century.
- **What she probably is in these years (?):** a flat-bottomed Wadden trader with straight raked ends and clinker
  sides, which sits upright when the tide leaves her, and which can be rowed. 12 to 16 m, 8 to 15 t (D). The Dutch
  inventory puts it carefully: the cog "possibly" grew out of the early medieval "Frisian ship", and shows features
  of the Norse build (clinker sides), of "a hypothetical Frisian flat-bottomed vessel" (the flush-laid bottom) and of
  Gallo-Roman building (the plank fastenings) [BJM].
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 870, and marked as a reconstruction. Her gift is the flats: she can work the
  Wadden, the Almere and the Limfjord where a keel ship must wait for water.

### D. Norse and Baltic traders

#### 4.13 The all-round ship of about 800

`norse_allround_ship`: 12 to 18 m, sail and many oars, a crew of 8 to 20 trading (Salme, Kvalsund, the Reric graves)
[JSON]. This is what a Dane or a Northman brings to Dorestad or Ribe in 793. Add to its evidence that the Dorestad
keel ship is built this way [MASS-Dor].

#### 4.14 The small sailing trader, 14 m

- **Called then:** byrðingr (?), skúta (?); the museum calls Skuldelev 3 a "coastal trader" [VSM-S3].
- **Evidence:** Skuldelev 3, about 1040, Danish oak: 14 x 3.3 x 0.9 m, displaces 9.6 t, carries 4.6 t, sail 45 m2,
  five oar ports, 5 to 8 men [VSM-S3]. A deck fore and aft and an open hold amidships.
- **Trials (Roar Ege, 1984-85):** about 4 kn over long distances; 8 to 10 kn at the most; six oars give 3.2 kn at
  the top and 2.0 kn kept up; beating in light winds makes 1.7 kn toward the wind; in the open Kattegat "the little
  trading ship struggles in the high waves, and water comes in across the gunwale" [VSM-Roar] [VSM-Trips].
- **Waters:** the Danish waters, the Baltic, the coasts. Not the open North Sea in bad weather.
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 900 (D: the cargo-only build is not found earlier). A farmer-merchant's or a
  small house's ship. It is also what "Dorestat 1" really is (section 11).

#### 4.15 The knarr

- **Called then:** Old Norse knǫrr; Old English cnear by 937 (?); kaupskip, a trading ship (?).
- **Evidence:** Äskekärr, 900 to 920: about 16 x 4.6 m, about 20 t, in use a century [sv-Ask]. Klåstad, 998: keel
  16.25 m, 20 to 22 m long, 4.5 m broad, lost with whetstones aboard [no-Kla]. The Tiel keel ship, 995 to 1008, of
  English and Ardennes timber [BJM]. Skuldelev 1, about 1030, of Sognefjord pine: 15.84 x 4.8 x 1.0 m, displacing
  20 t, carrying 24 t at 40% freeboard, sail 90 m2, two to four oars, six to eight men, five at the least
  [WP-Skuld] [CP]. She "could have sailed all over the North Sea and the Baltic as well as in the North Atlantic"
  and "may have been owned by a chieftain or cooperatively by a group of merchants" [VSM-S1].
- **Trials:** Ottar: 10 kn at the most under sail, 1.5 kn under oars; 9.8 kn on average through the Skagerrak in a
  gale with waves of 3 to 4 m; across the North Sea to Scotland, 262 sea miles, "only four degrees off course"
  [VSM-Ottar] [VSM-Trips]. Vidfamne (Äskekärr): 11 kn before a wind of 18 m/s; she "needs at least 7 t of ballast";
  she can tack and lie hove-to, and can be steered under six oars [sv-Ask]. A replica sailed from Greenland to
  Newfoundland in 1998 "could not sail to windward" until her steering oar was rebuilt [WP-Knarr].
- **Waters:** the open sea and the ocean. She draws 1.0 to 1.2 m, cannot be rowed against anything, and needs
  ballast when she is light.
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 900. Three times the Graveney boat's cargo with the same crew. The Wikipedia
  figures of 50 t displacement and a crew of 20 to 30 [WP-Vships] are not the finds' figures and should not be used.

#### 4.16 The great cargo ship

- **Evidence:** Hedeby 3, built at Hedeby about 1025: "a sturdily built cargo ship of a type similar to Skuldelev 1
  but with a cargo capacity of as much as c. 60 tons, according to the hypothetical reconstruction, giving a length
  of c. 22 m and a width of 6.25 m" [Hed3]; another summary gives 40 to 50 t [CP]. Then Lynæs, mid twelfth century,
  at least 60 t, and Bergen in the 1240s, over 150 t [CP].
- **Crew:** eight to twelve (D).
- **Waters:** open sea; she needs a jetty or lighters, and she cannot be hauled up a beach laden (D: about 1.5 m of
  draught).
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 1025. The end of the ladder. Only a house with partners fills her.

#### 4.17 Slav boats

`baltic_coastal_boat` (10 m, 2.5 t) stands for the Ralswiek and Szczecin boats [JSON]. Ralswiek's largest is 14 m by
3.4 m [de-Rals], so a larger Slav trader of 5 to 8 t (D) is true from the tenth century. NEW: the Slav longship
(Puck 2, 19 to 20 m, first half of the tenth century) [WP-Long] for the Wendish raiders of the eleventh.

### E. Ships a merchant meets and does not own

#### 4.18 The lord's ship

- **Called then:** karfi (later word) (?).
- **Evidence:** Oseberg (built about 820, buried 834; 21.58 x 5.10 m, 15 pairs of oars, sail about 90 m2), Gokstad
  (about 890; 23.80 x 5.10 m, 16 pairs, about 110 m2), Tune (about 900; 18.7 x 4.2 m, 11 or 12 pairs) [WP-Ose]
  [WP-Gok] [WP-Tune].
- **In the game:** the `norse_longship` row (22 m) is this ship. A jarl's or a king's reeve's.

#### 4.19 The small warship

- **Called then:** snekkja; Old English snacc (?). "With 13 benches, that is, 26 oarsmen, a snekke was regarded as
  the smallest ship usable for warfare" [WP-Long].
- **Evidence:** Ladby (900 to 950; 22 x 3 x 0.5 m; 16 pairs) [da-Ladby]; Skuldelev 5 (about 1030; 17.3 x 2.5 x 0.6
  m; 26 oars; about 30 men; sail 46 m2; built partly of old timber) [VSM-S5].
- **Trials (Helge Ask):** 5 to 6 kn kept up in a wind of 5 to 10 m/s on or abaft the beam; 2 kn at best tacking
  against it; 3.5 to 4 kn rowed in light air, 5 to 5.5 kn in a sprint. She capsized in 1993 while tacking in strong,
  gusting wind [VSM-Trials] [VSM-Trips] [VSM-Helge].
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 900: the coast guard's and the raider's ship of the tenth and eleventh centuries.
  She catches any trader in light air under oars. In a hard breeze and a sea the high-sided knarr can carry sail
  when she cannot (D).

#### 4.20 The great longship

- **Called then:** skeið; Old English scegð from 1008 (?); dreki for the largest (?).
- **Evidence:** Hedeby 1 (985; 30.9 x 2.7 m; up to 62 rowers) [WP-Hed1]; Roskilde 6 (about 1025; 36 to 37 m; 78
  rowers; about 100 men) [WP-Long]; Skuldelev 2 (1042, Dublin; about 30 x 3.8 x 1.0 m; 60 oars; 65 to 70 men; sail
  112 m2) [WP-Skuld].
- **Trials (Sea Stallion):** put at 6 to 8 kn on average and 17 kn at the most under sail; 2.5 kn on average and
  4.2 kn at the most under oars [VSM-SeaSt]. Roskilde to Dublin, 1 July to 14 August 2007, with stops [WP-SeaSt].
- **In the game:** NEW, from about 985. A king's. A merchant sees one when a fleet passes, and that is news worth
  carrying.

#### 4.21 English and Frankish king's ships

`warship_west`. Add as evidence: Alfred's sixty-oared ships of 896; the levy of 1008, one ship from 310 hides; the
bishops' ships of sixty and sixty-four oars in the wills (section 3). By 1000 the English warship is the Norse one
under an English name (scegð).

**How much sea.** The project's starting guesses by wave height (logboat 0.3 m, Gislinge 1.2, hide boat 2, Baltic

- Small sailing trader, 14 m: about 2 m. In high waves in the Kattegat water came in over the gunwale [VSM-Trips].
- Sea trader of the Graveney kind: about 2 m, not 3.5. Her freeboard is low [MASS-Grav].
- Knarr: about 4 m. Ottar ran at 9.8 kn in a gale in waves of 3 to 4 m [VSM-Trips]. The 3.5 m figure belongs here.
- Small warship: about 1.5 m, and gusts are her danger: Helge Ask capsized while tacking [VSM-Trips].
- Small coaster of the Skuldelev 6 kind: about 2 m. She came through a stiff gale, rolling [VSM-Trips].

**The river.** Hauled upstream at 10 to 12 km a day [BJM], Dorestad to Cologne (about 240 km by the river (D)) is
three weeks; the same water downstream at 50 km a day and more is five days (D). A six-oar boat that rows 2.5 to 3 kn
through the water gains hardly more on a current of 2 kn than the haulers do (D). Wine comes down fast and goes
nowhere quickly upstream: that is why the wine trade runs one way.

### 7.3 A ship's life and upkeep

- Skuldelev 3 was 20 to 30 years old when she was sunk in the barrier, and had been mended several times, with short
  lengths of plank let in below the waterline [EXARC].
- Roar Ege sailed 31 years and was given up at 32. In that time she took another 13.8 m3 of timber, 648 new rivets
  and 142 new spikes: a quarter of her timber and half her rivets over again [EXARC].
- The modern iron rivets "tend to need to be replaced after ca. 15 years", sooner below the waterline. Bog iron may
  have lasted longer; it is being tested [EXARC].
- The wool sail of 1985 "has outlived the ship" and is in use on her successor [EXARC] [VSM-Estrid].
- The museum's boats are launched in late spring and hauled out at the end of October, then tarred and mended over
  the winter [EXARC]. The period's own practice was the same (?).
- Skuldelev 1 was repaired with oak far from where she was built of pine: on the Oslofjord and in east Denmark
  [VSM-S1]. Skuldelev 5 was built and patched with old timber [VSM-S5]. Äskekärr was repaired about 1010, ninety
  years after she was built [sv-Ask]. Hedeby 1 lasted ten to twenty years before she was burnt [WP-Hed1].
- Barges: ten years, then into a quay [BJM].

For the game (D): upkeep of 2 to 3 per cent of the new price a year; a refit of the rivets in year 15 at about a
tenth of the price; the end at 25 to 30 years for a ship and 10 for a barge; an old hull is worth its timber.

## 8. Cost

### 8.1 The evidence there is

- **Tolls by size.** London, about 1000: a small boat a halfpenny, a larger boat with a sail a penny, a keel or a
  hulk four pence [JSON]. A sea-going trader was rated eight times a small boat.
- **The ship levy.** 1008: one warship from every 310 hides (?). At a geld of two shillings a hide, a common rate
  later in the century (?), 310 hides raise 7,440 d, or 31 pounds: a rough ceiling for a sixty-oared ship and her
  gear (D).
- **Wills.** An archbishop leaves his best ship, for sixty men, to the king, and a ship each to two shires
  [WP-Aelfric]. A ship is property on the scale of an estate.
- **Wages.** Harthacnut's seamen, 1040: eight marks a rowlock for the year (?). A mark is 160 d, so 1,280 d a man,
  3.5 d a day (D). These are fighting crews, the best paid men afloat. The geld for it was over 21,000 pounds
  [WP-Harth].
- **Shares.** Skuldelev 1 "may have been owned by a chieftain or cooperatively by a group of merchants" [VSM-S1]: the
  museum's reading, and it fits the Norse félag (research_scale_to_fleets.md).
- **Old timber had a price.** Skuldelev 5 was built partly from other ships; barges were sold into quays [VSM-S5]
  [BJM].
- **No sale price** for any merchant ship of 793 to 1066 was found (?).

## 10. The ladder: what a growing house really moved through

Two truths shape it. **Before about 900, a house grows by more hulls, not bigger ones.** No trader found from those
years carries more than 6 or 7 t. Ships of 18 to 22 m existed (Kvalsund, Oseberg), and might have carried 8 to 10 t
with a small crew (D), but they were built to be rowed by many. **After about 900, a house grows by size**: 20 t,
then 40 to 60 t, with the same handful of men. And all through, **the river and the sea are two fleets**: flat
craft that need oak and no iron, and keel ships that need iron, wool and a winter's work.

   felled after about 734, about 15 by 1.10 m [BJM]. The row's 10 by 1.5 m is the Weser boat; the Rhine one is longer
   and narrower.
2. **`towed_upstream_km_day`:** 18 (`logboat_expanded`, `river_barge`) and 20 (`river_estate_ship`). The Dutch study
   reckons 10 to 12 km a day upstream [BJM].
3. **`river_barge`:** 16 by 3 m and 7 t is right for 793. From about 826 the finds are 20 to 23 m and carry two to
   three times that. Kalkar is about 802, not about 805 [BJM].
4. **`gislinge`:** beam 1.5: the revised reconstruction of 2017 is 1.64 m [VSM-Lang]. `crew_range` "2-5": the museum
   says 2 to 4. `speed_max_kn` 6.5: the museum puts it at 6. `cargo_t` 0.9 "beyond crew": the museum's tonne is
   "four men and ten sheep", so it includes the men; BRIEF.md says 660 kg. One figure, about 0.65 t, should stand in
   both. `uses` has no trading in it, and she is the player's trading boat.
5. **`north_sea_merchant_ship`, `period_name`:** "OE ceol (keel) and hulc are attested later". Ceol is as old as
   Gildas (?). And the Frisian word is missing: cog, from the ninth century [BJM]. Its `uncertain` says the
   eighth-century Frisian hull is unknown; the one fragment found is Norse-built clinker [MASS-Dor].
6. **`north_sea_merchant_ship`, oars:** 2 kn for three hours with four oars and 6 t aboard. Ottar is put at 1.5 kn
   at the most; Roar Ege, with 4.6 t and six oars, keeps 2.0 [VSM-Ottar] [VSM-Trips]. Use 1 to 1.5.
   `gale_fragility` 0.4 is too kind to a hull of Graveney's low freeboard [MASS-Grav]: about 0.5 (D).
7. **`coaster_minster_ship`, oars:** 3 kn with six oars. Roar Ege keeps 2.0 and tops 3.2 with six; Kraka Fyr tops
   2.5 with four. Use 2 to 2.5.
8. **`norse_longship`, `rowing_sprint`:** "5.5 kn for 1.5 h, then 4 kn". Helge Ask sprints at 5 kn over a race and
   keeps 3.5 to 4; the Sea Stallion is put at 2.5 on average and 4.2 at the most [VSM-Trips] [VSM-SeaSt]. A sprint
   is a quarter of an hour, not an hour and a half (D). This is the chase rule, so it changes how often a trader
   gets away.
9. **`later_types_not_in_table`:** "the cog (12th c)". The hull found is twelfth century; the word and the due are
   ninth and tenth [BJM].
10. **`baltic_coastal_boat`:** the largest Ralswiek boat is 14 by 3.4 m [de-Rals]; the row's 10 m is the small end.
11. **`warship_west`:** add Alfred's sixty-oared ships (896), the levy of one ship from 310 hides (1008), and the
    bishops' ships of sixty and sixty-four oars.

### 11.3 Elsewhere in the project

## 12. What I could not check

- **The measured speed roses** (speed at each angle to the wind) of Roar Ege: in the report of 1997, not opened. The
  windward figures here rest on two sentences from the museum's pages.
- **The Giffords' half-scale Graveney boat, Ottor,** and their half-scale Sutton Hoo ship: the paper is in The
  Mariner's Mirror 82 (1996), pages 131 to 153; no figure from it is used.
- **The Slav boats:** only the one sentence on Ralswiek was read. Sizes of Ralswiek 2, Szczecin and Puck are from the
  game's table and from memory.
- **Hedeby 3:** read only in summaries of Crumlin-Pedersen (1997); the two summaries give 60 t and 40 to 50 t.
- **The coins:** that Dorestad and Quentovic struck ship deniers under Charlemagne and Louis the Pious is from a
  search summary; the hull shapes are as the Dutch inventory's figure caption describes them. No coin catalogue
  was read.
- **The Chronicle, the laws and the wills** (896, 937, 1008, 1040; IV Æthelred; Ælfwold's will; Æthelstan's
  prices): from memory and from the game's table. The wording must be taken from Giles, Ingram, Thorpe or Whitelock
  before any of it is quoted.
- **Adam of Bremen's sailing times** and **Gildas's keels:** from memory.
- **A sale price for a ship:** none found. Section 8.2 is arithmetic on replica hours and one assumed wage.
- **The Bruges boat:** only a library record of Marsden's paper of 1976, whose title calls her "a boat of the Roman
  period". The older medieval dating is from memory.
- **Klåstad's cargo of about 13 t** and the Gokstad mound's two larger boats: from the game's table and memory.
- **Distances** for Wulfstan's and Ohthere's voyages and for the Rhine: my own rough measures.
