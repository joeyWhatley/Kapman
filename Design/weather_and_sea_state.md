# Weather and sea state, 1:1 real

Design document, 4 October 2026. Scope: the weather of the North Sea and the Rhine delta as the game's levels feel it, from the climate's normals down to the hour; the sea the weather raises; how both drive HDRP's clouds, fog, light and Water System; and a deterministic day-by-day weather generator seeded by the year that reproduces the measured normals and carries the chronicle's recorded storms on their days.

What exists today (`Assets/Kapman/Code/Runtime/Levels`): `LevelWeather` picks one of eight `WeatherKind`s by hand or by a die every six game hours and eases a set of targets (cloud, rain, wind, fog) towards it; `HdrpSea` turns that wind into HDRP's swell and ripples with a lag; `HdrpWeatherDraw` builds a cloud map, drives HDRP's fog and the snow/frost/wet pass; `LevelClock` lights the world in lux and dims the Sun by a fixed 0.45 under full overcast; `LevelWater` moves the surface with the EOT20 tide and a flood slider. **The first build's synoptic model is already in Kapman2**: `Assets/Kapman/Code/Game/Sim/WeatherModel.cs` (559 lines, namespace `Kapman.Weather`, assembly `Kapman.Game`: `WeatherSample` struct, `ClimatePressure`, lows and highs with tracks and ridges, fronts, geostrophic wind, `Snapshot(hours)` / `Sample(snapshot, lat, lon, hours, overSea)`), ported on 4 October with the shim `Code/Game/Shims/Weather/LocalWeather.cs` (which makes its own `new WeatherModel(seed)`) and `FogBankField.cs`. `WorldSim.cs` constructs `new WeatherModel(793)` and samples it for every AI ship each hour (lines 67, 154, 244, 663, 695). Today the player's level does not read it: `LevelWeather` rolls its own die, so the fleets, `SeaWatch` and the raiders sail under one sky and the player under another. `Plans/storms.md` lists what that model lacked: no real storms, no chronicle storms, a sea capped at force 7, no swell from afar. This document keeps the Kapman2 code's shape (one weather, one sea, eased never switched), makes the existing `WeatherModel` the single source for the player and the fleets, extends it with a circulation chain, fronts, the chronicle's storms and a fuller sample, replaces the fixed targets with physics, and replaces the wind-to-sea mapping with a sea state model.

Rules kept: never run Unity; shippable data only; plain words. Figures marked (guess) are mine and need checking; everything else carries a source.

## 1. What is true

### 1.1 The measured climate, 1991-2020

The numbers below are computed from KNMI's open station data (daily and hourly series, 1991-2020, from the KNMI data service: POST to `https://www.daggegevens.knmi.nl/klimatologie/daggegevens` and `https://www.daggegevens.knmi.nl/klimatologie/uurgegevens` with `stns`, `vars`, `start`, `end`, `fmt=csv`, as documented at `https://www.knmi.nl/kennis-en-datacentrum/achtergrond/data-ophalen-vanuit-een-script`; the CSV header carries "SOURCE: ROYAL NETHERLANDS METEOROLOGICAL INSTITUTE (KNMI)"). **Provenance, honestly stated**: the script that made these tables was written in an earlier session's scratchpad and is not in the repository, nor are the downloads; there is no `Bake/weather` folder today. Until step 0 of the first slice (section 5) commits `Bake/weather/make_normals.py`, the raw KNMI CSVs and a SHA-256 of each download, and the script regenerates Tables A-E into this document, the tables are unaudited and the test tolerances built on them (4.3, tests 1, 2, 4, 5) are provisional. Every table below is therefore marked "(to be regenerated from make_normals.py; regenerated on: not yet)". **The day's cycle at De Kooy** (hourly means by season; hours 1-24 local):

- Fog (% of hours): winter 5.5 at 01 h rising to 7.1 at 08 h, falling to 3.9 at 15 h; spring 4.6 to 6.0 at 05 h, 0.8 at 13-14 h; summer 2.2 to 3.7 at 04 h, 0.3 all afternoon; autumn 4.0 to 5.5 at 06 h, 1.1 at 16 h.
- Wind (m/s): winter 6.3 all night, 7.0 at 12-13 h; spring 4.8 at night, 6.6 at 13-14 h; summer 4.0 at night, 6.2 at 14 h; autumn 5.1 at night, 6.5 at 13 h. The summer afternoon wind is half again the night's.
- Sea breeze: in summer the share of hours with the wind from N-NW (300-030 degrees) is 20% at 10-11 h and 31% at 17-18 h; in spring 21% to 31%; in winter flat at 15%. That is the sea breeze turning the wind onto the shore through the afternoon. KNMI: it sets in during the afternoon on fair warm days, reaches 20-40 km inland and sometimes past the middle of the country, and dies away in the evening (`https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeewind`); in general sea breezes reach 10-100 km inland and are 1-2 km deep, and an offshore gradient wind over 8 knots stops them (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_breeze`).
- Temperature: winter 3.7 at 06-07 h, 5.3 at 13-14 h (range 1.6); spring 7.2 at 04 h, 11.2 at 13-14 h (4.0); summer 14.9 at 03-04 h, 18.9 at 13 h (4.0); autumn 10.4 at 05 h, 13.2 at 13 h (2.8). Inland the range is twice that (De Bilt July: min 13.0, max 23.1 daily means). The minimum comes about dawn and the maximum two or three hours after solar noon (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnal_temperature_variation`).

**Winters and ice.** Freezing degree-hours at De Kooy (sum of hours' sub-zero degrees, per year): January 309, February 250, December 192, March 45, November 33: about 70 degree-days a winter on average, most of them in a few cold spells. The winters themselves (sum of sub-zero daily means, longest run of sub-zero days): 1996 174 / 23 days, 1997 97 / 13, 2012 69 / 15, 2010 63 / 11, 1991 61 / 15, 2003 54 / 8, 2011 48 / 7, 1994 47 / 9, 2013 44 / 14; eleven of thirty winters had under 10. KNMI's Hellmann cold number for De Bilt classes winters: under 40 mild, 100 cold, 160 very cold, over 300 severe; the harshest since 1901 were 1947 (342), 1963 (337), 1942 (332), 1940 (295), 1929 (229) (`https://www.knmi.nl/nederland-nu/klimatologie/lijsten/hellmann`). The Elfstedentocht needs 15 cm of natural ice along 200 km of Frisian canals and was held 15 times from 1909 to 1997 (1909, 1912, 1917, 1929, 1933, 1940, 1941, 1942, 1947, 1954, 1956, 1963, 1985, 1986, 1997): one winter in six through the 20th century, three in the last fifty years (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elfstedentocht`). Seawater of 35 g/kg freezes at about -2 degrees C (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater`); the Wadden flats, brackish and shallow, freeze earlier than the open sea. Ice grows roughly as the square root of accumulated freezing degree-days; the usual engineering constant is about 2-3 cm per square root of degree-day for still fresh water (guess, from the standard Stefan rule; the 1997 race, run after about 60 degree-days at the coast, gives a 2 cm constant: sqrt(60) x 2 = 15 cm, which fits).

**Sea temperature.** The only series fetched so far is a commercial satellite climatology for Scheveningen (`https://www.seatemperature.org/europe/netherlands/scheveningen.html`: Jan-Mar 6.4, Apr 8.9, May 11.8, Jun 14.9, Jul 16.9, Aug 18.2, Sep 18.1, Oct 15.6, Nov 12.9, Dec 9.4 °C; extremes 4.5 in January and 19.8 in August). Its identical January, February and March values are an artefact of that product: the coastal Dutch sea bottoms in late February to early March, near 4-5 °C in an ordinary winter and under 3 °C in a hard one (guess, until measured). Step 2 of `make_normals.py` reads the monthly means from Rijkswaterstaat's open sea-water temperature series (Waterinfo, stations Hoek van Holland and Scheveningen; open data, credit "Bron: Rijkswaterstaat") and the Scheveningen row is then regenerated like the others. What the model keys on is the sign of air minus sea: the sea lags the air by a month and more, so in April and May the air (9-13) is warmer than the sea (5-12): advection fog season at sea; from September to December the sea is warmer than the air, so the sea is clear while the land fogs.

**Fog physics.** Radiation fog needs clear skies, calm air and a dew point within about 2.5 degrees C; moderate or strong wind prevents it; advection (sea) fog is moist air cooled over colder water and can persist in a fresh wind (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog`). Hoar frost forms on clear, calm nights with the surface below the frost point; rime is supercooled fog freezing onto the windward side of rigging and twigs, hard rime at -2 to -10 degrees C in wind, soft rime in calm (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoar_frost`, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rime_ice`).

**Visibility.** Meteorological optical range by Koschmieder: MOR = 3.912 / b_ext for a 2% contrast threshold; WMO: fog under 1 km, mist 1-2 km, haze 2-5 km (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visibility`). HDRP's Fog Attenuation Distance is "the distance at which the fog has absorbed and out-scattered 63% of background light", i.e. the mean free path 1/b_ext (`https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@17.6/manual/fog-volume-override-reference.html`). So **MOR = 3.912 x meanFreePath**, and the game's FogDensity (= 1/meanFreePath) is 3.912/MOR.

**Light.** Direct sun at noon 120,000 lux; bright sunlight 111,000; full daylight in shade 20,000; overcast midday 1,000-2,000; very dark storm overcast under 200; clear sunrise/sunset 400; twilight under full overcast 40; deep twilight under storm cloud under 1; full moon 0.25; quarter moon 0.01; starlight 0.002; overcast moonless 0.0001 (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daylight`). The clock's fixed 0.45 under overcast is far too bright: a full nimbostratus deck passes about 1-2% of the Sun.

**Clouds.** Low (0-2 km): stratus (drizzle, snow grains), stratocumulus, fair-weather cumulus; middle (2-7 km): altostratus (a veil, light steady rain), altocumulus; high (5-13 km): cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus; through the depths: nimbostratus (steady rain, hours) and cumulonimbus (downpours, hail, thunder). Ahead of a warm front: cirrus 12-24 h before, then cirrostratus, altostratus, nimbostratus as the rain begins; a cold front is a narrow line of cumuliform and cumulonimbus cloud with little warning (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud`). At a cold front the wind veers SW to NW, pressure falls steadily ahead and rises sharply behind, a narrow rain band on the front and wider bands behind, temperature drops (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_front`). Winds circle anticlockwise round a low (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pressure_area`); Buys Ballot: back to the wind, low pressure on your left and somewhat ahead (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buys_Ballot%27s_law`). The Coriolis parameter f = 2 Omega sin(lat) with Omega 7.292e-5 rad/s is 1.15e-4 /s at 52 N (1.26e-4 is the value at 60 N; the existing `WeatherModel` line 330 computes f from latitude correctly) (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostrophic_wind`). A thunderstorm cell lives through developing, mature and dissipating stages of about 30 minutes each, its anvil at the tropopause; thunder follows the flash at 3 s a kilometre, sound at 343 m/s (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderstorm`, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunder`). Explosive deepening is more than 1 hPa an hour; the deepest North Atlantic low on record is the Braer storm, 914 hPa, January 1993 (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extratropical_cyclone`). Standard values not fetched this session (guess, from textbooks): a mature low is 1,000-2,000 km across and crosses the North Sea at 40-60 km/h; the surface wind over the sea is about 0.7 of the geostrophic wind and crosses the isobars 10-20 degrees towards low pressure, over land 0.4-0.5 and 25-35 degrees.

### 1.2 The climate of 793-1066

- European summers were cool from the 4th to the 7th century, warmed through the 9th-12th centuries and peaked in the 10th; the Medieval Climate Anomaly is put at 900-1200; the reconstruction is gridded from 755 CE (Luterbacher et al. 2016, Environmental Research Letters 11, `https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/11/2/024001`). The Medieval Warm Period is usually dated 950-1250, warm in western Europe, Iceland and Greenland, not globally synchronous (PAGES 2k 2019; `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Warm_Period`).
- Eldgjá (Iceland) erupted in 939-940, 232 million tonnes of sulphur dioxide, a Northern Hemisphere cooling of 0.7-1.5 degrees C in 940, severe winters in Europe 939-942 "with the sea and canals freezing", a dimmed sun ("the colour of blood from the beginning of day to midday", Chronicon Scotorum) (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eldgj%C3%A1`). The chronicle already carries it (`939-eldgja`).
- The chronicle's own weather: a hard winter 821/822 with the Rhine, Danube, Elbe and Seine frozen so that carts crossed for thirty days (ARF 821); the Christmas Eve gale of 800 in Northumbria, a south wind veering SW-W, the sea flooding the land (HR 800); the Clare storm of 16 March 804; the Frisian storm flood of 26 December 838, 2,437 drowned (AB 839); the great sea-flood of 28 September 1014 (ASC); the storm of 799 in the British sea; thunder on St Michael's day 799 in Ireland; a hail storm in 823. The Dutch flood lists add 1042 (Flemish coast and the Yser) (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_floods_in_the_Netherlands`, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_tides_of_the_North_Sea`); the London flood of 11 November 1099 is after the game's end in 1066 and is left out. `Plans/storms.md` adds, from research, Ireland 816 and 878, Swanage 877 (120 ships), 1009, 1039, 1049/50, 1052, St Thomas's night 1053.
- What to assume (guess, with reasoning): the modern 1991-2020 normals include about 1 degree C of recent warming over the 1961-1990 normals. For 793-900 use the normals cooled by 0.8 degrees C (winters a little harder: winters that freeze a flowing river channel, 150+ freezing degree-days, about one in six to eight rather than the one in ten of the Hellmann record, see 2.4); ramp to +0.3 degrees C over the modern normals by 950-1066 for summers and 0 for winters (the MCA was mostly a summer and a northern signal); 939-942 cold by 1.0-1.5 degrees C with a hazy, reddened sun through 939-940; 821/822 and the chronicle's winters forced as recorded. Storminess: no reliable medieval storm statistics exist for the North Sea; keep the modern frequencies and let the chronicle's storms be the "great" ones. Fog: cleaner pre-industrial air gives fewer fog days than the smoggy 1950-80s and about the modern count on the coast; the undrained river land (marsh, carr, standing water) gives more radiation fog inland: inland normals x 1.3.

### 1.3 The sea

- **Beaufort and sea state** (probable Hs by force, open sea, from the WMO table): F3 0.6-1.2 m, F4 1-2, F5 2-3, F6 3-4, F7 4-5.5 (foam blown in streaks), F8 5.5-7.5 (spindrift), F9 7-10 (dense streaks, crests topple), F10 9-12.5 (long overhanging crests), F11 11.5-16, F12 14+ (air filled with foam and spray). WMO sea state codes: slight 0.5-1.25 m, moderate 1.25-2.5, rough 2.5-4, very rough 4-6, high 6-9, very high 9-14 (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_state`). These open-ocean heights are never reached in the southern North Sea: it is fetch- and depth-limited.
- **Growth laws.** Fully developed sea: fp = 0.123 g/U10 and energy E = 0.005 rho U10^4/g (`https://www.coastalwiki.org/wiki/Statistical_description_of_wave_parameters`), which with Hs = 4 sqrt(m0) gives Hs = 0.028 U10^2 (10 m/s: 2.8 m; 20 m/s: 11 m) and Tp = 0.83 U10 (10 m/s: 8.3 s). Fetch-limited (JONSWAP, Hasselmann 1973 as given at `https://www.codecogs.com/library/engineering/fluid_mechanics/waves/spectra/jonswap.php`): omega_p = 2.84 g^0.7 X^-0.3 U^-0.4 (X the fetch in m), alpha = 0.033 (omega_p U/g)^(2/3), gamma 3.3, sigma 0.07 below the peak and 0.09 above; and the Shore Protection Manual's fetch law, which is written in the **adjusted wind** U_A = 0.71 U10^1.23 (U10 in m/s; SPM 1984 eq. 3-28a, the "wind stress factor"; from memory, the manual's chapter 3 could not be fetched this session, and the Coastal Engineering Manual of 2002 recasts the same law in the friction velocity u*): Hs = 0.0016 U_A sqrt(X/g), Tp = 0.2857 (U_A/g) (g X/U_A^2)^(1/3). Using raw U10 in it underestimates Hs by 20% at 10 m/s and 40% at 20 m/s, which is the whole southern North Sea's storm sea. The duration needed for the fetch to count is CEM 2002's t_x = 77.23 X^0.67 / (U10^0.34 g^0.33) (seconds, X in m, U10 raw), shorter durations acting as a shorter fetch X_eff = (t U10^0.34 g^0.33 / 77.23)^(1/0.67). Check against the fully-developed table at `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_wave` (fetched: 37 km/h over 139 km for 10 h gives average height 1.5 m, average period 5.7 s): 10.3 m/s gives U_A 12.5 m/s and Hs = 0.0016 x 12.5 x sqrt(139,000/9.81) = 2.4 m, which is 1.6 x the table's average height, as Rayleigh says it should be (Hs = 1.6 H_mean); t_x comes out 12.8 h. The first draft of this document used U10 and got 2.0 m for the same case, and noticed the gap.
- **Fetches that matter** (map measurement, guess to 20%): off Katwijk, N 600 km (open to Norway), NW 500, W 180 (to Norfolk), SW 150 (the Channel narrows), S/SE/E 0-20 (offshore). Thames estuary 20-60 km by direction; the Wadden Sea 5-20 km behind the islands; the Zuiderzee (Almere) 20-50 km; the Kromme Rijn at Dorestad 60-100 m across (a 10 m/s wind raises 5 cm of chop), the Lek 300-500 m (10 cm), the Rhine mouth 1-2 km (20-25 cm).
- **Shallow water.** Depth-limited breaking at H = 0.78 h (Battjes: the index varies 0.4-1.2 with slope, gamma = xi0^0.17 + 0.08); dispersion omega^2 = g k tanh(kh); shoaling Ks = sqrt(cg0/cg); refraction by Snell's law (`https://www.coastalwiki.org/wiki/Shallow-water_wave_theory`); Green's law H h^(1/4) = const once the depth is under a twentieth of the wavelength (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_shoaling`); deep-water breaking when H exceeds about 0.17 of the wavelength, shallow-water breaking at 0.8 h, wave speed 1.25 sqrt(L) in deep water, 3.1 sqrt(d) in shallow, groups at half the phase speed (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_wave`). Breaker type by Iribarren number xi = tan(slope)/sqrt(H/L0): spilling under 0.5, plunging 0.5-3.3, surging above (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iribarren_number`). On the Dutch sand beaches (slope 1:50-1:100, xi 0.1-0.3) the surf spills; on the chalk shingle of Kent it plunges.
- **Individual waves.** Rayleigh: one wave in ten exceeds 1.07 Hs, one in a hundred 1.51 Hs, one in a thousand 1.86 Hs; a rogue wave is over 2 Hs (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height`). The expected highest wave in N waves is H_max = Hs sqrt(0.5 ln N) (plus a small correction): with the zero-crossing period Tz about 0.78 Tp, a 30-minute run at Tp 8 s holds about 290 waves and H_max is 1.65-1.7 Hs; 1.9 Hs needs about 1,700 waves, three hours. Storm waves: Tp about 5 sqrt(Hs) (coastalwiki).
- **Tide and currents.** Semidiurnal, 12 h 25 min; springs and neaps in the North Sea come two days after full/new and quarter moons; slack water near high and low (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide`). Ranges: Den Helder 2.15 m, Rotterdam 2.39 m, London Bridge up to 7.99 m, the Wash 7.26 m, German Bight 3-4 m; mean depth 90 m, Dogger Bank 15-30 m; the sea 6 degrees C in winter, 17 in summer (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea`). Thames estuary: a 4 m range, tidal streams 2.6 knots at springs, the sandbanks (Long Sand, Kentish Knock, the Sunk, Girdler, Knock John) and deeps between (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Estuary`). The North Sea seiches lengthwise with a period of about 36 h; harbours and estuaries seiche a few centimetres over a few minutes, Merian T = 2L/sqrt(gh) (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiche`).
- **Storm surge.** 1 cm of sea level per hPa of pressure fall; wind set-up pushes water onto the downwind shore, worst over wide shallow shelves (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_surge`). The set-up law: the surface slope is the wind stress over the water column, d(eta)/dx = C_d rho_air U10^2 / (rho_water g h); the engineering form is delta_h = 0.5 kappa (U^2 / (g h)) F cos(phi) with kappa 2.2e-6 (IJsselmeer), 3.3e-6 (Ippen, open coasts) to 3.8e-6 (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_setup`), i.e. 0.5 kappa / g = 1.1-1.9e-7 s2/m; with the gale drag coefficient C_d 2.0e-3, rho_air 1.25 and rho_water 1,025 kg/m3 the constant is C_d rho_a / (rho_w g) = 2.5e-7 s2/m (C_d rises with wind speed, so the two agree). The North Sea is 30-90 m deep along most of a NW fetch from Norway and 15-25 m only in the last 100 km onto the Frisian coast, so the set-up is an integral along the fetch's depth profile, not one depth. The user's decision (2 Oct): the recorded storms give 2-3 m surges, force 10-11, gusts 35-40 m/s.
- **The Rhine.** 2,290 m3/s mean at the Dutch border, about 2,900 at the mouth; Alpine snowmelt gives high water in summer, the German rain tributaries in winter, so the total is relatively steady (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine`, `https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijn`). Lobith monthly means (guess, from memory of Rijkswaterstaat tables): about 3,000 in February-March, 1,500-1,700 in September-October; the record low about 600, the record flood 12,600 (January 1926). For c. 800 the dorestad notes already put summer water at 3 m NAP against dams at 4 m.

## 2. The model

### 2.1 Shape

Four layers, all deterministic from (world seed, year), all in `Kapman.Core` with no Unity dependency so they run in EditMode tests (the test assembly references only `Kapman.Core`) and, later, on a multiplayer host. Layers 2 and 3 are the existing `WeatherModel`, moved from `Kapman.Game` to `Kapman.Core` and extended in place, not a second model beside it: the player's level and every AI ship sample the same instance.

1. **Climate**: monthly normals by place class (coast / Wadden / Channel / river land / northern sea) from Table A-E, plus the year's offset (1.2).
2. **Synoptic engine** (the day): a Markov chain of circulation types placing lows, highs and their fronts on a map of the whole North Sea; evaluated at any (lat, lon, hour).
3. **Local weather** (the hour): the surface wind from the pressure field, the air mass, the day's cycle (temperature, sea breeze, fog), precipitation and cloud from the frontal phase, gusts.
4. **Sea state** (the level): wind sea and swell from the wind history and the level's fetch table; tide stream, surge, river stage, ice.

The chronicle's storms are inserted into layer 2 on their dates; everything below follows.

### 2.2 The synoptic engine

**Circulation types** (after Lamb's types, simplified): A anticyclonic (a high over or near the North Sea; light winds, clear or stratus, fog), C cyclonic (a low over the North Sea; wind shifting, showers), W westerly (lows passing north of Scotland; SW-W winds, fronts crossing every 1-3 days), SW (the storm track further south: deep lows across the North Sea; the gale type), NW (lows into Scandinavia; NW winds, cold showers), N (northerly behind a high to the west; cold, clear, showers on the coast), E (blocking high over Scandinavia; E-NE winds, dry; cold in winter, warm in late spring), S (a high east, a low west; S-SE winds, mild and moist).

The chain changes state once a day (at 00 h) with a month-dependent transition matrix P_m. Persistence is the diagonal: tuned so the daily wind's lag-1 autocorrelation comes out 0.55 and pressure's 0.81; the stationary distribution reproduces Table C (W+SW+NW about 50% in winter, N+NE+E 45% in May). Starting values (guess, to be fitted by the test in 4.3):

| from \ to | A | C | W | SW | NW | N | E | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (winter) | 0.55 | 0.03 | 0.14 | 0.06 | 0.06 | 0.04 | 0.06 | 0.06 |
| W (winter) | 0.12 | 0.08 | 0.45 | 0.15 | 0.12 | 0.03 | 0.02 | 0.03 |

Twelve matrices (one a month, interpolated by day) are data, not code: `weather/circulation.json`.

**Pressure field.** p(lat, lon, t) = p_clim(month, lat, lon) + sum over systems of s_i A_i(t) exp(-d_i^2) where d_i is the distance from the system's centre scaled by its radii, s_i +1 for a high and -1 for a low. p_clim is the existing `WeatherModel.ClimatePressure` (line 106 of the Kapman2 file: Azores high, Icelandic low, Siberian high, the westerly belt), used as is. Each circulation type spawns systems with a template: a track (start, heading, speed 35-60 km/h), a life (deepening 18-30 h, mature 12-24 h, filling 24-48 h), a depth (ordinary lows 985-1005 hPa; SW type 965-990; the rare severe low 945-970, allowed November to March with probability tuned to 0.1-0.2 F10 days a year on the open coast), radii 400-900 km, and an asymmetry (the strongest gradient on the south and south-west side of the low, where the fronts run). Highs: 1025-1045 hPa, 800-1,500 km, slow (10-20 km/h). Systems live across type changes: the chain decides what is spawned and where the track goes; the field is the sum of what is alive. Positions are interpolated linearly within the hour; depths cubically.

**Fronts.** Each low carries a warm front (from the centre, bearing 120 degrees, bowed) and a cold front (bearing 210 degrees, straighter, moving faster so it occludes after 36-48 h). An observer's frontal phase phi is the signed distance (km) to the nearest front, scaled: phi < -300 km ahead of the warm front (cirrus); -300..-100 (cirrostratus, altostratus thickening, pressure falling 1-2 hPa/3 h, wind backing to S); -100..0 (nimbostratus, steady rain 1-3 mm/h, stratus fractus below); the warm sector (stratus 150-400 m, drizzle, mild, SW, pressure steady, poor visibility 3-8 km); the cold front 0..+30 km (a line of cumulonimbus, 5-15 mm/h for 1-2 h, squalls at 1.5-1.8 times the mean wind, the wind veering SW to NW over 1-3 h, pressure rising sharply, temperature down 3-6 degrees); behind +30..+300 (clearing, cumulus and showers in the cold air, gusty, visibility 20-40 km); then the ridge. These are the hourly curves the local weather reads; nothing switches.

**The year seed.** `SimRandom.For(worldSeed ^ (year * 0x9E37), "weather")` streams the chain and the system templates: the same world seed replays the same century; a different seed gives different ordinary weather; the chronicle's storms are the same in every world because they are data, not draws. Determinism from (seed, year) holds on one machine; it is not relied on between peers: `Math.Exp`, Perlin noise and float summation can differ between Mono and IL2CPP or x64 and ARM. In multiplayer one host evaluates the engine and streams the hourly `WeatherSample` for each level (a few hundred bytes an hour; a snapshot of the live systems on join); peers only interpolate between samples, and the sea state is derived locally from the streamed samples, so every peer sees the same front at the same hour.

**Chronicle storms** (`weather/storms_793.json`, one entry per recorded storm: date and hour of peak, track (start lat/lon, heading, speed), central pressure, radius, asymmetry, peak wind and gust at a named place, surge height and coast, region ids). On those days the chain's draws are overridden: the storm's low is spawned 48 h before its peak, random systems within 1,000 km are quietened (their amplitude fades to 0.3 over 24 h), and the type is forced to SW or NW for the storm's duration. The approach is natural: pressure falls over two days, a long swell arrives 12-24 h ahead (2.5), the wind backs S and rises, the cold front veers it W-NW at the peak, and the surge comes with the high water nearest the peak. Entries for the twelve storms listed in 1.2 (eight from the plan plus the four in the chronicle) are the data to write; the 838 and 1014 entries are NW force 10-11 with 2-3 m surges on the Frisian / English coasts; 800 is a south wind veering SW-W over Northumbria.

### 2.3 Local weather, hour by hour

Output is the existing `Kapman.Weather.WeatherSample` struct (`WeatherModel.cs` lines 11-62: pressure, windFrom, windSpeed, gustiness, cumulus, layered, storm, rain, fog, temperature, `Snow`, `Cover`, `VisibilityKm`, `Describe()`), extended in place so every existing reader compiles: new fields gust (m/s), dewPoint, cloud cover by layer (low, middle, high) and type (stratus / cumulus / cumulonimbus / altostratus / cirrus), cloudBase (m), cloudDepth (m), cloudTransmittance (2.5), snowDepth (cm), hail (0-1), fog stays 0-1, visibility MOR (m; `VisibilityKm` becomes MOR / 1,000 and keeps its old fallback when MOR is unset), frontPhase, seaBreeze (0-1), thunder (0-1), rainbow (bool), auroraChance (0-1).

- **Wind.** Geostrophic Vg = |grad p| / (rho f), rho 1.25 kg/m3, f = 2 Omega sin(lat) computed from latitude as the existing code does (1.15e-4 at 52 N); surface U10 = k Vg turned theta towards the low: over sea k 0.70, theta 15 degrees; over the river land k 0.45, theta 30 degrees (guess, standard values). Add the sea breeze and the day's cycle: U10 x (1 + 0.25 D(h)) over land and (1 + 0.12 D(h)) at the coast with D the daytime bell (0 at night, 1 at 14 h, scaled by sunshine and by the season's day length), from the diurnal data above. Add a slow noise (Perlin in time, 3 h scale) of +-10% speed and +-8 degrees so no hour is exactly its neighbour; the hourly direction change then matches the measured median of 10 degrees. Gust = U10 x G with G 1.55 in steady air (measured 1.52-1.58), 1.75 in showers, 1.9 at a cold front; the gust field for HDRP's clouds, the water's ripples and the ship is a moving 2D noise of 200-800 m cells advected at the wind speed (the plan's S1).
- **Sea breeze.** When the type is A, N, E or the gradient wind is under 4 m/s, the sky at least half clear, the month April-September and the land-sea temperature difference over 3 degrees C: a component of 3-6 m/s blowing from the sea onto the coast, starting at 11-13 h, peaking at 16-17 h, gone by 21 h, reaching 30 km inland (Dorestad at 100 km does not feel it); at night a land breeze of 1-2 m/s off the land. This gives the measured afternoon N-NW share and the afternoon wind rise.
- **Temperature.** T = T_clim(month, place) + dT_airmass(type: E in winter -4, N -2, S +3, SW +2 in winter) + daily range R(cloud, wind, place) x cos-shaped cycle with the minimum at dawn and the maximum 2.5 h after solar noon; R = 9 degrees inland, 4 at the coast, 1.5 at sea, times (1 - 0.6 cloud) times (1 - 0.4 min(1, U10/8)). Dew point from the air mass's humidity (sea air 85-95%, continental E air 50-70%, warm sector 95%).
- **Fog.** Radiation fog over land and river: probability rises when the sky is clear, U10 under 2.5 m/s, the dew point depression under 2 degrees, between dusk and two hours after sunrise, in autumn and winter (long nights); it forms over the water first (the river mist already in `HdrpWeatherDraw`), thickens to MOR 100-300 m by dawn, lifts to stratus and burns off by 10-11 h in summer, persists all day in winter under a high (the measured runs of 2-12 fog days under winter anticyclones). Advection fog at sea: when the air temperature exceeds the sea temperature by 2 degrees or more with dew point near the sea temperature, wind 2-8 m/s, mostly April-June and in mild winter southerlies; it sits in banks (the first build's `FogBankField`: patches 2-10 km across that drift with the wind) and lasts until the wind veers or the air cools. Freezing fog when T < 0: rime on rigging and reeds (the snow/frost pass gets a rime term). Sea smoke: cold air (T < -8) over open water in winter: wisps 1-3 m high over the river and the Wadden channels.
- **Precipitation** by frontal phase and type: warm front 1-3 mm/h steady for 4-8 h; warm sector drizzle 0.1-0.5; cold front 5-15 for 1-2 h; cold-air showers 2-8 mm/h for 15-40 min, 2-6 an afternoon, cells 2-5 km across moving with the wind; C type showers all day; A dry; E dry (winter snow grains); summer heat thunderstorms when T > 24 inland, dew point > 15, type A/S/E, from 15 h, a cell lasting 30-60 min with the three-stage life, flashes every 5-20 s near the core (user's choice), thunder arriving at 3 s/km. Snow instead of rain when T < 1 at the surface, sleet 1-3; snow lies while T < 1 and melts above (the existing `snowLying` logic, with the rate tied to mm/h). Four lines the brief's living-world list asks for and the first draft left out:
  - **Snow depth** in cm: depth += rate (mm/h water) x 10 (fresh snow at 1:10) x hours while T < 1; depth -= 3 cm per positive degree-day of melt plus 1 cm/h in rain at T > 2; `snowLying` becomes min(1, depth / 3 cm) so the pass is unchanged. Dorestad's 821/822 winter lies under snow for weeks; an ordinary winter a few days at a time (Table E: 18 snow days a year on the coast).
  - **Hail** from cumulonimbus cells whose top is colder than -20 °C (summer heat storms with T > 24 inland; spring showers in cold NW air behind a front, March-May): hail 0-1 for the cell's mature stage, 5-15 minutes under the core; the sound and the ship's deck get it as a rate.
  - **Rainbow** flag when the Sun is above the horizon and under 42 degrees, rain is falling within 2-5 km in the direction opposite the Sun (a shower cell or a departing front) and the sky toward the Sun is at least half clear; `SkyDome` draws the bow (primary 42 degrees, secondary 51) in the antisolar direction; it is a sign the player learns to read as "the shower has passed".
  - **Aurora**: `LevelWeather` already draws one on a hashed 1.5% of nights (line 186). Its chance now comes from this engine: a clear-sky night (low and middle cover under 0.3, MOR over 10 km) times a Kp-style geomagnetic draw (a 27-day recurrence from a seeded stream: 1.5% of nights on average at 52 N, 6% at 60 N for the Norway levels), dimmed to a quarter through the 939-940 Eldgjá haze and brightened in the equinox months (March-April, September-October, when auroral activity peaks).
- **Cloud.** The type and front give the layers: low cover (stratus / stratocumulus / cumulus), middle (altostratus / altocumulus), high (cirrus, cirrostratus); cloud base for cumulus 125 x (T - Td) m (a standard rule, from memory), stratus 150-400 m, nimbostratus 300-800 m, altostratus 3,000-5,000 m, cirrus 8,000-10,000 m; cover by octas drawn so the month's mean NG and the clear/dull day shares match Table D. Cloud optical depth tau from type and thickness (fair cumulus 5-20 where it covers, stratocumulus 10-30, stratus 15-40, altostratus 5-20, nimbostratus 50-200, cumulonimbus 100-500, cirrus 0.1-2): the Sun's transmitted light is what reaches the ground.
- **Visibility.** 1/MOR = 1/MOR_air + 1/MOR_fog + 1/MOR_rain + 1/MOR_snow + 1/MOR_spray. MOR_air 40-60 km in clean polar air, 15-25 km in warm-sector or continental haze, 5-10 km in summer haze under a high (the measured 20 km+ share is 60% in summer, 30% in winter); MOR_fog from the fog state (thick 50-200 m, fog 200-1,000, mist 1-2 km); MOR_rain about 10 km at 1 mm/h, 3 km at 10 mm/h, 1 km in a downpour (guess, standard order of magnitude); MOR_snow 0.5-1 km in moderate snow; MOR_spray at sea from force 8: 5 km, force 10: 2 km. The result is one number read by the fog draw, the lookout, the AI fleets and the sound.

### 2.4 Sea state

Per level, stepped every game minute (real second), from the wind history and a fetch table `levels/<id>/fetch.json` (8 sectors: fetch km, a depth profile along the fetch (m at 0, 25, 50, 75, 100% of the fetch), exposure 0-1).

**Time, in real seconds.** The player lives at 1 s = 1 game minute and the ship moves at real speed, so every time constant below is also a real duration on screen:

| What | Game time | Real time at 1x | At 4x | Acceptable at 4x? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure falling before a chronicle storm | 48 h | 48 min | 12 min | yes |
| Warm-front cloud sequence (cirrus to rain) | 12-24 h | 12-24 min | 3-6 min | yes |
| Swell lead before a storm 500 km out | 12-24 h | 12-24 min | 3-6 min | yes |
| Sea build-up, fresh breeze over 100 km | 3-6 h | 3-6 min | 45-90 s | yes |
| Sea build-up, gale over 500 km | 10-15 h | 10-15 min | 2.5-4 min | yes |
| Cold front passage (veer SW to NW, squalls) | 1-3 h | 1-3 min | 15-45 s | hurried: readable but no time to act |
| Thunderstorm cell, birth to death | 60-90 min | 60-90 s | 15-22 s | no |
| Fog burn-off after sunrise | 1-2 h | 1-2 min | 15-30 s | yes |
| Wind sea decay after the wind drops (e-fold) | 6 h | 6 min | 90 s | yes |

So the engine asks `LevelClock` to drop to 1x (and refuses 4x) while a cold front is within 30 km, a cumulonimbus cell within 5 km, or the wind at force 8 or more: the dangerous part of the weather is always met at real pace, and the quiet parts may be hurried. `Plans/storms.md` already forbids 4x in multiplayer. The sea state model steps at clock speed (4x at 4x); the water surface's own wave motion runs at real speed, see 2.5.

- **Wind sea.** The target Hs_eq(U_A, X_eff, h) is the SPM fetch-limited height in the adjusted wind U_A = 0.71 U10^1.23 (1.3), capped by the fully developed value in U10 (Hs = 0.028 U10^2) and by depth (Hs <= 0.5 h as a working cap and the TMA damping of the long waves in depth under Tp^2/8); X_eff is the lesser of the sector's fetch and the duration-limited fetch (CEM's t_x law in raw U10) from the wind's recent history (hours the wind has held within 30 degrees and 20% of its present strength). Worked: 20 m/s (U_A 28.3) over 150 km for 6 h: t_x is 10.9 h so the sea is duration-limited, X_eff 63 km, Hs 3.6 m (the first draft's raw-U10 version gave 2.6 m). The state Hs relaxes toward the target with the growth time t_x (hours, from the duration law: 3-6 h for a fresh breeze over 100 km, 10-15 h for a gale over 500 km) and decays after the wind drops with an e-fold time of 6 h for the short wind sea; Tp follows from the fetch law and is bounded below by 5 sqrt(Hs) in storms; direction follows the wind with a lag of 1-2 h; directional spread 25-35 degrees (JONSWAP), narrowing with fetch. Steepness s = Hs/L0 with L0 = g Tp^2/(2 pi): 0.03-0.05 ordinary, 0.06-0.08 steep (young, fetch-limited, wind against tide); whitecap fraction W = 3.8e-6 U10^3.4 (Monahan's law, from memory; 10 m/s: 0.9%, 20 m/s: 10%).
- **Swell.** The synoptic engine knows every low's position, depth and the wind on its strong side. Each 6 h, each low with U > 15 m/s emits a swell train toward the level: Hs_swell = 0.3 x Hs_storm x (1 - d/3000 km) (guess, a plain attenuation), Tp = Tp_storm, travelling at the group speed cg = g Tp/(4 pi) (12 s: 9.4 m/s, 34 km/h): a storm 500 km out announces itself 15 h ahead with a long low swell under a quiet sky, the oldest warning the sailors had. Up to three trains are kept (Hs, Tp, direction, arrival, decay e-fold 24 h). The ocean levels are shortened 1:150, but the sea state is computed at the level's real place, so the swell's lead time is real.
- **Warm start.** The wind sea and the swell are stateful (the duration-limited fetch needs the last 24 h of wind; the swell trains need every low of the last 48 h), but a level loads at an arbitrary hour, the clock jumps (`LevelClock` T/Y/U keys: an hour, a day, a month; the "jump to date" tool), and a save loads mid-storm. So on level entry, on any clock jump and on load, `SeaStateModel.WarmStart(hours)` replays the engine's hourly samples for the previous 48 h at the level's point (48 stateless `Sample` calls: microseconds) and re-emits the swell trains from the lows alive in those snapshots, rebuilding Hs, Tp, direction, spread and the trains before the first frame; then `HdrpSea.SettleSea()` snaps the surface to it. Without this every level would open on a flat sea that builds for real minutes, and the 15-hour swell warning would be missing exactly when the player enters the ocean level.
- **Combined sea.** Hs_total = sqrt(Hs_sea^2 + sum Hs_swell^2); "confused" C = the angle between wind sea and the largest swell over 90 degrees, 0-1. H_1/10 = 1.27 Hs; the expected highest wave of a run H_max(N) = Hs sqrt(0.5 ln N) with N = duration / Tz and Tz = 0.78 Tp: 1.65-1.7 Hs over 30 minutes, 1.9 Hs over 3 hours, for the danger model in `Plans/storms.md` S6 (the first draft's 1.9 Hs per half hour would have thrown a rogue-sized wave at the ship every thirty minutes).
- **Tide stream, surge, river.** The tidal stream at the level's point: speed from the EOT20 tide's rate (`LevelWater.TideRate`) scaled by a per-level factor (open Dutch coast 0.5-1 knot at springs alongshore, flood running NE; Thames 2.6 knots at springs in the channels; Wadden channels 1-2 knots) and a direction table; wind against tide raises the steepness by 1.3 and the whitecap fraction by 1.5 (guess, the sailors' rule of thumb in numbers). Wind-driven surface drift 3% of U10. Surge S = 0.01 (1013 - p) + k U10^2 cos(theta) x sum over the fetch's depth profile of (dF_i / h_i), with k = C_d rho_a / (rho_w g) = 2.5e-7 s2/m (C_d 2.0e-3 at gale strength, 1.3), F in metres and h in metres from the sector's depth profile in `fetch.json` (an effective depth of 40 m if the level has none). Worked, the 838 case: 26 m/s NW at 970 hPa onto the Frisian coast: inverse barometer 0.43 m; set-up over 400 km at 40 m = 2.5e-7 x 676 x 4e5 / 40 = 1.69 m, plus the last 100 km at 20 m = 0.85 m: 2.5-3.0 m in all, the user's 2-3 m. (The first draft wrote k 2.5e-5, two orders of magnitude out: with F in metres it gives 422 m.) The water level in `LevelWater` becomes base + tide + surge + river stage + flood. River stage and current at Dorestad from the Rhine's monthly discharge (2.3's table) and the year's rain anomaly (the chain's wet winters give a February stage 1-2 m up; the flood slider becomes an output). Seiche: a cosmetic +-3 cm oscillation at the harbour's Merian period (the Dorestad dam field, L 400 m, h 3 m: 2L/sqrt(gh) = 150 s).
- **Ice.** Freezing degree-days FDD accumulate on days with the mean under 0 degrees C (fresh water) or under -2 (sea), and thaw at 3 x the positive degree-days. Fresh water, by how fast it moves: edge ice on ditches and the dam field's dead corners at 5 FDD; the slack basins and the dam field (still water) closed at 30-60 FDD, thickness 2 sqrt(FDD) cm there (the 1997 Elfstedentocht's 15 cm at about 60 FDD is still-canal ice); the Kromme Rijn's main channel (flow 0.5-1 m/s) does not close until 150+ FDD (guess, from the record: the Dutch rivers froze across in 1947 and 1963, Hellmann 342 and 337, and the chronicle's carts crossed the Rhine in 821/822; 60-80 FDD closes canals, not a flowing channel; the first draft's 60-80 would have shut the harbour in a third of modern winters). Between those states, from about 80 FDD and again at the thaw, the channel runs **drift ice**: floes breaking off the basins and the banks and running on the current, the real hazard to moored hulls (a sound, a decal field moving at the current, hull damage in the storm plan's terms). Of the 119 winters in KNMI's Hellmann list (1902-2020) twelve reached 150 (`https://www.knmi.nl/nederland-nu/klimatologie/lijsten/hellmann`: 1917, 1929, 1940, 1941, 1942, 1947, 1956, 1963, 1979, 1985, 1987, 1996): one in ten; with the 0.8 degree cooling of 793-900 about one in six to eight (guess), and the forced 821/822 and 939-942 on top. Wadden: grease ice on the flats at 20 sea-FDD, the channels blocked at 80-100 (the winters of 1947, 1963 and 1996 reach this; the chronicle's 821/822 and 939-942 do). The ice state feeds `HdrpSea` (which already stills the bands with `weather.Ice`), the shore (ice foot, pancake ice as decals), the ship (closed rivers and harbours: a prompt and a wait, as `TideWait` does), the sound (creaking, booming at night).

### 2.5 From the model to HDRP

**Water System** (`https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@17.6/api/UnityEngine.Rendering.HighDefinition.WaterSurface.html`, `.../manual/settings-and-properties-related-to-the-water-system.html`, `.../manual/water-water-system-simulation.html`). Ocean surfaces have two swell bands and one ripple band; `largeWindSpeed` (km/h, 0-250) sets the swell's amplitude and shape "indirectly, non-linearly"; `largeOrientationValue` (degrees anticlockwise from +x) steers it when `largeChaos` (0-1) is under 1; `largeCurrentSpeedValue` (km/h) translates the swell; `largeBand0Multiplier` / `largeBand1Multiplier` (0-1) scale the two swell bands; `repetitionSize` (250-5,000 m) sets the patch and so the largest wave the simulation can hold; `ripplesWindSpeed` (km/h, 0-15), `ripplesChaos`, `ripplesOrientationValue`, `ripplesMotionMode` for the wind sea's short end; `timeMultiplier` (0-10); `simulationFoamAmount` (0-1) and `simulationFoamWindCurve` (normalised distant wind to foam); `foamPersistenceMultiplier`; `maximumHeightOverride` (0 up: the height the system assumes for its bounds); `scriptInteractions` and the CPU simulation for queries. The spectrum HDRP uses is not documented; so the one real link between the model and the picture must be **measured**: an editor tool (`Kapman > Water > Measure sea`) sets `repetitionSize` and `largeWindSpeed` on a grid, with each band multiplier at 0 and 1 in turn, samples the CPU simulation over 2,000 points for 60 s and records Hs (4 x the standard deviation of height) and Tp (zero-crossings) per band, writing `data/water/hdrp_sea_calibration.json`, once per `repetitionSize` the levels use (ocean 1,500-2,000 m, river 250-500 m). The runtime then **fits**, it does not invert: HDRP gives one wind knob that moves Hs and Tp together, two amplitude-only multipliers (0-1; they cannot shorten a wave) and a `repetitionSize` fixed at load that bounds the longest wave, so a steep young sea (Hs 3 m, Tp 6 s) and a long swell (Hs 2 m, Tp 12 s) cannot both be hit from (largeWindSpeed, band0, band1). The fit is 2-D with a stated residual: choose `largeWindSpeed` for the wind sea's Tp as closely as the table allows, then the band multipliers for Hs exactly; log the Tp error in `DevHud`; and any swell train that differs from the wind sea by over 60 degrees in direction or over 3 s in Tp is **not** fed to the bands at all but to the Gerstner deformer (next table), which has exact Hs, Tp and direction. The old path, feeding the wind in km/h as `HdrpSea` does today, stays as the fallback when no calibration file exists. What HDRP cannot do, and what stands in for it:

| Need | HDRP | Stand-in |
|---|---|---|
| Swell from a different direction than the wind sea | both swell bands share one orientation | band 0 = swell (the model's largest train: orientation, long, low chaos), band 1 = wind sea's long end (chaos 0.6-0.9, its own amplitude); when swell and wind sea differ by over 60 degrees, a Texture deformer fed by a compute shader adds our own 4-8 Gerstner swell components (allowed: home-made code, not assets) |
| Steepness / wind-against-tide | not a parameter | ripples wind up, `largeChaos` up, foam amount up by the model's steepness factor |
| Breaking waves on the shore | "Breaking waves on shorelines" is a stated non-feature | Shore Wave deformer + foam decals driven by the model's breaker height 0.78 h and type (spilling on sand, plunging on shingle); surf line placed by depth |
| Depth-limited heights | the surface is one flat sim | the model caps Hs by the level's depth; band multipliers follow |
| Spindrift, blown spray, spray haze | none | VFX Graph reading the water's height/normal textures (plan S4) from force 8; the spray haze is a visibility term |
| Foam streaks along the wind | foam is isotropic | the foam wind curve plus a streak decal texture oriented downwind (plan S4) |
| Current speed varying over a surface | constant speed per surface | per-level current decals (already used on the river); the tidal stream changes the whole surface's `largeCurrentSpeedValue` |
| Sea ice | none | the existing band stilling, plus an ice shader decal field (plan) |

Repetition size: 1,500-2,000 m once at load on ocean levels (the plan's S4), 250-500 m on rivers. The user's 2 Oct choice ("about half real height at real length" for storm seas on screen) stands until answered in section 6.

**Fog and haze.** `fog.meanFreePath = MOR / 3.912`; the model's MOR replaces `FogDensity` (keep the property as 3.912/MOR for the passes). Height: radiation fog 30-120 m deep (`maximumHeight` = water level + depth); advection fog 200-500 m; haze 1,000-2,500 m with the mixed layer. `HdrpWeatherDraw.MeanFreePath` clamps at 40 m (line 198) and its 3 October notes record that dense fog rendered black in batch shots and that a 25 m mean free path "made a black wall at dawn" (line 278): the clamp is a workaround for an open rendering bug, not a design choice, so it **stays at 40 m** (MOR 156 m: fog, not thick fog) until the black-fog cause is found by the lighting document's diagnostics (`Shots.DiagFog`, the sky-cubemap test in `lighting_and_atmosphere.md` section 2); only then does it drop to 10 m (thick fog at 50 m MOR is 13 m). That order is in the build plan (4.1). The local mist volume (switched off on 3 Oct because it rendered black in batch shots) is the right tool for the river mist and the fog banks once that is understood; until then the global fog carries both, with the bank field modulating MOR as the ship moves. Fog colour: `SkyColor` mode as now; albedo 0.95; in a storm the tint darkens as now.

**Clouds.** HDRP's volumetric clouds are one layer: the cloud map's R is coverage, G rain, B the type along the LUT's horizontal axis; the LUT's R is the density profile by height, G erosion, B ambient occlusion (`https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.high-definition@17.6/manual/volumetric-clouds-volume-override-reference.html`). `HdrpWeatherDraw` already builds both. The model gives it: the lowest significant layer's type, base and depth (`cloudBase`, `cloudDepth` become per-frame outputs: cumulus 600-1,500 m base and 1,000-2,500 m depth in summer afternoons, stratus 200-400 m base and 300-600 m depth, nimbostratus 400-800 m base and 3,000-5,000 m depth, cumulonimbus 800 m base and 8,000-10,000 m depth with the tower in the LUT), cover and rain by map cell from the shower cells and the front's rain band (so rain curtains hang under the right clouds), the patch scale (showers 2-5 km, fronts 50-200 km bands). The middle and high layers go on HDRP's 2D **Cloud Layer** override (two cheap textured layers with their own opacity, wind and shadows): altostratus and cirrus veils ahead of a warm front, mackerel sky, the thin alto that haloes the sun. Cloud shadows stay on; `shadowOpacity` follows the low layer's optical depth.

**Light.** This document owns T_cloud, the transmittance of the cloud column that scales the Sun's lux; `LevelClock.overcastLight` (fixed 0.45, line 55; `lighting_and_atmosphere.md` section 2 line 121 still writes "T_cloud is 1 clear, 0.45 at full overcast, 0.1 in a storm deck") becomes an **input** that `LevelWeather` sets each frame from `WeatherSample.cloudTransmittance`, and the lighting document should reference this table rather than carry its own value, so that whichever is built second does not overwrite the first. No formula: the first draft's "exp(-0.1 tau)" was not a physical law (Beer's law exp(-tau) is the direct beam, which is zero under anything thicker than cirrus; what reaches the ground under a deck is the diffuse transmittance, which a two-stream estimate puts near 1 / (1 + 0.75 tau (1 - g)) with g about 0.85, i.e. 7% at tau 120, above every measured overcast reading). The Sun's lux is driven by a transmittance table by cloud type, blended by cover, that reproduces the cited illuminance table (`https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daylight`: overcast midday 1,000-2,000 lux, very dark storm overcast under 200, against 100,000 clear); tau is kept only for the cloud draw:

| Cloud type | tau (draw) | T_cloud where it covers | Noon ground lux (clear 100,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| clear | 0 | 1.0 | 100,000 |
| cirrus, cirrostratus | 0.1-2 | 0.8-0.9 | 80,000-90,000 |
| altostratus | 5-20 | 0.2-0.5 | 20,000-50,000 |
| stratocumulus, fair cumulus (where it covers) | 10-30 | 0.1-0.2 | 10,000-20,000 |
| stratus | 15-40 | 0.03-0.08 | 3,000-8,000 |
| nimbostratus | 50-200 | 0.01-0.025 | 1,000-2,500 |
| cumulonimbus (under the core) | 100-500 | 0.002-0.005 | 200-500 |

T_cloud = sum over layers of cover_i x T_i + (1 - cover) x 1.0 for the lowest significant layer, multiplied by the thin veils above (cirrus 0.85 at full cover); the two-stream value is the ceiling the table never exceeds. The storm's 1-1.5 stops darker the user asked for comes out of the table rather than a dimmer. HDRP's sky still lights the sky's own colour; the Sun's lux is dimmed by T_cloud; the ambient probe dimmer on the clouds follows the deck. Exposure limits already come down at dusk (`SkyDome`); under a storm deck at noon the metered EV falls by itself. Note for the ship document: `Design/ship_physics.md` (lines 5, 78, 98, 186, 287, 297) cites a `swell_storm_seas.md` for the sea-state model; it means this document, and the file name there should be corrected.

## 3. In the game

What the player sees: a sky and a sea that are never "a kind", only a state going somewhere. A long low swell under a quiet grey morning means a storm is out there; mares' tails thickening into a veil, the Sun with a ring, the wind backing to the south and freshening, mean rain by evening (a crewman says "it's going to blow"); a hard line of dark cloud with a bright edge from the west means the cold front and a sudden veer with squalls, then clearing, cold, sharp air and a lumpy sea; spring mornings on the Wadden start in fog that burns off by nine; the afternoon sea breeze fills in from the north-west on fair summer days and dies at dusk; frosty high-pressure weeks in January glaze the ditches and the dam field, send drift ice down the Kromme Rijn, and in the hardest winters close its channel so the harbour waits; a NW gale at springs lifts the sea over the terps. The word "glass" (the barometer, 1643) stays in debug only; the crew speak of the sky, the swell and the birds, and no game text names an instrument.

| The cold sharp air behind the front, far horizons | MOR 20-40 km: the far coast appears; the sky's blue deepens | `Visibility` to `fog.meanFreePath` |
| Frost and rime on rigging and reeds | the snow/frost pass with the rime term, windward side first | `SnowFrostPass` from `temperature`, `fog`, `windFrom` |
| Drift ice running on the river | floe decals moving at the current, a creaking and knocking at the hull | `IceModel` state "drift" |

**The lore loop.** Reading the sky is a skill the player builds, not a readout, and it has a payoff beyond surviving: (1) each sign above has a one-line reading the crew's seaman gives the first time it appears on a voyage ("that swell's from the north-west, and there's no wind from there: something's coming"), with a reliability from his `seamanship` stat (a wrong reading now and then, from a poor seaman, is part of the game); (2) each sign he has explained unlocks that line as a council entry the player can ask for again ("what do you make of the sky?"), so a new crew from another port knows other signs (the Frisian knows the Wadden fog, the Northumbrian the haar); (3) the voyage log records, at departure and every watch, the sky in the seaman's words and the sea met, so a player can read back "left Dorestad under mares' tails, gale by the second night" and learn by replay; (4) port talk (`npc_text_encounters.md`) carries yesterday's wind and the fishermen's reading of tomorrow's, which is this engine's sample at that port, true or a little wrong by the speaker's skill. What the player earns: a voyage made in a good window is faster and the cargo arrives dry (the economy document's spoilage); a storm read a day early is a day in port, not a wreck; and the reputation document's "a careful master" grows from logged windows kept.

What the player does with it:

- **Weather windows.** Every voyage is a bet on the next day or two read from the sky. The chart mode shows the lookout's sky and the swell direction, not a forecast. The crew council's seaman (CK3-style) gives his reading with a reliability from his `seamanship`: "wind's gone round to the south, I'd stay another day". The 2 Oct decision stands: natural signs only, no pressure readout outside debug.
- **Choices in weather.** The storm stage of `Plans/storms.md` S6 (shorten sail, run, lie to, make for shelter, beach, jettison, bail) is fed by this model's real sea (Hs, Tp, steepness, H_max, wind against tide, lee shore distance) and the ship's limits; the sea the player sees is the same state scaled for the look.
- **Fog.** Visibility is one number: in fog the lookout's range falls, soundings and the lead line matter, bells and surf are heard before anything is seen, raiders and traders appear late; the AI fleets read the same MOR.
- **Tide and wind together.** Leaving a river mouth or the Thames against wind-over-tide is a short steep sea the ship hates; the voyage planner already waits for the tide; it should now also show "wind against the ebb".
- **Seasons and years.** Ice closes the dam field and the slack basins at Dorestad in about one winter in three, and the river channel itself, with the harbour shut, in about one winter in six to eight early in the game (guess, from the Hellmann record in 2.4), rarer as the MCA warms, forced in 821/822 and 939-942, with drift ice on the channel in the weeks either side; the 939-940 haze dims the sun for months; floods raise the river over the dam field in wet winters; the economy document's prices move with late ships and lost cargo (storm losses are news; see `economy_and_trade.md`).
- **The chronicle's storms** arrive on their days in every game, with the swell and the sky two days ahead, so a player who reads the signs makes port and one who does not meets the sea that drowned 2,437 Frisians. Replay: the same century, a different seed, different ordinary weather; the great storms the same.
- **Fun.** The weather is the game's main source of variance between voyages that is neither random nor unfair: it is readable, it builds over hours, and it rewards attention and the crew's knowledge. The user's "frosty mornings on a calm open ocean" and "stormy sea with the boat trying its best" are not two kinds but two ends of one state, and every day sits somewhere between them.

Sound and the Build mode are other documents' (`audio_design.md`; the dressing is the user's); this model gives them wind force and direction, rain rate, thunder distance, fog, surf height, ice state as numbers.

### 4.3 Tests (EditMode, headless, `Kapman.Tests.EditMode`)

1. `NormalsReproduced`: run 30 years at the coast class. Night 1 (loose): monthly Beaufort shares of hourly wind within 8 points of Table B; Hoek gale days 5-20 a year. ### 4.4 Performance (RTX 4070, 1440p, 60 Hz)

The generator is a few microseconds a frame on the CPU; the sea state a few more; nothing allocates. The GPU side starts from the budget `Research/hdrp_realism_recipe.md` section 9 already totals for the base scene at 1440p (all estimates): base HDRP deferred + TAA + terrain + 150k trees 6-8 ms, sun PCSS 1.5-2, SSAO 0.7, volumetric fog Medium 1.2, volumetric clouds with shadows 2, water 2, night fires 2, post 0.8, APV 0.3: **15-18 ms**, which the recipe says needs DLSS Quality or STP to land at 60. This document adds: the Gerstner swell deformer compute 0.3 ms, the 2D Cloud Layer 0.2, spindrift VFX 0.5 in gales only, foam and streak decals 0.3, the CPU water simulation for the ship's probes already counted: **about 1.3 ms more, 16-19 ms before upscaling**. So the stated requirement is **DLSS Quality (or STP) at 1440p**, which brings the shaded resolution to 1707x960 and the frame to an estimated 11-13 ms; a native 1440p/60 is not promised. The ordered cut list when a scene misses 16.7 ms with DLSS on: cloud shadows off (-0.5), PCSS to PCF Medium (-0.8), fog Medium to Low (-0.5), water simulation 256 to 128 (-0.7), spindrift off (-0.5), the Cloud Layer off (-0.2). None of these numbers is measured: the second slice adds `Kapman > Weather > Profile`, an editor step that loads the three named scenes of 2.5 (frosty dawn, sleet day, gale at dusk) in batch, records the GPU timings per pass with `FrameTimingManager` and the HDRP debug counters, and writes `data/weather/profile_<date>.json`, so the cut list is applied from measurement.
