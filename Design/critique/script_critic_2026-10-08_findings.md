# The script critics' full findings, 8 October 2026

The two readers behind Design/critique/script_critic_2026-10-08.md, as they reported.

## The opening and the cards of the home waters

**What was read.** WHAT I READ. context.md, opening.md and first_cards.md, each whole (first_cards.md is 2,494 lines and 295 cards: 112 crew, 120 harbour, 63 sea). The first two files and lines 1 to 533 of the cards came through the Read tool, which stops at a page limit. The rest of the same file I printed in line ranges from the shell, read-only. Nothing was searched, edited or written, and no other file was opened (ravn_rural_trial.md was not on my list). Counts are hand counts from one reading, marked as counted or estimated; the true numbers are higher. The opening file has no keys, so I cite it by section and speaker.

LIMITS ON THIS CRITIQUE.
1. The cards file says each choice is followed by an outcome paragraph ('-> ...'). There is not one in the file. I have seen no result text, so I cannot judge whether a player knows what just happened.
2. 62 of the 120 harbour cards, from harbour.gate_bench.traiectum to harbour.counts_post.emuthon, have a scene and choices and no spoken line at all.
3. 43 of the 112 crew cards are the evening song. So only about 190 of the 295 cards have dialogue of their own.
4. Many cards hold alternate lines for different years or states (crew.cargo.swords.steersman beside crew.cargo.swords.steersman_864) with nothing to show which is used when. Where a contradiction or wrong date may be gated in the data, I say so.

THE OPENING, IN ORDER.
The questions. Five lines, and they are the weakest words the lender has: 'From where do you hail?' and 'And by what do you swear?' are stage-medieval, three of the five begin 'And', and the man who 'counts while he talks' does not count once while asking.
The seven paragraphs. These work. In about 150 words the player learns the debt, the day, the boat, the cargo and the threat, and meets a man. 'The one that leans.' and 'two men and a rope' do more than any explanation could. Three faults. The counting is only at the two ends ('Ten. Eleven. A dozen.' in the second paragraph, 'Thirteen... no. Twelve.' in the seventh) and nothing says what he is counting, so the quirk lives in the stage note more than in the speech. 'Wokerer' is never glossed on screen. And he shares the word 'mind' with Ravn ('Counted, mind, not weighed').
The boat shown. 'She floats. He has watched her do it.' repeats the lender's best joke half a minute after he made it. Ravn then makes it a third time ('She floats! That is the good news.').
Ravn. Eight paragraphs and about 290 words, twice the lender's length. The first two are a man. Paragraphs three to five each give a task and one small confession ('you have not seen my sewing', 'I can hardly row one'); those confessions are his whole character and they are four to six words each. Paragraph six is the manual: twelve button names, 'the clock', and 'Space stops it', from a Northman of 793, against the maker's own rule. Paragraph eight repeats the tasks, and then the screen lists them again, so the player gets the same five jobs three times. The caption under his name ('Talks a great deal and means well') tells the player what to think before he speaks, and it is not true on the page: he never says one thing that is not needed. He says prices as '12 d', '4 d', '9 d', which nobody can say aloud. He says of the lender 'He does not say much, that one' just after the player has clicked through seven paragraphs of him. From the last question to the first thing the player may do there are fifteen paragraphs in a row.
The first landing. The log line is plain and good. Ravn's 'That is the whole of the trade, you know' explains the game, though 'I forget who told me. Sell before you eat.' nearly earns it. 'And dry. Mostly dry.' is the 'most of the good news' joke again.
Keep: the lender's paragraphs three to six; Ravn's confessions; 'He says mine. He says that of everything.'; 'I forget things. That is how I know where it is kept.'

WHERE THE MAKER'S RULES THEMSELVES HURT.
Plain words: obeyed, and it did not cure the machine sound, because the cleverness moved out of the vocabulary and into the shape of the sentence. The rule needs a twin: plain sentences.
Nobody shortens a word: it suits the lender, the bishop and the prior. It hurts Ravn, the farm boy and the drunk skipper, who have no way left to sound loose. If the rule stays, looseness has to come from run-on sentences and broken ones, and the script has almost none. The quoted verse breaks the rule anyway ('o'er' in crew.riddle.wind.dane, 'call'd' in crew.riddle.anchor.lookout).
One paragraph at a time: obeyed per screen and defeated by number (fifteen in a row in the opening; scene lines of 50 to 65 words in the late harbour cards, such as harbour.guests_gift.walichrum).
No hint lines: broken by Ravn's button paragraph, by the teller doing the pay sum (crew.pay.reckoning.tally), and by the '(sure)', '(likely)', '(doubtful)' on every sea choice.
No colons or brackets in narration: broken in teller lines (crew.silver.three_kinds.fact, crew.news.godfred.fact, crew.riddle.dew.fact, crew.faith.lent_meat.law), in most scene lines of the crew file, and in nearly every choice.

**Verdict.** Line by line this is competent, well researched and often funny, and it reads fast. The trouble is that it is all funny in the same way: nearly every speaker says three or four short sentences and closes on a dry remark, so after twenty cards the reader stops hearing people and hears one writer. That is the one thing most wrong with it: one voice in forty coats. The lender's clipped manner and his counting, which were meant to be his alone, are also the moneyer's, the widow's, the toll man's, the prior's and the ale-wives'; the words were made plain as ordered, but the cleverness moved into the shape of the sentence, and that shape is what sounds like a machine. The opening is the best-built part, and the lender is a man in 150 words; then it spends its best joke three times in two minutes, and Ravn, at twice the lender's length, stops being a man and reads out the screen, 'Space stops it' included. The crew cards state the situation in the scene line and then have someone say it again, and the sea cards have the steersman give the right answer, and a bracket confirm it, before the player chooses. The history is the real strength and should be kept, but it works only where it presses on someone in the boat; elsewhere it is a footnote in a sailor's mouth. The most alive lines are the few where somebody is not clever at all, only sees a thing or is unsure of it, and there are perhaps twenty of those in six hundred. The script also disagrees with itself about who is owed the 1920 pennies, a moneylender in the opening and a count in the cards, and a player will meet that inside the first hour.

**Scores (1 to 10).** voices 4, clarity 5, variety 3, sense of the time 7, economy 5

### Habits

**The dry last word.** A speaker says the thing, stops, and adds a flat sentence of two to six words that undercuts it or shows him unimpressed. *How often:* Counted: at least 60 lines end this way. Estimate: one spoken line in four in the harbour cards, one in five at sea. The opening does it three times ('I have watched her do it', 'That is most of the good news', 'Mostly dry').

- `tollman.memory_honest`: You hid nothing last time. I looked.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.counts_court.shoes`: Last time your helpers looked at their shoes. I remember shoes.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.vecht_crossing.mud`: You sat on my mud a whole tide last time. I waved. It seemed polite.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.lent_table.kept`: You kept the fast with us last year, and your men did not die of it. I had wondered.
- `rhine_frisia.daventre.host_buyer.buys`: I pay the count's price, on a stick, and the stick is paid at Michaelmas. Usually.
- `sea.gale.breaks.say.worse / sea.water.foul.say.worse`: I have been in worse. I did not enjoy that either. / I have drunk worse. Not on purpose.

*Why it hurts:* It is one joke. A toll man, a steward, a ferryman, a guest-master, a king's buyer and a Dane in a gale all have the same sense of humour, and it is a modern English one: understatement. After a dozen the player sees the full stop coming and hears the writer waiting for the laugh. It also means nobody in this world is ever simply angry, afraid, tired or dull.

*Rule for the rewrite:* One dry last word per port, and it belongs to one person. Everyone else stops when the thing is said.

**One breath for everybody.** Every speaker talks in runs of three to five short sentences of four to twelve words, with a full stop where a talker would run on. Often the first is a one-word or two-word scrap ('You. Good.', 'Wax.', 'Sit.'). *How often:* Estimate: nine spoken lines in ten outside the song. I found no line where someone runs on, and only three where someone breaks off or gropes (crew.cargo.book.lookout_2, sea.surf.heard.say.where, sea.land.wrong.say.roof).

- `opening 1, the lender`: The boat. End of the bank. The one that leans.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.kings_mint.greet`: Put it on the pan. All of it. I do not talk to silver in a purse.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.widows_store.again`: You. Good. Your corner is as you left it, and the mice have been spoken to.
- `rhine_frisia.daventre.heath_iron.hands`: Show me your hands. Rope and oar. Good. I do not sell to men with soft hands; they come back to complain.
- `rhine_frisia.wijnaldum.sicco_of_the_flats.vlie`: The Vlie is a road with no edges. I know where the edges are today. Ask me again tomorrow.
- `rhine_frisia.walichrum.wiltruds_house.offa`: Offa is dead. The English king. I had it from a man who had it from a monk. The ports will open by the next moon, and they will be begging for black stones on the Strand.

*Why it hurts:* With plain words and no shortened words, rhythm is the only tool left for telling people apart, and the script gives everyone the same rhythm. The lender's scraps, the one mannerism the maker asked for, are not his: the moneyer, the widow and the smith have them too. The talkers are worse served. The scene says Sicco is 'talking before he is in earshot' and that the skipper at Wiltrud's is telling it 'for the third time, louder each time'; both then speak as tidily as a clerk. Ravn 'talks a great deal' only in his caption.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Choose each speaker's breath before his words: the lender in scraps, Ravn in one long sentence that loses its end, the mate in one sentence and no more. Nobody else may borrow it.

**Not this. That..** The speaker first denies something nobody claimed, then states the point. *How often:* Counted: 26, from at least 15 different speakers.

- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.saint_martins_plate.order`: The plate is shown to those who give. That is not a price. It is an order of events.
- `sea.quarrel.oar.say.mine`: The oar is mine while I am aboard. That is not pride. That is how boats stay the right way up.
- `rhine_frisia.daventre.hathugards_question.promised`: You said you would ask along the Rhine. I do not ask what you heard. I ask whether you asked.
- `rhine_frisia.doccinga.aalsum_cloth.same_buyer`: I do not want a buyer. I want the same buyer, every year at shearing, so that I need not sell to whoever lands.
- `crew.death.kin.mother`: I did not come to shout. I came for what he earned and what he owned, and to hear where you put him.
- `rhine_frisia.wijnaldum.bare_mound.asks`: I do not want it back. I want to know where it went.

*Why it hurts:* This is the most recognisable machine sentence there is. Once it is emphasis. Twenty-six times it is a tic, and the first half is wasted every time: nobody said the mother had come to shout or that the steersman was proud. It also gives a grieving mother, a weaver, a herdsman and a priest the same lawyer's way of putting things.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Say what it is. Never begin by saying what it is not.

**The home-made proverb.** A general truth about 'a man', guests, silver or the sea, in a balanced sentence, usually closing the line. *How often:* Counted: 43, not including the quoted verse. More than 20 begin 'A man'.

- `crew.home.michaelmas.mate`: A man kept against a promise rows like a sack.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.black_haired_merchant.lodging`: A man who lodges guests ends by feeding poets.
- `rhine_frisia.daventre.chest_received.lid`: Lids outlast bishops.
- `rhine_frisia.witla.pilots_shed.head`: A thing that is written down can be sold.
- `sea.fog.horn.say.honest`: A man who blows a horn in fog is honest, or wants to be taken for it.
- `sea.water.thirst.say.fools`: Thirst makes fools first and corpses after. We are at fools.

*Why it hurts:* The Dane is the one man with a right to sayings, and his are real ones from his own people ('Praise ice when you have crossed it', crew.cold.ice.dane). When the mate, the merchant, the priest, the pilot and the steersman coin their own, his voice is lost and the whole coast talks like a book of maxims. A proverb also shuts the subject: the line ends the talk instead of leaving the player something to answer.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Only the Dane speaks in sayings. Anyone else who starts 'A man...' must say 'I' or 'you' and mean this boat, today.

**Speakers who footnote their own speech.** A character tells you that he is saying a thing, how often he will say it, or what he is not saying. *How often:* Counted: 28, from 14 different speakers.

- `crew.death.body.steersman`: That is days off, and the dead do not keep. Now I have said both.
- `crew.council.wind.mate`: I do not say go or stay. I say what is in the sack.
- `crew.theft.caught.mate`: That is all I can say for him, and I have said it.
- `tollman.law_mail`: I say it to every hull, so that none can say he was not told.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.lent_table.pork`: There was a smell of pork on the strand last Lent. I name no boat.
- `sea.fast.nofish.say.saxony`: We are not in Saxony. I only mention it.

*Why it hurts:* In the steersman it could be a true quirk: a fair man who always puts the case against himself ('I say that against myself', crew.pay.wage_or_share.steersman; 'I have said the dear one too, so you can choose', crew.cold.ice.steersman). Spread over the mate, the lookout, the Dane, a skipper, the toll man, a priest, a prior, a guest-master and a farm wife it is the writer hedging, and everybody sounds like a careful witness. The same sentence even passes between two men: 'I only say what the song says.' is the lookout's in crew.bored.calm.lookout and the steersman's in crew.song.18.steersman.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Only the steersman says what he is saying. Everyone else just says it.

**Said in the scene, then said again.** The scene line states the situation, often what someone wants or says, and the first spoken or teller line repeats it, sometimes word for word. The card title often makes a third time. *How often:* Counted: about 50 of the 68 crew cards outside the song, about 20 of the 63 sea cards, about 10 of the 57 harbour cards that have lines. If the scene line is only a designer's note and is never shown, strike this; if it is shown, it is the first thing to cut.

- `crew.pay.promise_due.mate`: SCENE: You promised the men their pay at the next landing. This is the next landing. / mate: You said the next landing. This is a landing.
- `crew.pay.count_or_crew.mate`: SCENE: The count's day is near and the men's day is past. There is silver enough for one of them. / mate: The count's day is close and the men's day is past. There is silver for one of them.
- `crew.sick.cough.fact`: SCENE: The hand has coughed through two watches. The mate does not like the sound of it. / teller: The hand has coughed through two watches. He says it is the salt. It does not sound like salt.
- `crew.faith.white_shirt.dane`: SCENE: ...The Dane says he has been. / dane: I have been baptised. Three times.
- `crew.hunger.wheat.mate`: SCENE: ...The men are watching what you do with it. / mate: The men are watching what you do with the sacks. So am I.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.vecht_hand.short`: SCENE: She counts your crew as they come up the bank, and finds the number short. / alewife: You are a man short. I counted your crew as they came up the bank. I count everybody.

*Why it hurts:* The player reads each situation twice, and the scene line gets there first and takes the life out of the line. The mate's 'This is a landing' and the Dane's 'Three times' are both good jokes that the scene has already told.

*Rule for the rewrite:* The scene line says where we are and what can be seen. It never says what anyone wants, says or feels.

**Label first, lesson second.** In the sea cards the steersman's line opens with a one-word or two-word name for what the scene has just shown, then explains it. *How often:* Counted: 16 lines in the 63 sea cards.

- `sea.fog.bank.say.fog`: Fog. Warm air on a cold sea. It comes like a thief and stays like a kinsman.
- `sea.herring.shoal.say.herring`: Herring. The whole sea has come up to the top.
- `sea.ice.floes.say.rotten`: Spring ice. Too rotten to bear a man, and hard enough to open a plank. The worst of both.
- `sea.frost.dawn.say.ropes`: Frost at sea. The ropes will not run and the sail will crack before it bends. Nothing gets hoisted until it is beaten soft.
- `sea.squall.line.say.squall`: A squall. When it hits, it will turn the wind inside out. Short and nasty.
- `sea.porpoise.roll.say.learned`: Sea-pigs. The learned say they play before a storm.

*Why it hurts:* The scene has shown it, often with the same word (the scene of sea.porpoise.roll begins 'Porpoises:'). The steersman becomes a caption, then gives the lesson, and then the choices carry '(sure)' and '(doubtful)'. The player is told three times and has nothing left to decide. In sea.frost.dawn he says 'Nothing gets hoisted until it is beaten soft' and the choices are 'Beat the ice out ... (sure)' and 'Hoist as she is ... (doubtful)'.

*Rule for the rewrite:* The steersman never names what is on the screen. He says what he fears or what he would do, and he may be wrong.

**Everybody counts.** Counting, tallies, notches and sticks are given to nearly every character, and 'count' is also the title of the nobleman in the story. *How often:* Counted: the verb or noun about 55 times, used of at least 16 different people, besides some 30 uses of 'the count' for the lord. Twelve harbour scene lines have a stranger counting your crew, hull, bales, heads or waterline.

- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.widows_store.greet`: I count when they come in and when they go out. Between the two nobody counts but me.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.vecht_hand.short`: I counted your crew as they came up the bank. I count everybody.
- `rhine_frisia.emuthon.priest_in_the_sheep_house.bowls`: You counted. Most men do not count bowls.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.priors_store.hunger`: They are at my gate every morning, and I have counted the sacks twice.
- `crew.winter.spring.fact`: The mate has counted them twice, as if that might help.
- `crew.pay.reckoning.mate_debt`: They know about the count. They say the count has a hall, and they have an oar each.

*Why it hurts:* The maker gave the lender one quirk, that he counts. The script then gave it to the widow, two ale-wives, the prior, the mate, the toll man, the Dane, the clerks and the hosts, so it marks nobody. And because the lender says 'I count it then', a player who reads 'the count's day' takes it for the day of reckoning, not a lord's day.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Only the lender counts aloud. Other people weigh, look, sniff, feel the cloth; and the lord is never called 'the count' in the same card as a number.

**The narrator's wry aside.** The teller and the scene lines end on an arch remark or a knowing comparison. *How often:* Estimate: a third of the scene lines and half of the teller lines. I marked about 35. Five scene lines use the frame 'as a man looks at...', three of them with a horse (harbour.priest_for_the_ems.traiectum, harbour.saints_tenth.medemelacha, harbour.across_one_cloth.daventre).

- `crew.bored.calm.fact`: The sea is so flat that the boat's own reflection is tiresome.
- `crew.winter.haul_out.fact`: She comes up the strand on rollers with eight men on the rope, and sits there looking twice the size and half as clever.
- `harbour.thiadbods_dam.dorestad (scene)`: He is glad to see you. He is glad to see everyone.
- `harbour.tan_pits.walichrum (scene)`: The smell arrived before you did.
- `harbour.menders_bank.walichrum (scene)`: A man splits an oak log with wedges, slowly, as if it were the last oak log in the world. On this island it nearly is.
- `harbour.grave_at_de_heul.dorestad (scene)`: an old skipper of the dams, dead in his bed, which the bank finds remarkable.

*Why it hurts:* The teller has the same wit as the people, so there is no plain ground for them to stand out against. The teller is also the best eye in the script ('a cheese the size of a helmet', 'taking wagers in eggs'), and the smirk spends that eye on jokes.

*Rule for the rewrite:* The teller sees and does not smile. A narration ends on a thing, not on a remark about it.

**Three things, the third one the joke.** Lists come in threes, and the third item is the turn. *How often:* Counted: at least 25 lists of exactly three. Lists of two or of four are rare (crew.death.kin.mate has four).

- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.vecht_hand.him`: He poles, he can splice, and he owes me for the winter.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.our_own_fire.tiel`: My cousin came up the river with a scale, a wife and nothing else.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.priors_store.greet`: I keep the gate, the store and the porridge.
- `crew.hire.second_son.fact`: Big hands, bare feet, and a bundle that is mostly cheese.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.breaking_beach.boy`: He can bail, splice and keep quiet. Two of those are rare.
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.school_chest.worth`: Forty calves, a year of a man's eyes, and one wet night undoes it.

*Why it hurts:* Three is the length a list takes when nobody has chosen its length, and the turned third item is the dry last word again in another place. Read together they tick like a clock.

*Rule for the rewrite:* A list is two things or five. If the third thing is a joke, cut the list and keep the joke.

**Met before they greet you.** A stranger in harbour is introduced by what he does before he speaks to you, or before your rope is fast. *How often:* Counted: 19 of the 120 harbour scene lines are built on 'before'.

- `harbour.heath_iron.daventre (scene)`: He looks at your hands before your face.
- `harbour.kings_cellar.noviomagus (scene)`: Its keeper lifts a sack to feel the weight before he greets you.
- `harbour.chest_received.daventre (scene)`: they count your crew before they count the chest.
- `harbour.who_crossed.daventre (scene)`: He finishes the notch before he looks up.
- `harbour.weaving_huts.wijnaldum (scene)`: The widow who owns the looms looks at your cloak before she looks at you, and is sorry for it.
- `harbour.herimans_die.emuthon (scene)`: names your forefathers before she names a price.

*Why it hurts:* It is a good way to meet one person. As the way to meet one in six it tells the player that every port keeps the same shrewd watcher at the landing, and the ports run together.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Open on what the person is doing with their hands, never on the order in which they notice you.

**The footnote in the mouth.** A law, a book or a dated event is recited with its clause and figures by someone aboard, or by the teller as an aside. *How often:* Counted: 26 lines; 13 of them are the steersman's in the sea cards.

- `crew.hunger.shore.lookout`: By the West Saxons' law a stranger who leaves the road and neither shouts nor blows a horn is taken for a thief, to be killed or ransomed.
- `crew.fight.blows.steersman`: By our law a grip on the hair is two shillings and a ducking is four, at three pennies to the shilling.
- `sea.shoal.lead.say.paul`: The ship that carried Paul to Rome sounded and found twenty fathoms, and then fifteen. Then they dropped four anchors and prayed for daylight.
- `sea.night.light.say.bede`: Bede the Englishman wrote that at midsummer a watcher at midnight cannot tell evening's end from morning's beginning. He lived at Jarrow.
- `sea.whale.blow.say.ohthere`: A Northman told King Alfred of whales fifty ells long, and that six men killed sixty sea-beasts in two days. The king had it written down.
- `crew.pay.wage_or_share.fellowship`: Men who put their goods in one keel and divide the gain have a name for it now. The Danes cut it on their memorial stones: he owned a ship with such a man.

*Why it hurts:* The research is right and the speaker is wrong. A Frisian boatman gives Bede's words, the soundings from the Acts and the figures of a report made to an English king. The good version exists in the same file: crew.death.body.law ends 'It does not say what a boat is to do.' and crew.faith.lent_meat.law ends 'The mate has stopped chewing.' There the fact lands on someone in the boat.

*Rule for the rewrite:* A fact comes in only as something a man aboard heard, fears or is owed. No figures, and the line must end on this boat.

**Everyone has a riddle, and the card answers it.** Riddles are posed by a dozen different people, some of them twice over, and the title and scene line usually name the answer. *How often:* Counted: 18 riddle cards from 12 different askers (Ravn, the Dane, the lookout, a schoolmaster, two clerks, a salter, a scholar in an ale-house, a tanner, a boat mender, a Norse and an English shipmaster). The anchor is asked three times and the calm twice. Six of the crew's seven riddles are solved by their own title or scene.

- `crew.riddle.calm.dane_2`: That is five names. Men have a sixth, a short one. You are sitting in it.
- `crew.riddle.hail (scene)`: A squall has gone through and left hail in the bilge. The Dane has a riddle for that too.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.aldswiths_bench.anchor`: two iron points joined in one. I fight the wind and the deep water. I search the middle of the stream, and I bite the land itself.
- `sea.anchor.english.say.riddle`: I have a thing aboard that fights wind and wave together, and wins by lying still. So have you.
- `crew.riddle.anchor.lookout`: One begins: 'Oft with the wave I must contend, and against the wind fight.' It ends: 'Say what I am call'd.'
- `sea.anchor.english.say.english`: The English and their riddles. They would ask a drowning man what goes on one foot and has many ribs.

*Why it hurts:* A riddle from one man is character. From a salter, a tanner and two passing shipmasters it is the game's machinery showing, and the steersman's joke about 'the English and their riddles' admits it. A riddle whose card is titled 'A riddle at anchor' and begins 'The boat lies to her anchor' is not a riddle.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Riddles belong to Ravn and to people who can write. A riddle card never shows or names its answer anywhere on it.

### Best

- `opening 1, the lender, third paragraph`: The boat. End of the bank. The one that leans. Six oars, a mast, a sail of sorts. She floats. I have watched her do it.  
  *Voice, joke and everything the player needs to know, in 25 words. 'The one that leans' tells you what he has sold you without his saying so. This is what the lender should sound like throughout.*
- `opening 1, the lender, fifth paragraph`: Michaelmas. I count it then. Not the day after. The day after I bring two men and a rope, and I do not stay to hear why.  
  *The deadline and the price of missing it, with no explaining. The rope does the work a rules screen would do.*
- `opening 3, Ravn, fifth paragraph`: The wine in her is yours to sell, mind. He says mine. He says that of everything. You owe him for it all the same, so sell it dear.  
  *Ravn is useful and himself at once. He gives the player a fact, takes a side, and stays honest about the debt. The other seven paragraphs should work like this one.*
- `crew.tongue.letters.mate`: A man's name can be written on a list, and the list sent a long way south. I want to know what my own looks like. Then I can look for one that is near it.  
  *A reason to learn to read that only this man has. His whole history is in it and none of it is explained. It leaves the player wondering whose name is near his.*
- `crew.death.body.fact`: He lies along the bottom boards under the spare sail. The boat is seven paces long, and there is nowhere aboard her that is not beside him.  
  *Narration that sees and does not smile. The measurement carries the feeling.*
- `crew.song.17.steersman`: Finn was our king. I know where this goes; I have heard the English tell it. The guests are always the heroes, and the host is the one who is killed in his own hall.  
  *History used as it should be: it matters to a man in the boat. A Frisian hearing the English poem from the losing side is the best idea in the script, and it gives the player a real choice of whose telling to hear.*
- `crew.song.43.fact`: Nobody speaks. The water runs along the strakes. After a time the mate says that was a long way to row for one grave, and that he is glad he came.  
  *Forty-three evenings paid off in three sentences, and reported, not spoken, so the mate keeps his silence. The dry remark is earned here because he follows it with a plain one.*
- `rhine_frisia.daventre.hathugards_question.question`: Before I pour. Have you seen a Saxon of about thirty, with a scar through the left eyebrow from a calf's kick? He was thirteen when they counted him off. He answers to Brunric, or did.  
  *A habit (she asks before she pours) that holds a story, and it pays off four lines later in 'Sit. For once I pour first.' (rhine_frisia.daventre.hathugards_question.found). She wants something; that is why she sounds like no one else.*
- `rhine_frisia.walichrum.side_room.told`: My sister has not left the side room in eleven years. Her face. That is all there is to know.  
  *Two words do it. She breaks off because she cannot say more, not because the writer wants a rhythm.*
- `sea.wreck.survivors (scene)`: They do not wave. They have been waving since yesterday.  
  *The one scene line that turns on a fact the player would not have guessed and that changes how the men look. No remark is added.*
- `sea.fire.water.say.hand`: My hand. Look at my hand.  
  *A person seeing something, with no wit at all. The same is true of 'It has not finished coming up.' (sea.whale.blow.say.coming) and 'There is a roof. A church, or a barn. Something with a roof.' (sea.land.wrong.say.roof). These are the lines that do not sound like a machine.*
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.breaking_beach.boy_speaks`: I can keep quiet.  
  *Four words, set up by his uncle's 'He can bail, splice and keep quiet. Two of those are rare.' The joke is in who speaks and when, not in a turn of phrase.*

### Worst

- `opening 3, Ravn, sixth paragraph`: The buttons up there are yours. The top row is the ship: Cast off, Voyage, Crew, Hold. The middle row is where we are: Trade, the alehouse, the minster, Rest. The bottom row is what you know: Wares, Journey, Errands, the Book. The clock is at the very top: Space stops it, and the numbers hurry it.  
  *Why:* It breaks the maker's rule against hint lines in the plainest way possible. A Northman of 793 names a keyboard key and a clock. It lists twelve labels the player can read for himself. And it is the longest paragraph in the opening, placed just where Ravn had become a person.  
  *Better:* Cut it whole. The green frame and the list 'Before she sails' already do this work. If he must say something here: 'Look about you before we go. I will wait. I am good at waiting.'
- `opening 1, the lender's questions`: From where do you hail? ... And by what do you swear?  
  *Why:* Stage-medieval from a man who a moment later speaks in scraps. The counting, his one quirk, is missing from the only part of the scene where the player is waiting on him.  
  *Better:* 'Where are you from?' 'Name?' 'Who do you pray to?' 'And before this?' Put the count between them: 'Four. Five. Name?'
- `opening 2, the boat shown`: She floats. He has watched her do it.  
  *Why:* The lender's best joke, told again by the screen half a minute later, and Ravn tells it a third time straight after ('She floats! That is the good news.').  
  *Better:* 'Your boat.' and nothing more. Or give one new thing: 'Your boat. She has no name yet.'
- `opening 3, Ravn, third paragraph`: Have it mended before we put out: 12 d and six hours, there under the buttons.  
  *Why:* Nobody can say '12 d' aloud. 'Six hours' is clock time, where the rest of the script reckons by tides and 'the ninth hour'. 'Under the buttons' points at the screen.  
  *Better:* 'Have it mended before we put out. Twelve pennies and half a day. I would sew it myself, but you have not seen my sewing.'
- `crew.pay.reckoning.tally`: A man aboard is owed a penny for every four days, cut as a notch on the tally stick by the mast. Four men and a month make thirty pennies.  
  *Why:* The teller states a rule of the game and does the sum for the player. This is the explaining the maker forbade.  
  *Better:* Cut the teller. Let the mate carry it: 'Thirty pennies. It is cut on the stick. They want it now, before the ale-house does.'
- `crew.pay.count_or_crew.mate`: The count's day is close and the men's day is past. There is silver for one of them. I would sooner the count went short: a count has never rowed you off a lee shore.  
  *Why:* Its first two sentences are the scene line over again. It names a creditor the opening never mentioned. Only the last clause is the mate.  
  *Better:* 'Pay the men. The lender never rowed you off a lee shore.' (once it is settled who is owed).
- `sea.fog.bank.say.fog`: Fog. Warm air on a cold sea. It comes like a thief and stays like a kinsman.  
  *Why:* Three habits in fifteen words: it names what the scene shows, gives a modern account of how fog forms, and closes on a made proverb.  
  *Better:* 'Look hard at the land. In a little while it will be gone.'
- `rhine_frisia.traiectum.saint_martins_plate.order`: The plate is shown to those who give. That is not a price. It is an order of events.  
  *Why:* 'An order of events' is office talk of our own day, and the line is the 'Not this. That.' turn at its barest.  
  *Better:* 'The plate is shown to those who give. Give first.'
- `crew.riddle.wind.dane`: 'Whence comes the wind that fares o'er the waves yet never itself is seen?'  
  *Why:* It is not a riddle, since the answer is in the question. The true answer is a giant in eagle's shape that no player can guess. 'Whence' and 'fares' are not plain, and 'o'er' shortens a word against the rule.  
  *Better:* 'Nobody has seen the wind. So where does it come from? My grandfather said a great bird sits at the end of the sky. Say something better.'
- `crew.silver.three_kinds.dane`: A stamp is a king saying: trust me. I trust the knife. Cut it, bend it, weigh it. Silver does not lie. Kings have been known to.  
  *Why:* Five maxims in a row, and 'have been known to' is club-room English. A real difference between two men is lost under polish.  
  *Better:* 'I do not know your king. I know silver. Cut it and weigh it.'
- `sea.water.thirst.say.fools`: Thirst makes fools first and corpses after. We are at fools.  
  *Why:* The scene says tongues are thick and lips are split. Then a man turns an epigram and tops it. It is the writer talking, at the one moment a character should barely be able to.  
  *Better:* 'We land at the first green we see.'
- `rhine_frisia.wijnaldum.sicco_of_the_flats.vlie`: The Vlie is a road with no edges. I know where the edges are today. Ask me again tomorrow.  
  *Why:* A fair line given to the wrong man. The scene promises someone 'talking before he is in earshot'; he delivers three trimmed sentences like everyone else.  
  *Better:* 'The Vlie moved again last week, nobody knows it but me, there was a bank by the second withy and now it is by the third, are you going north, you should not go north without me.'

### Where a player is lost

- `opening 1, the lender / crew.pay.reckoning.mate_debt / rhine_frisia.dorestad.widows_store.debt`: The opening says the 1920 pennies are owed to Wursing, a moneylender who will come with 'two men and a rope'. The cards say they are owed to a count with a hall and a steward: 'They know about the count. They say the count has a hall, and they have an oar each.' and 'The count's stick hangs in his hall with your name cut on it.' Because the lender's whole manner is counting ('I count it then'), a new player will read 'the count' and 'the count's day' as the reckoning, not as a lord.  
  *Fix:* Decide who holds the stick and call him one thing everywhere. If the count stands behind the lender, the lender must say so in the opening, in one line.
- `opening 3, Ravn, sixth paragraph`: Ravn explains twelve buttons, the clock and the Space key. The player can see the buttons; the maker ruled out exactly this.  
  *Fix:* Cut the paragraph. Let the list 'Before she sails' and the green frame lead.
- `opening 3, Ravn, paragraphs three, four and seven`: Prices are spoken as '12 d', '4 d', '9 d'. Nothing has told the player that d means pennies; the lender said 'pennies'. The sea cards add a third way ('for six pennies' weight of silver').  
  *Fix:* In speech, always 'twelve pennies'. Keep d for the log and the choices only.
- `opening 3, Ravn, eighth paragraph, and the list after it`: The five jobs are given three times: across paragraphs three to seven, again in 'So. Mend the sail, find the men, buy well. Then a course, and cast off.', and again in the list on screen.  
  *Fix:* Keep the on-screen list. End Ravn on 'I forget things. That is how I know where it is kept.' without the recital.
- `opening 3, the caption under Ravn's name`: 'Norse. Talks a great deal and means well' tells the player what to think of him before he speaks, and 'He came with the boat. Nobody told you.' gives away his first line ('I come with the boat. He did not say?').  
  *Fix:* Caption: his name and 'Norse'. Let him do the rest.
- `opening 1 and 3: 'the Wokerer', 'Keep it so.'`: Nothing on screen says Wokerer means usurer, so the line reads as a name with no sting. 'Keep it so.' after 'you have not seen my sewing' is unclear: keep what so?  
  *Fix:* Let the lender sour it himself in a word or drop it. Cut 'Keep it so.'
- `harbour.tollman.frankish (tollman.greet)`: He says the toll is 'a tenth of what you sell here, taken when you sell it. Not before'. Then he asks what you carry and the choices are 'Declare the cargo' and 'Hide the swords under the ballast'. If the toll falls only on sales, why declare? And how do you hide swords with him standing at the boat?  
  *Fix:* Have him say what he is looking for now (forbidden goods) apart from what he takes later (the tenth). Move the hiding to before he comes down.
- `crew.riddle.fog, .dew, .anchor, .hail, .calm; sea.anchor.english`: The title and scene give the answer ('A riddle at anchor', 'The boat lies to her anchor'; 'left hail in the bilge. The Dane has a riddle for that too'). 'Take the riddle' does not say what taking does or where an answer is given. crew.riddle.wind has an answer no player can reach.  
  *Fix:* Retitle each card by the hour or the weather of a different day, strip the answer from the scene, and let the Dane say how he wants his answer ('Tell me when you have it. I will not ask.').
- `crew.song.fitt_02 to crew.song.fitt_42`: The lookout's line tells the whole part before the player chooses 'Listen' or 'Not tonight'. What does Listen give that has not just been read? The same scene sentence opens 40 cards, and three teller lines (crew.song.fog, crew.song.calm, crew.song.blow) are each used 14 times in strict turn.  
  *Fix:* Before the choice, one sentence of what tonight's part is about. The telling comes after 'Listen'. Write the weather line fresh or leave it out.
- `sea cards, the brackets on every choice`: '(sure)', '(likely)', '(doubtful)' are never explained and often mean nothing: 'Say it is only a ring (sure)', 'Shoo it off the cargo (sure)', 'Sail on: they asked for nothing (sure)'. Sure of what? In sea.fog.bank all three choices are '(sure)'. Where they do mean something, the steersman has already given the answer (sea.bar.ebb.say.walls, sea.frost.dawn.say.ropes, sea.overboard.chilled.say.inside).  
  *Fix:* Drop the brackets from choices that are not a gamble. Let the crew disagree about the risky ones, as in crew.council.wind, and let the steersman be wrong sometimes.
- `crew.hunger.shore; sea.wreck.survivors`: The scene sets up a danger (an axe held low, a child running for neighbours) and all three choices begin 'Blow the horn and', so the question the card raises is not asked. In sea.wreck.survivors all three choices are 'Take them off'.  
  *Fix:* Offer the thing the scene is about: walk up without the horn, or leave them the bread and sail. Or cut the build-up and present it as a plain bargain.
- `crew.pay.reckoning (choices three and four)`: 'Open a cask of the cargo's ale tonight; silver at the next sale' and 'Ask them to wait' both put the men off. The difference is not shown.  
  *Fix:* Name the cost in each: the ale is lost cargo, the plain asking is lost goodwill.
- `harbour.gate_bench.traiectum and the 61 cards after it`: There are no spoken lines, yet the choices lean on things nobody has said: 'Lend him the twenty pennies', 'Take what he left with the prior' (who is he?), 'Lie another day in the creek, as the sheep say' (harbour.sheep_on_the_dune.walichrum), 'Tell him what he is standing in' (harbour.tan_pits.walichrum), 'Give him his mother's answer' (harbour.captive_in_the_kings_house.noviomagus). Their scene lines are also the longest in the file, 50 to 65 words.  
  *Fix:* Until lines exist, every choice must make sense from the scene alone. When lines are written, move half of each scene into a mouth.
- `rhine_frisia.dorestad.widows_store.york_known / rhine_frisia.doccinga.knot_of_pennies.the_knot`: 'It holds less than the price.' and 'Let it be the first silver of the price.' The price of what? In the lines shown, nobody ever says what is owed at York or why.  
  *Fix:* One plain sentence from Thiadburg the first time: what was done at York and what it costs to mend.
- `crew.death.churchyard.priest / crew.death.churchyard.no_church`: A priest questions you, and the teller says 'There is no church here and no priest.' These must be alternates, but nothing marks them, and the choices ('Pay for the grave, a mass and bread for the poor') fit only one.  
  *Fix:* Split into two cards or give each alternate its own choices.
- `crew.home.asks.dane`: 'A man is master at home. A pair of goats and a patched roof.' A player who does not know the old saying cannot tell whether the Dane is for the hand going home or against it.  
  *Fix:* Let him take a side: 'Let him go. A man with a roof of his own rows better for it.'
- `names across the harbour cards`: Thiadburg, Thiadbod, Thiadlef and Thiadwih; Liudger and Liudbod; Bernhild, Bernlef and Berntrud; Wiltrud, Wilsuit and Wendilgard; Hathugard, Hartwin, Hamacar, Hadda, Hildeburg and Heriman. True to the time, and impossible to keep apart on a first voyage.

- `all cards`: No outcome text was in the file. A player who chooses and sees only numbers move will not know what happened or why.  
  *Fix:* One plain sentence after each choice, in the teller's voice, saying what was done and what it cost.

## Four story chains and the people of the home ports

**What was read.** All four files, whole and in order: context.md; first_chains.md (four chains, 54 cards, 180 outcome paragraphs); first_people.md (92 people, 744 spoken lines); ravn_rural_trial.md (Ravn's lines before and after). Method note: I read through the shell and not the Read tool, because this session's standing instruction required it, and I ran simple pattern counts over the same four files, so where I say "count" it is a count and where I say "estimate" it is my reading. No other file was opened and none was changed; one scratch file made for a count was deleted.

**Verdict.** It reads far better than placeholder text has a right to: the chains are tight, the history sits inside the scenes instead of on top of them, and the best cards (the widow and the candle, Ravn's last question, the Saxon's "I can row.") could go into a finished game untouched. The one thing most wrong is that there is a single voice: king, alewife, pilot, bishop and the teller all speak in the same dry, clipped, knowing register and close on the same wry turn, so ninety-two people come down to about a dozen and the crew's quarrels sound like one man arguing with himself. The cause can be measured: the 744 port lines average 7.65 words a sentence, 630 of them are exactly two or three sentences long, and the designer's own sheets call fourteen people "dry" and seventeen "exact". As stories: the riddle trail is the best, it builds from a stick to a blind man's farm and its ending pays exactly what card five set up, though the player never once answers a riddle and picks the ending at s08 without being told so. The barrow is the best built (each card adds a claimant to the gold and each ending costs something you can see), but two of its four closing cards (s11, s12) offer one button, because the size of the haul was settled at s08. The storm vow's saint road is the best use of choice in the script, since the bishop reads back every corner you cut, while its Thor road is two cards long and its Gyrwe cards belong to another story. The old gods is the weakest: it is the storm vow told a second time (a storm, saint or heathen or nobody, a measured candle, forty days on bread and water, a feast at winter's start), its trip to Hleidr does not bear on its ending, and everything is decided on one card at the dune. And Ravn is two men, a tumbling talker on the quay and a grave oracle in the chains who has to keep telling us that he talks too much.

**Scores (1 to 10).** voices 4, clarity 6, variety 3, sense of the time 8, economy 6

### Habits

**The sting in the tail.** Nearly every line of talk, and most outcome paragraphs, close on a short dry turn: a reversal, a paradox or a small proverb. It is not kept to the 'saying' slot, which is meant for proverbs; rumours, greetings and farewells do it too. *How often:* Estimate: about two port lines in three (I checked the first two people line by line: Brunger 8 of 8, Hathugard 5 of 8). Count: 410 of the 744 port lines end on a sentence of eight words or fewer, 266 on six or fewer.

- `daventre.einbert rumour 2`: Buy rivets. Nobody was ever hanged for rivets.
- `doccinga.gerbrand rumour 3`: I strike his name until someone brings me a new one. A penny does not mourn.
- `dorestad.tollman rumour 3`: They pay the saint. I have never met a saint who gave change.
- `emuthon.imma rumour 3`: They weigh them anyway. A Dane would weigh his mother.
- `noviomagus.liudrad rumour 1`: The law says each lord feeds his own. The road has no lord.
- `chain_barrow.s09.out_church`: The Northman says a bishop is a king with a book, and takes his oar.

*Why it hurts:* A turn at the end of every line teaches the player the tune in ten minutes; after that they wait for the last sentence and skim the news before it. It makes a toll man as witty as a bishop. And it buries the good ones: "The road has no lord" deserves better than to be one of five hundred.

*Rule for the rewrite:* One sting to a person. Every other line says its news and stops a sentence early.

**One dry wit in every mouth.** Everyone meets the player with the same cool, amused sizing-up, most often as an either-or. Nobody is glad, frightened, hurried, flattering or slow. *How often:* Count: seven greetings are built on "either X or Y"; the sheets call 14 of 92 people "dry", 17 "exact", 15 "quick", 10 "patient", and 21 are said to count.

- `doccinga.radbrand greet_again`: Still afloat. Then you listened, or you were lucky. I charge the same for both.
- `doccinga.northman greet_again`: You come back to a place with nothing to buy. Either you like my ale or you want to know something.
- `noviomagus.tiel_merchant greet`: A stranger with a clean boat. Either you are honest or you are new. Sit down, I will find out which.
- `walichrum.eggihard greet_again`: You keep coming back to an island with no wall. Either trade is good or you are a brave man.
- `doccinga.gerbrand greet_again`: The merchant with the honest bag. Or the careful one. I have not yet decided which.
- `medemelacha.eilbern greet_again`: There he is. Still afloat, still honest? One of those wears off.

*Why it hurts:* A sour pilot, an old Danish warrior, a burned-out merchant, a Frankish count, a moneyer and a smuggler make the same joke in the same shape. Cover the names and it is one man. The sameness starts in the sheets, which are six sheets written ninety times.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Before writing a greeting, decide what this person wants from the boat today, and let them ask for it badly.

**Two or three short sentences, the last the shortest.** The line as a unit is always the same: two or three clipped statements, fragments welcome, shortest last. Nobody runs on, nobody trails off, nobody loses the thread. *How often:* Count: 630 of the 744 port lines are exactly two or three sentences; the average sentence is 7.65 words; 699 of 1,990 sentences are five words or fewer; only 7 sentences in the whole file pass twenty words.

- `dorestad.folcbert greet`: Shipman. You have my boat under you and my silver in her. Michaelmas. Hartwin has the stick.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s05.hand_hroald`: Not a skald. A skald makes. Hroald kept.
- `wijnaldum.sicco greet`: You came in on the wrong side of the stake. It does not matter today. It will matter at springs.
- `chain_barrow.s12.reeve_helm`: But the word is that the finder is to be paid and not asked how. So.
- `emuthon.liafbraht farewell`: Go with God. Go quietly.

*Why it hurts:* Short is right; uniformly short is a drum. Sicco's sheet says he "talks all the time and loudly" and he speaks in the same three short sentences as the man who "says a thing once". This is partly the maker's rules at work: plain words, little text and no shortened words together take away the cheap ways of telling a count from a ferryman, and the writing has answered with one rhythm for all.

*Rule for the rewrite:* In every port let one person talk in a single long sentence that loses its way, and one in four words.

**The disclaimer: "I only say".** A speaker pushes the player while pretending not to: "I only say what I see", "I do not say X. I say Y.", "I did not make it." *How often:* Count: 30 in the chains and port lines (17 and 13), and 8 more among Ravn's lines in the trial file. Ravn, Aldger and Beornwine all have it, in the very scenes where they argue.

- `chain_old_gods.s02.hand_door`: I only say what I see.
- `chain_old_gods.s06.steersman_candle`: I am the steersman. I say what I see.
- `chain_storm_vow.s06.lookout_send`: I only say what was said.
- `chain_barrow.s08.steersman_spoiled`: I do not say take it. I say that is how it is.
- `chain_storm_vow.end_saint.bishop_sent`: That is not how it was said. I only note it.
- `medemelacha.radward rumour 1`: I do not say what happened to the third. I say I count.

*Why it hurts:* It is a good trick, which is why it cannot belong to everybody. Shared by three crewmen, a bishop, a cellarer and a toll man it stops being a trait and becomes the writer's way of dropping a hint.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Give "I only say" to Beornwine, the boy raised not to contradict his elders, and strike it from every other mouth.

**Greetings from one mould.** The first words on meeting or returning come from a stock of four: "You again", "Still afloat", "Good." and "Sit." *How often:* Count: 13 people greet a returning player with "You again", 9 with "Still afloat", 12 with a form of "You came back"; "Good." stands as a sentence by itself 23 times; a form of "Sit" comes in 36 lines; 7 first greetings open "You came in / up / over / by".

- `doccinga.northman greet`: A merchant. Good.
- `traiectum.schoolmaster greet`: A merchant. Good.
- `walichrum.harald greet`: A merchant. Good.
- `emuthon.tetta greet_again`: You again. Your bench is as you left it, and so is what you owe me for the ale.
- `traiectum.cellarer greet_again`: You again. I kept your place by the fire. The straw is the same straw.
- `walichrum.unno greet_again`: Still afloat. Good. I have kept ten sacks dry for you, and a thing to ask.

*Why it hurts:* The greeting is the line the player hears most, at every port on every visit. If ten ports open on the same four words the coast is one town.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Keep "You again", "Still afloat", "Good." and "Sit." out of every first sentence, and start from what the person is doing with their hands when you walk up.

**The meaningful silence.** The teller ends an outcome by reporting who did not speak, thank, look or sit. *How often:* Count: 46 of the 180 outcome paragraphs.

- `chain_barrow.s02.out_take`: Nobody speaks until the boat is over the bar.
- `chain_barrow.s09.out_sale`: Nobody says anything. That is the men's answer.
- `chain_old_gods.s09.out_alms`: He does not say he thinks better of you.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s09.out_dig_success`: Nobody aboard says a word until the open sea, and then it is only the course.
- `chain_storm_vow.s04.out_refuse`: Nobody aboard speaks until she is out of sight.
- `chain_storm_vow.s05.out_one`: He does not speak again that day.

*Why it hurts:* It is the teller's only way of showing that the crew minds, so by the second chain "nobody speaks" is a red mark the player reads without feeling. The script knows a better way and uses it too seldom: "He scrubs the cloak before he gives it back." and the oar laid "with the blade toward the land".

*Rule for the rewrite:* One silence to a chain. Everywhere else, show what a man does with his hands or his gear.

**The spoken stage direction.** A person says their own mannerism aloud instead of having it. *How often:* Count: Aldger says "The worst first." five times and "Now what can be done." three times across two chains; Ravn says he forgets or loses things four times; at least four port people do the like.

- `chain_old_gods.s01.steersman_worst`: The worst first. She will not take much more of this.
- `chain_storm_vow.s10.steersman_ways`: Now what can be done. Swear it again before them with a nearer day, and go.
- `traiectum.schoolmaster farewell`: Keep it dry. Everything I say to anyone ends with that.
- `dorestad.moneyer greet`: On the pan. My grandfather would not talk to silver in a purse and I see no reason to begin.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s12.hand_book`: I held all of that once and lost most of it. I lose things.
- `chain_barrow.s09.hand_word`: I have not spoken of it since, and you know how I talk.

*Why it hurts:* The maker asked for quirks like the lender who counts and loses the count. That lender does not say "I count while I talk". A man who names his habit has read his own sheet. The script can do it properly, once: witla.gerlef age, "I am forty. I have seen that bar move nine times in fifty years."

*Rule for the rewrite:* A mannerism is something the speaker does not know he does. If he names it, cut the name and keep the doing.

**Ravn's talk is reported, not heard.** In the chains Ravn speaks in grave, measured fours, and the teller (or Ravn) keeps reminding us that he is a chatterer. *How often:* Count: 8 reminders in the four chains.

- `chain_barrow.s03.out_back`: The Northman says nothing for once, which from him is thanks.
- `chain_old_gods.s03.out_hand`: The hand says nothing for once, which is how you know he agrees.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s09.hand_warning`: I will say it once, and you know I am not a man who says a thing once.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s08.out_mound`: He does not speak on the walk back, and he is a man who needs a reason for that.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s11.out_self`: Then he says: "You meant it well," which from him is a hard word.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s12.out_kin`: and then, being Ravn, "The roof leaks over that bed."

*Why it hurts:* The same joke falls on the same card of two chains (s03, the choice that sides with him). The reminders are needed only because the Ravn of the chains does not talk much; he is a different man from dorestad.ravn, and the teller is covering the join.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Let him run on for a line when nothing is at stake, so that when he says four words the player notices without being told.

**The farewell errand.** The goodbye is an instruction for the road: "If you pass, see or meet X, do Y." *How often:* Count: 36 of 93 farewells carry an "if"; 30 of those are this shape, and seven are the same warning about sails.

- `daventre.liudbod farewell`: If you pass Traiectum, tell the bishop the bell rope is ready. It lacks only the bell.
- `dorestad.thiadlef farewell`: If you see sails from the north, do not come and tell me. Run.
- `walichrum.hemming farewell`: If you see many sails together, do not count them. Turn.
- `traiectum.bernbod farewell`: And if you see sails on the Lek, row first and count them after.
- `noviomagus.haribrand farewell`: If you pass a Saxon on the Rhine with a scar through his left brow, say Haribrand keeps the Ketil wood.
- `witla.thiadlint farewell`: If you pass Ghent, tell the cellarer the cows are alive.

*Why it hurts:* Besides the sameness, in a game "if you pass X, tell Y" reads as a task. The player cannot tell the few that may be real (Haribrand's brother, Hathugard's boy, Unno's sister) from the thirty that are colour.

*Rule for the rewrite:* A farewell says goodbye. Keep "if you pass" for the people who truly have an errand in the game.

**One fact, many mouths.** A piece of research the writer liked is handed to several people in several ports. *How often:* Count: the king's complaint about short Frisian cloaks is made by 8 people in 6 ports; a priest who wants a bell, 5 ports; "cattle die and kinsmen die", 4 speakers; a priest who has prayed for your boat, 4; an Arab coin with "writing like worms", 3; the weaver's hand worth a quarter more at law, 2; Pope Gregory's letter about keeping the shrines, 2 bishops in 2 chains.

- `emuthon.tetta saying`: A short cloak is a long cloak sold to a Frank.
- `doccinga.meinhild saying`: A short cloak well woven is warmer than a long one badly sold.
- `dorestad.tollman saying`: A short cloak for the long price. The king complained of ours. He still bought them.
- `witla.folcrad saying`: Charles was sold a short cloak at the long price, they say.
- `medemelacha.liudbrand saying`: Here they would say he sold a short cloak at the long price.
- `wijnaldum.liafburg rumour 3`: They say the king himself complained our cloaks are too short. Let him pay for the long ones, then.

*Why it hurts:* The player hears the note-card. One good anecdote is a find; the same anecdote in six ports is a hand-out, and it tells the player that the ports were written from one page.

*Rule for the rewrite:* One fact, one mouth. Whoever else knew it talks about their own day instead.

**The echo tag.** A plain statement followed by a two- to five-word echo that makes it general: always, everybody, "They do", "Kings do". *How often:* Count by hand: 21, three of them in one port (Dorestad).

- `dorestad.hartwin farewell`: Michaelmas. I shall be here. I am always here.
- `dorestad.tollman farewell`: Go on. I have written you down. I write everybody down.
- `dorestad.thiadburg greet_again`: I looked this morning. I look every morning.
- `noviomagus.louis rumour 1`: They had gone. They are always gone.
- `medemelacha.liudbrand rumour 1`: The priests will go back. The priests always go back.
- `medemelacha.ansfried rumour 1`: When he is grown he may want it back. Kings do.

*Why it hurts:* It is a cadence, not a thought. Cut the echo and nothing is lost; leave it and an emperor, a steward and a toll man share a breath.

*Rule for the rewrite:* If the last sentence only repeats the one before it with "always" or "everybody" added, cut it.

**The teller explains the gesture.** After showing a thing, the narration adds a clause that says what it meant: a trailing "which…" or a "like a man who…". *How often:* Count: 18 trailing ", which…" clauses in the chains, about a dozen of them glosses, and about a dozen "like a man who / as a man…" similes.

- `chain_storm_vow.s04.out_piece`: goes without a word of thanks, which is the right way to go.
- `chain_barrow.s09.out_earth`: holds it all the way out over the bar, which he has never done.
- `chain_storm_vow.s02.out_stand_saint`: You say all three parts again in daylight, which is harder.
- `chain_barrow.s03.out_church`: is asleep at once, like a man who has put down a load.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s08.out_heir`: He looks like a man who has been handed something hot.
- `chain_riddle_trail.s03.master_fog`: which is a joke, and you will see why when you read the stick.

*Why it hurts:* The maker's rule is not to explain everything. These clauses hand the player the meaning of a picture they have just been shown, and the simile is always the same simile.

*Rule for the rewrite:* End on the thing seen. If the next clause begins "which" or "like a man who", try the sentence without it first.

**Threes with a turn on the third.** A list of three in which the third item is the joke. *How often:* Count by hand: about 14.

- `dorestad.aldswith crew`: One is good, one is thirsty and one is my cousin.
- `doccinga.liafhild saying`: Butter keeps a week, cloth a lifetime, a grudge three generations.
- `walichrum.unno saying`: promised, certain, and not this year.
- `witla.radward saying`: The first boat sells dear, the second sells, the third carries it home again.
- `dorestad.hrotberct greet`: I do not give credit, I do not give lodging, and I do not give advice. Wax, I sell.

*Why it hurts:* It is the most recognisable machine rhythm there is, and five different people have it.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Lists are of two or of four. A three must be three plain things.

**Favourite words: nobody, twice.** "Nobody" is the sign for significance and "twice" the sign for care, mistrust and emphasis alike. *How often:* Count: "nobody" 91 times (59 in the chains, 32 in the ports); "twice" 48 times (22 and 26).

- `chain_barrow.s05.out_sell`: He weighs it twice, against pennies and then against barleycorns
- `chain_barrow.s12.out_gear`: It takes the reeve and both thegns to count it, and they count it twice.
- `walichrum.wiltrud rumour 3`: The king's man counted the sheep twice this spring.
- `wijnaldum.folcward rumour 1`: He counted the sheep twice and got two numbers.
- `traiectum.hunger greet_again`: and yours is one I count twice.
- `dorestad.aldswith rumour 2`: He said 'wine' twice. Men do not say it twice.

*Why it hurts:* A reader stops seeing a word used ninety times and starts hearing whoever keeps reaching for it.

*Rule for the rewrite:* Each of the two words once in a chain and once in a port.

### Ravn, somewhat rural

No, not as it is trialled. The trial makes him an English stage countryman and not a Northman from a farm: "nowt", "summat", "yon", "mebbe" and the dropped h put him in a northern English county, in a game whose rule is plain words and whose earlier period vocabulary was called obnoxious. It costs reading: 441 apostrophes, and a line like "'E's a thing in 'is tarn that 'e didn't 'ave afore." (chain_storm_vow.s14.hand_thor) has to be decoded. And it makes a clown of the one man aboard who is learned in his own way: "I were the best 'e 'ad." (chain_riddle_trail.s05.hand_bench) turns a confession into a turn, and "'Tisn't nowt." wrecks "It is not nothing." (chain_riddle_trail.s10.hand_stone). Two things in the trial are worth keeping. The first is its rule that the old words stay clean: in "'Ere it is, then. What did I have yesterday that I want today? It harms men. It hinders words, and yet it rouses them." (first_question.ask) the step up from his own talk to the kept words is the best idea on the page, and it is what the riddle trail is about. The second is the shortening alone: since nobody else in the game shortens a word, "I'll not carry one. Not into that ground." (chain_riddle_trail.s09.hand_spade) already sets him apart with no dialect in it. So keep I'll, don't, that's, we're, "I'll not" and his "mind"; drop the dropped h, the clipped -in', "me" for "my", "were" for "was", "a man as", "them" for "those", and every one of nowt, owt, summat, 'tis, afore, yon, mebbe and aye. In the grave scenes go the opposite way from the trial and take even the shortenings out, so that "Do not help me. Whatever you see me do, whatever you hear me say, do not help me." (chain_riddle_trail.s11.hand_low) stands as it is now; the trial's "Don't help me" is lighter and worse. That also mends the fault I found in him: he shortens when he chatters and says every word whole when it matters, and the two Ravns become one man with two gears.
